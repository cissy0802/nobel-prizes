The immune system keeps a squad whose job is not to fight

Hidden among our T cells is a small population of guards that restrain colleagues who attack the wrong target. Finding them took a Japanese pathologist over a decade and a line of sick mice left over from the Manhattan Project

Mary E. BrunkowInstitute for Systems Biology · USA · 1/3Fred RamsdellSonoma Biotherapeutics · USA · 1/3Shimon SakaguchiOsaka University · Japan · 1/3
for their discoveries concerning peripheral immune tolerance
Announced 2025-10-06 · 2026-10-17 · BigCat

Immunology spent a century learning how the body fights. This prize went to the cells that tell it to stop.

You catch a cold. Day three, your throat is at its worst; a week later it's gone. The virus was cleared, fine. The odd part is that the army stood down too. Who called the ceasefire?

There's a stranger question underneath. Your T cells recognise enemies with a surface sensor, the T-cell receptor, and each one is assembled by randomly shuffling gene segments. In theory that yields more than a million billion (10¹⁵) shapes. Shuffle randomly enough and some of those sensors will fit your own thyroid, pancreas or joints.

Yet most people go through life without their immune system turning on them. Who keeps that restraint in place?

The price of random keys, and an idea everyone walked away from

In the early 1900s Paul Ehrlich already had a name for the immune system attacking its owner: horror autotoxicus. The question sat there for decades.

By the 1980s the field thought it had the answer. T cells mature in the thymus, a small organ behind the breastbone, and sit an exam there: any cell that reacts strongly to "self" is deleted. This is central tolerance, and for years it was treated as the only safeguard against self-attack.

But no exam is perfect, and something had to handle the escapees. In the 1970s some researchers proposed "suppressor T cells". The field was messy, had no reliable markers, and leaned on a genetic locus called I-J. In 1983 better sequencing showed that I-J did not exist. Enthusiasm evaporated and the field more or less collapsed, taking with it the very idea that some T cells might police others.

Remove the thymus on day three, and the mouse turns on itself

Shimon Sakaguchi graduated in medicine from Kyoto University in 1976. As a pathology graduate student he came across a puzzling result from the Aichi Cancer Center in Nagoya: in 1969 Yasuaki Nishizuka and Teruyo Sakakura removed the thymus from mice three days after birth. The expectation was fewer T cells and a weaker immune system. Instead the mice developed inflamed ovaries, stomachs and thyroids. Their immune systems were attacking them.

Sakaguchi left graduate school to join that lab. In 1982 they showed the disease could be prevented by transferring a fraction of T cells from normal mice. Somewhere among ordinary T cells, something was braking.

Which cells, though? The markers of the day were crude, and the search took him more than a decade. In 1995, in The Journal of Immunology, he named it: about 10% of the CD4 T cells in a normal mouse carry a surface protein called CD25. Strip those out, inject the rest into mice that lack a thymus, and every recipient developed autoimmune disease — thyroid, stomach, pancreatic islets, joints. Add the CD25 cells back soon after, and the disease was held off, more effectively the more you added.

These became known as regulatory T cells, or Tregs. Many were unconvinced. CD25 also shows up on ordinary T cells once they're activated, so it was not a clean ID badge — and a field that had just been burned by suppressor cells wanted harder proof.

Whatever other immunologists might think, the finding that normal T cells can suppress a disease development, that is always I can come back to.— Shimon Sakaguchi · phone interview with nobelprize.org on announcement day

A line of sick mice from the Manhattan Project

The harder proof came from across the Pacific, by a strange route. In the 1940s, at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee, researchers studying radiation in mice for the Manhattan Project noticed a spontaneous mutant: some males were born with scaly, flaky skin and hugely swollen spleens and lymph nodes, and died within weeks. They called it scurfy. Females were fine, because the mutation sat on the X chromosome and they had a healthy spare. The line was kept going for roughly half a century without anyone knowing what it was good for.

In the 1990s, at Celltech Chiroscience, a small biotech in Bothell near Seattle, Mary Brunkow and Fred Ramsdell set out to find the mutation. The mouse X chromosome runs to about 170 million base pairs. They narrowed the search to 500,000, found roughly 20 candidate genes there, and compared them one by one between sick and healthy mice. Every one checked out until the last, the twentieth: two extra bases had been inserted, throwing the reading frame off so the protein stopped halfway. They named the new gene Foxp3, and showed that adding a working copy back rescued the scurfy males.

Along the way they suspected that IPEX, a very rare inherited disease in boys that is fatal without a stem-cell transplant, was the human scurfy. Samples gathered from paediatricians worldwide bore it out: in 2001 two papers in Nature Genetics showed that the same gene, broken, causes the same disease in mice and in boys.

One gene, one cell type, two doors

Sakaguchi's team placed the last piece in 2003: Foxp3 is switched on only in the CD25 cells, and forcing it into ordinary T cells turns them into regulatory T cells. At almost the same moment, Ramsdell's group and Alexander Rudensky in Seattle reached the same conclusion from different angles. Lose one cell type, controlled by one gene, and both mice and humans fall into horror autotoxicus.

That redrew the picture of tolerance. Central tolerance is a pre-deployment exam. Regulatory T cells are born in the thymus but patrol the whole body for life — the layer the committee calls peripheral immune tolerance. Their methods are unglamorous: direct contact, calming signal molecules, and hoarding interleukin-2 (IL-2), a growth factor other T cells need, starving overeager colleagues. They're also part of why the army stands down once an infection is cleared.

Above all, Foxp3 gave researchers a molecular handle: these cells could now be identified, counted and isolated. Two doors opened, pointing in opposite directions. Tumours recruit crowds of Tregs as bodyguards, so cancer researchers want to remove them; in autoimmune disease and transplantation, the aim is to make more. Ramsdell recalls writing into patents around 2000 that harnessing these cells could treat autoimmune disease — but back then nobody was going to do cell therapy for it. Two decades later he co-founded Sonoma Biotherapeutics, with Rudensky and others, to build exactly that.

◐ Off the podium

The prize stops at three people, and the committee's own scientific background names several who didn't make it: Fiona Powrie in Oxford, who showed in 1990 that a subset of CD4 cells protected mice; Bruce Hall in Sydney, who described similar CD4 and CD25 cells in transplant work between 1984 and 1990; Chatila's team, who found mutations in the same gene in IPEX patients in 2000 but lacked causal proof; and Rudensky, whose 2003 paper ran alongside Ramsdell's.

There's also a contrast in setting: two of the laureates worked not at a university but at a small company that closed in early 2004, after which Brunkow left the field. On announcement night she took the 1 a.m. calls from Sweden for spam, silenced her phone and went back to sleep. Ramsdell was camping off-grid in the Wyoming mountains and learned the news from the 200 text messages flooding his wife's phone.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Honestly: no Treg-based therapy has reached the market yet, as both the committee's scientific background and a Japanese popular-science piece from Kodansha say outright. The first families to benefit were those of boys with IPEX, whose doctors since 2001 have had a specific gene to test. The wider effect is invisible to patients: in autoimmunity, cancer immunology and transplantation, these cells are now a variable everyone measures.

What may change next

The committee counts more than 200 clinical trials involving regulatory T cells. Closest to the pharmacy are drugs that feed them. Rezpegaldesleukin, an IL-2-type drug designed to favour Tregs, was tested in 393 adults with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (eczema): after 16 weeks, eczema scores fell by 53–61% on average versus 31% on placebo. It appeared in The Lancet in August 2026; the trial was company-funded, and injection-site reactions were common. Cell therapy is earlier and bumpier. In a 2024 trial in 110 children with type 1 diabetes, a single infusion of expanded Tregs was safe but did not slow the loss of insulin-producing function. In March 2026 the UK company Quell reported that, of 9 liver-transplant patients given engineered CAR-Tregs, all 5 who attempted weaning came off the main anti-rejection drug tacrolimus for at least six months without rejection (still on a low dose of another drug) — interim, company-reported numbers. On the cancer side, antibodies that clear Tregs out of tumours are in clinical trials.

What it does not mean

It does not mean "a stronger immune system is better", and it is no endorsement of immune-boosting supplements: the point of this prize is that immune health is a balance of accelerator and brake. Nor are Tregs simply "good cells" to multiply — next to a tumour they are accomplices. And this is not the discovery of immune tolerance itself; that was Burnet and Medawar's 1960 prize for acquired immunological tolerance. This prize is for the layer that enforces tolerance across the body.

A way of thinking to borrow

Suppressor-cell research was dragged down by its worst evidence — a locus that didn't exist — and the sound core observation was thrown out with it. Falsifying the worst version of an idea is not falsifying the idea. When judging an abandoned line of work, ask: what was actually refuted, the phenomenon or one explanation of it?

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • AI · generate, then filter: T-cell receptors are sampled from a vast combinatorial space, so harmful outputs are guaranteed, and there are two gates — a training-time filter (the thymus exam) and a runtime guard (Tregs). Large-model safety has the same shape: however clean the pretraining data, deployment still needs a layer that catches bad outputs in the field. The difference: the immune system's guards are drawn from the same generator.
  • Control theory · negative feedback and damping: by soaking up IL-2, Tregs starve the "more fighting breeds more fighters" loop of fuel — a damping term. A system with only an accelerator either stalls or runs away. This site's 2026 Chemistry page covers the other half: how chiral autocatalysis uses positive feedback to blow a tiny bias up into totality.
  • Neuroscience · excitation–inhibition balance: the cortex also relies on a minority of inhibitory neurons to hold back excitatory ones; when inhibition fails, local firing can spread into a seizure. Same structure: a small population of brake cells keeps a high-gain network stable.
  • Philosophy of science · Lakatos's research programmes: a programme's hard core can be right while its protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses is badly wrong. The suppressor-cell core — some T cells actively restrain autoimmunity — survived; what fell was the I-J shell. Sakaguchi held the core and swapped the belt.
  • Buddhism · non-self: immunological "self" is not a fixed list but a restraint actively maintained every day — "self" is whatever the system chooses not to attack. That matches the view of self as a sustained process rather than a substance; the difference is that when the immune system's boundary slips, the consequence is very concrete disease.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. T-cell receptors are assembled randomly from gene segments, over 10¹⁵ possible shapes, so some inevitably recognise the body's own tissue.
  2. Central tolerance — deleting self-reactive T cells in the thymus — was long seen as the only safeguard, but it cannot catch every escapee.
  3. The 1970s suppressor T cell hypothesis collapsed under messy methods and a nonexistent I-J locus, and the field was largely abandoned.
  4. Mice whose thymus is removed on day three develop multi-organ autoimmunity, the anomaly that set Shimon Sakaguchi looking for an immune brake.
  5. In 1995 Sakaguchi showed that removing the roughly 10% of CD4 T cells carrying CD25 triggers body-wide autoimmunity; adding them back prevents it.
  6. Brunkow and Ramsdell found the Foxp3 mutation in the last of about 20 candidate genes in scurfy mice, a line born of Manhattan Project radiation research.
  7. In 2001 FOXP3 mutations were shown to cause both the scurfy disease in mice and fatal IPEX in human boys.
  8. In 2003 Foxp3 was shown to define regulatory T cells: born in the thymus, enforcing peripheral immune tolerance throughout life.
  9. No Treg therapy is approved yet; an IL-2-type drug worked in an eczema trial, while a single Treg infusion failed in type 1 diabetes.
  10. Immune health is a balance, not a matter of strength, much as a minority of inhibitory neurons keeps the brain's high-gain network stable.

// Think further

Had the suppressor-cell field not collapsed, would regulatory T cells have been found sooner?
Not necessarily. What was missing was not only confidence but tools: without monoclonal antibodies you couldn't separate cell subsets, and without sequencing you couldn't find Foxp3. The collapse may have delayed the right idea by a few years, but tooling set the earliest date it could be proven. Flip it around, too: having been burned once, the field demanded unusually strong evidence in 1995, and the final chain of proof was all the stronger for it.
The scurfy line was kept for about fifty years before meeting the people who understood it. Can today's research system afford to keep things "of no known use"?
Ramsdell went out of his way to thank the people at Oak Ridge who kept those mice going. Think of datasets, biobanks and open-source code with the same shape: the upkeep falls on one group, the payoff lands on another decades later. Who should pay for that kind of option?
The same cells are wanted in larger numbers in autoimmunity and smaller numbers in tumours. Can you adjust them in one place only?
That is the logic of CAR-Tregs and of antibodies aimed at markers found on tumour Tregs: give the guards an address label, or remove only the ones at the tumour's door. The hard part is that the body is connected — does a local change spill over? It's the same class of problem as asking whether rate-limiting one node in a distributed system just pushes the load onto others.
