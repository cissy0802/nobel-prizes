You can weigh what you cannot see, by watching the stars that race around it

Half the prize went to pen and paper: proof that Einstein's theory cannot avoid black holes. Half went to telescopes: a dark object of four million suns at the heart of our galaxy

Fall into a supermassive black hole and, at the moment you cross over, you feel nothing at all. The real point of no return looks perfectly ordinary up close.

Throw a ball up and it comes back down. Throw it harder and it climbs higher; throw it hard enough and it leaves the Earth for good. So what if a star pulled so hard that not even light was fast enough to get away?

In 1783 the English clergyman and astronomer John Michell worked exactly that out. Then he went one step further: we could never see such a star, but if something luminous happened to orbit it, we might infer the hidden body from the way its companions moved.

More than two centuries later, someone actually did it. One number from the committee's popular write-up to hold in your head: squeeze the whole Earth down to about nine millimetres across, the size of a marble, and it becomes a black hole.

The equations had it all along. Almost nobody believed it was real

Einstein finished general relativity in November 1915. On 13 January 1916, under two months later and less than four months before his own death, the German astronomer Karl Schwarzschild published the first exact solution: how spacetime curves around a round, non-rotating mass. Inside it sat a strange radius. Cross it and you can never come back out. Today we call it the event horizon.

But the solution was too perfect. In 1939 Robert Oppenheimer and his student Hartland Snyder followed a ball of dust as it collapsed and found that the star "tends to close itself off from any communication with a distant observer; only its gravitational field persists." They had assumed two ideal things, though: dust with no pressure, and perfect symmetry. Real stars are lumpy and they spin. Could the infalling matter simply miss the centre and bounce back out? That same year Einstein published his own doubts about horizons. In 1963 the Soviet physicists Evgeny Lifshitz and Isaak Khalatnikov went further and argued that a realistic, general collapse in relativity would produce no singularity at all.

Also in 1963, astronomers found the first quasar: a pinprick of light so far away that it had to shine about a thousand times brighter than our entire galaxy. The most natural way to pour that much energy out of so small a region was matter falling into a very heavy black hole. The theory gave no guarantee that such things could form, yet the sky seemed to be full of them.

The idea that arrived while crossing the street

In the autumn of 1964 Roger Penrose was teaching mathematics at Birkbeck College in London. One day he was walking with his friend Ivor Robinson. At a crossroads the two stopped talking to watch for traffic, then picked the conversation up again on the far side. After Robinson left, Penrose felt an odd elation he couldn't place. He went back over the whole day, down to what he had eaten for breakfast, until he landed on that crossing. Somewhere between the kerbs he had found a test for when a collapse has passed the point of no return, with no assumption of symmetry at all. He called it a trapped surface.

Here is the picture. In ordinary space, flash a shell of light outward from a sphere and the light spreads out, like a balloon being inflated. A trapped surface is a closed surface where even the light heading outward is forced to converge. Penrose proved that once such a surface exists, and as long as matter carries positive energy (which all real matter does), the collapse cannot be stopped: it must run into a singularity, an edge where space and time themselves come to an end. The three-page paper appeared in Physical Review Letters in January 1965. The committee's scientific background calls it the first genuinely post-Einsteinian result in general relativity.

One more counterintuitive detail. When a trapped surface forms, the matter need not be especially dense, and the popular write-up notes that you would feel nothing as you crossed the horizon of a supermassive black hole. The point of no return isn't a wall. It is a fact about the global geometry. A few years later Penrose and Stephen Hawking turned the same reasoning on the universe as a whole: under reasonable assumptions, the Big Bang must also have a singularity in its past.

Weighing the galactic centre through a wall of dust

If theory says black holes are unavoidable, is there one on our doorstep? The centre of the Milky Way hosts a strong radio source called Sagittarius A*, but between us and it lies so much dust that fewer than one visible photon in a billion gets through. Switch to the near-infrared and the loss drops to about a factor of ten.

Then there is the air. Stars twinkle because pockets of warmer and cooler air overhead act like wobbling lenses that smear the image. From the early 1990s, two teams went after the problem separately: Reinhard Genzel's group at the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics, using the European Southern Observatory's telescopes in Chile, and Andrea Ghez's group at UCLA, using the Keck telescopes in Hawaii. At first both used a brute-force trick: take a rapid series of exposures just over a tenth of a second long, freezing the stars before the air could move, then shift them into line and stack them. Later came adaptive optics: a laser lights up an artificial guide star high in the atmosphere, the system measures how the air distorts it, and a thin mirror bends in real time to cancel the distortion.

The logic of the test was clean. If the centre were a big swarm of ordinary stars, orbits further in would not get dramatically faster. If it were a single point, the closer a star, the faster it must move, the way Mercury outruns Neptune. In 1998 Ghez's team tracked 90 stars, the fastest moving at around 1,400 km per second, and the fall-off of speed with distance fit a single central point of about 2.6 million solar masses. Genzel's team's earlier data showed the same pattern.

The clincher was a star called S2 (S0-2 to Ghez's team). It completes an orbit in under 16 years, so a human career can follow several laps. In 2002 it swept through its closest approach, just 17 light-hours from the centre. Combined, the two teams' data showed about four million solar masses packed into a region no bigger than our solar system. Within known physics, only one kind of object can do that.

There’s nothing like competition to keep you going, to propel you forwards. And to get it right – these measurements are hard.— Andrea Ghez · telephone interview on the day of the announcement · 2020

Why one pen and two telescopes share a single prize

The committee's scientific background opens with Michell's 1783 sentence: "we could have no information from light", yet from the motions of bodies revolving around such stars "we might still perhaps … infer the existence of the central ones with some degree of probability." Genzel and Ghez, it says, made "precisely this inference." Penrose's theorem turned the black hole from a mathematical curiosity into a consequence the theory could not escape, which gave astronomers a reason to go looking. One half says "they must form"; the other says "here is one."

Since then the measurements have crept ever closer. In May 2018 S2 swung through closest approach again at more than 25 million km per hour, almost three percent of the speed of light, and GRAVITY, an instrument that combines ESO's four 8-metre telescopes with Genzel among its leading investigators, detected the gravitational redshift general relativity predicts. In 2020 the same team measured S2's orbit turning by about 12 arcminutes per lap, again as Einstein predicted. On 12 May 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of Sagittarius A*, built from data taken in April 2017: a ring of glowing gas around a dark centre, with a size that matched theory.

Still, the committee chose its words with care: a "supermassive compact object", not "a black hole." Its scientific background ends by noting that whether the structure near the horizon fully matches general relativity remains an open question.

◐ Off the podium

Nobel Prizes are not awarded posthumously. Stephen Hawking, Penrose's partner on the singularity theorems, died in 2018. The astronomer Martin Rees put it plainly: "Sadly, this award was too much delayed to allow Hawking to share the credit with Penrose" (AFP report, 6 October 2020, carried by phys.org).

A footnote in the scientific background adds an irony. One of the men who claimed in 1921 that general relativity was incomplete was the Swedish optician Allvar Gullstrand, the 1911 laureate in medicine and a long-time member of the Nobel Committee for Physics, who argued against giving Einstein the prize.

On the day of the announcement, the 89-year-old Penrose spent a long stretch of his phone call on his cyclic cosmology, a scheme, he admitted, "which I’m having trouble persuading the cosmologists about."

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Honestly, almost nothing you can buy. The nearest relative is GPS: the committee's popular write-up points out that general relativity has a practical use in that most common of navigation tools. But that is a small correction in weak gravity, and it has nothing to do with black holes or singularities. The other thing you have probably seen is the 2022 image of Sagittarius A* in the news. And adaptive optics, pushed hard by the effort to see the galactic centre, now sits on big telescopes like the VLT and Keck.

What may change next

In January 2024 the European Space Agency approved LISA (ESA): three spacecraft forming a triangle 2.5 million km on a side, due to launch in 2035, listening among other things for the ripples in spacetime when giant black holes at the centres of galaxies collide. Genzel said on the day of his prize that a space mission of this kind would probably be what finally clinches the case. These missions will change what we know about gravity and the universe, not next year's phone. What lies inside a black hole needs a theory uniting relativity and quantum mechanics, and nobody can put a date on that.

What it does not mean

It doesn't mean we are in danger. Genzel called Sagittarius A* a dwarf by the standards of active black holes, one that "doesn't do much"; we circle it slowly from more than twenty thousand light years away, one lap every 200-odd million years. It doesn't mean infinite density is real. The theorem says general relativity stops working there, not that nature contains an actual infinity. Nor is every detail of black holes now confirmed. The prize names a "compact object", and the physics near the horizon is still being tested.

A way of thinking to borrow

Penrose's move: don't lean on idealised assumptions; find a criterion that needs as little as possible. Instead of solving the equations for a messy star, he asked only whether a trapped surface exists, so the answer survives all of reality's dents and bumps. Genzel and Ghez's move: when you can't see something, watch what it does to its neighbours, and work out in advance which observable separates the rival explanations.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · Lamport's partial order: Lamport has written that "Time, Clocks and the Ordering of Events" grew from his feel for special relativity: there is no total order of events everyone agrees on, only a partial order of what can causally affect what (his notes on his papers). Penrose's proof also works purely with that causal structure, reasoning about where light can reach rather than solving equations. An event horizon is a boundary in the causal graph: messages sent from inside are never delivered outside.
  • Consensus · the commit point: in Paxos, a value is chosen the instant a majority accepts it, yet no node notices anything at that instant. A trapped surface is the same shape: it can form at modest density, someone crossing the horizon feels nothing, and still the outcome is fixed. In both, the point of no return is a global property, not a local event. The difference is that consensus is about knowledge; the black hole is about physics.
  • Statistical inference · latent variables and model comparison: the central mass is a latent variable, reachable only through stellar orbits. What made it work was choosing an observable that splits the hypotheses: how speed falls off with distance separates "a swarm" from "a point". GRAVITY's fit carries a parameter that is 0 for Newton and 1 for Einstein; it came out near 1.1, a textbook nested-model test. Two independent teams are the plainest form of cross-validation, or, as Ghez put it, "somebody else is going to figure out your mistakes."
  • Multi-messenger astronomy · on this site: light, gravitational waves and neutrinos are three channels for listening near black holes. In the 2026 physics prize on IceCube, the neutrino hints from NGC 1068 also point to the neighbourhood of a galaxy's central black hole. Same unseen object, different messenger.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. John Michell imagined in 1783 a dark star that light could not escape, and suggested inferring it from bodies that orbit it.
  2. Schwarzschild's 1916 solution already contained an event horizon, but Oppenheimer's 1939 collapse relied on perfect symmetry, so many doubted black holes really form.
  3. Crossing a London street in autumn 1964, Penrose hit on the trapped surface: a closed surface from which even outgoing light converges.
  4. The singularity theorem says a trapped surface plus positive energy makes collapse unstoppable, with no symmetry required: hence a "robust prediction".
  5. The point of no return is locally unremarkable: matter need not be very dense, and crossing a supermassive horizon is not felt.
  6. Two independent teams led by Genzel and Ghez used near-infrared light to see through dust, and short exposures plus adaptive optics to beat the air.
  7. The star S2 orbits the galactic centre in under 16 years, showing about four million solar masses packed into less than a solar system.
  8. The committee wrote "supermassive compact object", not "black hole"; whether the horizon region matches general relativity is still being tested.
  9. The prize has almost no direct products; its legacy is GRAVITY's relativity tests, the 2022 Sagittarius A* image and LISA, planned for 2035.
  10. A trapped surface and a consensus commit point share a structure: the outcome becomes irreversible while no local observer feels the moment.

// Think further

Penrose proved that general relativity produces singularities. Did that prove black holes exist, or that the theory breaks down there?
Both readings hold, depending on where you stand. For astronomers it showed black holes are not an artefact of symmetry assumptions, so they were worth hunting. For theorists it showed the theory predicting its own failure point. A good theory that marks where it stops being trustworthy is showing a kind of strength.
The committee said "compact object" rather than "black hole". Necessary caution, or too conservative?
Follow the chain of evidence. S2 only gets to around 1,400 Schwarzschild radii from the centre; anything closer rests on flares and the 2022 image. "Something extremely heavy and small is here" is nailed down; "it has a horizon" is an inference. Keeping what was measured apart from what was deduced is a habit worth copying.
Two teams competed for nearly thirty years instead of merging. When is independent replication worth more than collaboration?
When the measurement is hard and systematic errors are hard to catch from the inside. Two sets of telescopes, software and people arriving at the same orbit carry more weight than one bigger team. The price is duplicated effort. Replicas in a distributed system run the same trade: redundancy bought for trust.
