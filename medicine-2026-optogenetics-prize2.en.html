A light switch for the brain, found in pond scum

A protein that a green alga uses to chase light was moved into nerve cells, and now a beam of light can switch one kind of brain cell on or off

for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics
2026-10-12 · BigCat

For a century, neuroscience's problem was not that it couldn't see the brain. It was that it couldn't press the right button.

You move into an old house. There is a row of light switches on the wall and none of them is labelled. To find out which one runs which lamp, you don't sit and watch the bulbs all evening. You flip them one at a time.

The brain is that wall, except it has around 90 billion switches, tangled together, and cells that run completely different jobs often sit side by side. Most twentieth-century neuroscience could only watch the bulbs: this region lights up when you're afraid, but is it the cause of the fear, or just along for the ride? There was no way to flip the switches one by one.

This year's medicine prize is for a way to fit one class of brain cell with its own private switch, a switch borrowed from a single-celled alga that swims around in ponds.

Able to watch, unable to touch

People did try to press buttons. Push current through an electrode and every nearby cell gets zapped, whatever its type. Use a drug and it takes minutes to act, while neural signals live on the scale of milliseconds. Cut out a piece of brain and see what the animal loses, and you are demolishing rooms, not flipping switches. The committee's scientific background puts it precisely: these methods were good at establishing correlations, but could not manipulate specific cell types inside intact circuits on a millisecond timescale.

In 1979 and again in 1999, Francis Crick, co-discoverer of the DNA double helix, wrote down a wish: a method by which "all neurons of just one type could be activated or inactivated, leaving the others more or less unaltered". The ideal signal, he guessed, would be light, an idea he admitted was "rather far-fetched".

Around then, people tried firing lasers straight at neurons; in 2002 Gero Miesenböck put three proteins from the fruit-fly eye into neurons together; others bolted a synthetic light-sensitive latch onto an ion channel. Each proved the road could be travelled, but they needed several genes at once, or chemicals pumped into the brain, and the signal passed through too many hands to be fast. Slowly the spec became clear: one protein that catches light and opens a gate by itself, deliverable as a gene.

An alga that chases light, and a hypothesis nobody believed

Chlamydomonas is a single-celled green alga. Stir it into a dish and the water turns faintly green; light one side and the green slowly drifts toward the lamp. It senses light with an "eyespot", a tiny orange dot on the cell surface.

In the early 1990s, Peter Hegemann, at the Max Planck Institute for Biochemistry, used minuscule electrodes to record what happened when the eyespot was lit. The electrical response came half a millisecond after the light. In the human eye, light sets off a long chemical relay that ends with an ion channel (a tiny gate in the cell membrane that lets charged particles through) opening, and the whole thing takes at least 10 milliseconds. How could a single cell be twenty times faster than your eye?

Hegemann could think of only one answer: in the alga, the light catcher and the gate are the same protein. Nobody had ever seen an ion channel that responded to light by itself, and the idea met scepticism. Trying to prove it by isolating the protein, he found that it fell apart as soon as it left its home, and the hunt dragged on for nearly a decade. The break came from Japan: around 2000, researchers there published large numbers of the alga's gene sequences, and Hegemann's group spotted two that looked like light-sensing proteins.

He called Georg Nagel at the Max Planck Institute of Biophysics in Frankfurt. Nagel's speciality was injecting unfamiliar genes into frog eggs, which then mass-produce the protein and display it on their surface, where he could measure it with electrodes. In 2002 the first protein opened under green light and let in mostly protons. Nagel later recalled that the start was hard, until "some wrong solutions which had a low pH" suddenly gave him a clear signal. In 2003 came the second, channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2): hit it with blue light and within 0.2 milliseconds it opened, letting positively charged ions such as sodium and calcium pour in. Put into human kidney cells, it made those cells fire on command too.

The 2003 paper ends by noting that ChR2 could be used to depolarize small or large cells "simply by illumination". Hegemann remembers that the first comment from Science was "An ion channel from pond scum", and that "the Chlamydomonas community was not amused".

A psychiatrist puts the switch into neurons

When Karl Deisseroth studied medicine at Stanford he was leaning toward neurosurgery, until a rotation in psychiatry confronted him with his patients' suffering. Why can't a depressed person feel joy? The drugs he could offer often failed and came with heavy side effects. He decided he first had to understand the living brain, and took the risk of chasing Crick's far-fetched switch.

He wrote to Nagel for the ChR2 gene and used a virus to carry it into rat neurons in a dish, half expecting the delicate cells to be harmed by the foreign protein. Nothing went wrong. Each flash of blue light made a neuron fire once. In the 2005 Nature Neuroscience paper, a train of flashes produced a matching train of spikes, one for one, with millisecond precision. There was luck too: the protein needs retinal, a form of vitamin A, as its light-catching part, and Nagel noticed that the trace amounts already present in neurons were enough. Nothing had to be added.

In 2006 the method got its name, optogenetics: "opto" for precision in time, "genetics" for fitting the switch only to one kind of cell. In 2007, Deisseroth's group threaded a thin optical fibre through a small hole in the skull into the motor cortex of a living mouse. The light came on and the whiskers moved. The same year, with Luis de Lecea, they lit a small group of cells in the hypothalamus and sleeping mice woke up. Also in 2007, a protein from an archaeon that pumps chloride into the cell under yellow light became the "off" switch: blue for on, yellow for off, toggled in the same circuit.

From reading the brain to writing to it

Deisseroth summed it up on the day of the announcement:

We're not using light to collect information, we're using light to cause things to happen.— Karl Deisseroth · interview with STAT · October 2026

Once you can flip a switch, you can ask "is this enough?" and "is this needed?". In 2012, Deisseroth and Susumu Tonegawa fitted switches to the hippocampal cells that happened to be active while a mouse was frightened. Later, in a safe cage, they shone light on those cells and the mouse froze, as if it were back in the bad place. In 2013 Tonegawa's lab went further and made mice fear a place where nothing bad had ever happened to them: a false memory.

One seemingly seamless thing after another came apart. In the midbrain, dopamine cells that look alike are split by where their input comes from: one route drives reward, another drives aversion. A mouse's care for its pups divides into separate cell groups for handling the pups, for seeking them out, and for dealing with other adults. Anxiety turns out not to have a single "anxiety centre" but to be assembled from pathways that can be toggled separately. Deisseroth even used light to force a mouse's heart to beat faster, and found that a racing heart by itself can deepen anxiety.

The committee places all this in a long lineage. Camillo Golgi's staining method let Santiago Ramón y Cajal see individual neurons (Nobel Prize 1906); the patch clamp gave direct access to single ion channels (Nobel Prize 1991). Optogenetics is the next tool on that line: change the tool, and you change the questions you can ask.

◐ Off the podium

The Nobel caps a prize at three people, and plenty of names stayed in the audience this time. The first author of the landmark 2005 paper was Ed Boyden, then a PhD student in Deisseroth's lab and now at MIT. STAT reported that many on social media felt he should not have been left out. The same piece mentions Zhuo-Hua Pan of Wayne State University in Detroit, who put ChR2 into retinal cells in 2004 but could not get the work published until 2006. Ernst Bamberg, a co-author on the key 2002, 2003 and 2005 papers, and the groups of John Spudich and Tetsuo Takahashi, who independently found the same genes, all appear in the committee's background.

In his prize-day call, Nagel admitted mild surprise that Deisseroth had described putting ChR2 into neurons as a risky project: it expressed so well in kidney cells, why wouldn't it work in neurons? But Deisseroth's work spreading and explaining the tool, he added, was "absolutely important".

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Honestly: nothing at the pharmacy yet. Nagel's first reaction was that it was too early for this prize: "so far I know of one patient who profited from optogenetics". That patient, reported in Nature Medicine in 2021, had been blinded by retinitis pigmentosa, an inherited disease in which the eye's light-sensing cells slowly die. Doctors injected one eye with a virus carrying a light-sensitive protein's gene and gave the patient engineered goggles that turn changes in the scene into light pulses on the retina. Using that eye alone, the patient could perceive, locate, count and touch objects on a table; before the injection, with or without goggles, they could see none.

Where it has reached you is indirectly: the "thirst circuits", "reward circuits" and "your heartbeat shapes your mood" you read about in popular science increasingly come from optogenetic experiments.

What may change next

The nearest step is in the eye, which is conveniently transparent. Nanoscope's optogenetic therapy Mogenry (formerly MCO-010) reported phase 2b results in 2023: 7 of 18 treated patients gained at least 0.3 logMAR in best-corrected acuity (about three lines on an eye chart), against 1 of 9 given a sham injection. In September 2026 the US FDA accepted its licence application; it has not been approved. The committee also mentions that stimulating the auditory nerve with light rather than current might make cochlear implants more precise. Putting switches into human brains to treat depression would need both gene therapy and a way to get light through the skull, and remains a laboratory idea.

What it does not mean

It does not mean "controlling human thoughts with light". The mouse false-memory work required prior genetic engineering, a hole in the skull and an implanted fibre, and what it produced was freezing, not an implanted story. Nor does it mean "one group of cells = one emotion": a 2015 methods paper co-written by Boyden warns that switching a whole population on or off at once can drive downstream circuits in ways that never happen naturally, and that the light itself heats tissue, so results need many controls.

A way of thinking to borrow

To find out whether A causes B, watching them move together is not enough. You need to move only A, move it fast, and be able to let go, then ask separately: if I switch on only A, does B appear? If I switch off only A, does B vanish?

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Causal inference · observing vs intervening: "what is Y when we see X" and "what is Y when we force X to a value" are different questions; the second is an intervention. fMRI and electrode recordings answer the first. Optogenetics was the first way to answer the second at the level of one cell type. The difference: a real intervention ripples through the circuit, rather than changing a single variable as in the formula.
  • Distributed systems · fault injection: dashboards show you which services alarm together; chaos engineering deliberately kills one specific instance to see if the system holds. Optogenetic silencing is fault injection for a cell type, and activation is synthetic traffic. Even cascading failures map over: downstream circuits being driven unnaturally is exactly the side effect Boyden and colleagues warn about.
  • AI · activation patching and feature steering: interpretability work first reads a feature inside a model with a probe, then amplifies or ablates it and watches the output change. Same step from read to write. A neural network lets you touch every unit exactly; the brain doesn't, which is why genes have to do the labelling.
  • Neuroscience · attractors and memory: as the 2024 physics prize page explains, give a Hopfield network part of a cue and the whole memory gets pulled back. The 2012 experiment is almost its biological version: light up only the original cells and the whole fear returns, as if the memory were a hollow in a landscape that you roll into once you touch its edge.
  • Buddhism · the five aggregates: Buddhist thought holds that the "self" is a bundle of five components (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness) that looks like one thing but can be taken apart. Optogenetics' dissection of anxiety, parenting and aggression has the same shape: a state that feels whole turns out to be several separately switchable pathways. The difference is the purpose: Buddhism takes it apart in order to stop clinging to it, science in order to repair it.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Twentieth-century neuroscience could mostly only watch which areas lit up, unable to move one cell type on a millisecond scale, so causes were hard to pin down.
  2. Francis Crick imagined in 1979 activating or silencing just one type of neuron, and guessed that light would be the ideal signal.
  3. The alga's eyespot produces current half a millisecond after light arrives, which led Peter Hegemann to propose that one protein both senses light and opens the gate.
  4. Georg Nagel injected algal genes into frog eggs and in 2003 showed channelrhodopsin-2 opening within 0.2 milliseconds of blue light.
  5. In 2005 Karl Deisseroth put channelrhodopsin-2 into rat neurons, and each flash of blue light made a neuron fire once.
  6. Optogenetics, named in 2006, uses light for timing precision and genes to fit switches only into one type of cell.
  7. In 2012, lighting up the hippocampal cells that had been active during a fright made mice freeze even in a safe cage.
  8. Optogenetics splits seemingly unified states such as anxiety or caring for young into several pathways that can be switched separately.
  9. So far the clinical payoff is mostly in the eye: in 2021 a blind patient saw objects on a table again with a light-sensitive protein and special goggles.
  10. Optogenetics resembles fault injection in distributed systems: switch off one class of cells precisely and see whether the whole system holds up.

// Think further

Light up a set of cells and the mouse is afraid. Is the fear memory "stored" in those cells?
What the experiment shows is sufficiency: activating them is enough to bring back the fear. But a key being enough doesn't mean the lock and the room are inside the key. The memory may live in those cells and their connections, with the cells serving as the entry point. Whether deleting the entry deletes the memory is a separate experiment.
Nagel knows of one patient who has benefited. Should a medicine prize reward lives already saved, or doors opened?
The 1906 and 1991 medicine prizes also went to methods that cured nobody directly at the time. But the prize has "medicine" in its name, and giving it to a tool that mainly serves basic research is itself a value judgment: understanding comes before treatment.
Hegemann says young researchers shouldn't improve optogenetics; they should work on something new. Is he right?
Optogenetics came from a "useless" question: how does an alga chase light? He notes that the first paper on that problem appeared in 1866. Yet most of optogenetics' progress since has come from generation after generation of improved proteins. Which is scarcer, curiosity or engineering?
