ECONOMIC SCIENCES · 2024

Where colonisers kept dying, countries are poorer today

Three economists treated colonial history as a natural experiment to show that a society's rules shape its wealth centuries later, then used game theory to explain why bad rules are so hard to change, and when they change anyway

Daron AcemogluMIT · 1/3Simon JohnsonMIT · 1/3James A. RobinsonUniversity of Chicago · 1/3
for studies of how institutions are formed and affect prosperity
2026-10-27 · BigCat

Five hundred years ago, Aztec Mexico was more urbanised than the land that became the United States and Canada. Today the order is reversed. The mountains and the weather stayed put. The rules changed.

You have probably seen a place like this: a street, a river or a fence, with people on both sides eating similar food and listening to similar music, yet living very different lives.

Nogales is one of them, a city cut in half by a fence. The northern half is in Arizona. Its residents are relatively well off, live long, mostly finish high school, and can vote out politicians they dislike. The southern half is in Sonora, Mexico, a fairly wealthy part of the country, and still considerably poorer: organised crime makes running a business risky, and corrupt politicians are hard to remove. The climate is identical. The northern side used to be Mexico, so long-time residents share many ancestors.

Zoom out and the richest fifth of the world's countries are about 30 times richer than the poorest fifth, and the gap is not closing. The 2024 laureates set out to prove something everyone nods along to and almost no one can demonstrate: the difference is mainly about rules.

Everyone said rules matter. Nobody could prove it

The idea was old. Adam Smith had it. Douglass North, the 1993 laureate, called institutions "the rules of the game in a society": whose property is safe, who may trade, who can replace the people in charge.

The trouble is causation. Rich countries do have better institutions, but perhaps people demand elections and courts once they are rich. That is "modernisation theory", associated with Seymour Lipset in 1959. Or a third factor drives both: Montesquieu argued in 1748 that temperate climates are more productive than the tropics, and Max Weber traced the industrial revolution to the Protestant Reformation. Rich and poor countries differ in a hundred ways, and correlation is not causation.

The clean test would be to hand random countries good rules and wait a few centuries. Impossible. So the laureates went looking for a natural experiment in history: something that shifted the rules without directly shifting everything else. They found European colonisation.

Soldiers, bishops and sailors

From around 1500, Europeans colonised much of the world, but not the same way everywhere. Where they settled in large numbers, in what became the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, they built rules that protected property and constrained power, for themselves. Elsewhere a thin layer of Europeans ran the place to move resources out as fast as possible; the Belgian Congo was the extreme case. The laureates call the first kind inclusive institutions and the second extractive institutions.

Whether Europeans settled depended heavily on whether they died. In their 2001 paper, Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson used the historian Philip Curtin's records of mortality among soldiers and sailors stationed in the colonies between the 17th and 19th centuries, filling gaps in Latin America with bishops' mortality computed from Vatican records. Where malaria and yellow fever were rife, the numbers were staggering: in extreme episodes, annual deaths per thousand exceeded a thousand, because the dead kept being replaced by new arrivals. People knew it at the time. The Pilgrim fathers chose North America over Guyana because Guyana was a death trap.

Every link held up. Higher mortality, fewer European settlers, weaker constraints on rulers in 1900, weaker protection against government expropriation today, lower income today. One standard deviation more settler mortality goes with roughly 47 percent lower income per head now. The pattern survives controls for latitude, climate, religion, legal origin and which power did the colonising, and it survives dropping Africa, or dropping the four "neo-Europes".

The 2002 paper came at it from another side. There is no GDP for 1500, so they used urbanisation as a proxy: only places with productive farming and decent transport can feed big cities. The result was a reversal of fortune. The Mughals, Aztecs and Incas were among the richest civilisations of 1500; the countries on their former territory are now far poorer than the United States, Canada, Australia or New Zealand. Five percentage points more urbanisation in 1500 goes with about a third less income per head today.

Two details make geography hard to rescue. There was no reversal in the five centuries before colonisation: places rich in 1000 were still rich in 1500. Nor was there any reversal among countries that were never colonised. And the timing fits: the formerly poor colonies pulled ahead in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, with the industrial revolution. As late as the mid-1700s, industrial output in what is now India exceeded that of the United States. Mountains do not change their minds in 1800, but new technology only takes root where the rules let ordinary people invest and keep the gains.

Why not reform? Because promises are cheap

If inclusive rules would make everyone richer in the long run, why don't ruling elites adopt them? From 2000 onward, Acemoglu and Robinson answered with game theory, and the heart of the answer is the commitment problem.

The elite can tell the masses: calm down, we will take less from now on. But while power stays in their hands they can take it back the moment things settle, and everyone knows it. The reverse offer fails too: "hand over power and we will compensate you" is not credible, because once the elite have stepped down nobody has a reason to pay. Both sides can see that a deal would leave everyone better off, and neither can make the other believe it. So extractive rules persist, with mass poverty below and a rich elite above.

The same trap explains why democracy sometimes arrives. The masses lack votes, but they are many. When they mobilise and revolution becomes a real threat, promises of reform are still not believed, and the only credible promise left is to hand over the decision itself: to extend the vote. Britain widened suffrage in several steps, each preceded by general strikes and mass protest; Sweden decided on universal suffrage in December 1918 after widespread rioting in the wake of the Russian revolution. Elites had other options: Germany in the 1880s built a basic welfare state without extending the franchise.

The framework explains a colder fact too. Elites sometimes block new technology not because it is unprofitable, but because it threatens their hold on power. This is the political replacement effect: while Britain and the United States industrialised quickly in the 19th century, Russia and Austria-Hungary lagged.

A new way of reading history

The committee treats the two strands as one contribution: the first shows with data that rules shape prosperity, the second explains where rules come from and why they stick. Together they launched a whole field, often called historical persistence studies.

A well-known example is Melissa Dell's 2010 study of the mita, a forced mining-labour system in what is now Peru and Bolivia between 1573 and 1812. Centuries later, household consumption inside the old mita boundary was about 25 percent lower than just outside it. An administrative line abolished long ago still shows up in living standards.

What is democracy worth? Acemoglu and co-authors used data on 175 countries from 1960 to 2010 and found that democratisation raises long-run GDP per head by 20 to 25 percent, but the full effect takes about 20 years to arrive.

Reducing the vast differences in income between countries is one of our time’s greatest challenges. The laureates have demonstrated the importance of societal institutions for achieving this.— Jakob Svensson, Chair of the Committee · 2024 press release

◐ Off the podium

The committee's own background names Stanley Engerman and Kenneth Sokoloff (1997), who had already traced how factor endowments and colonial rule shaped institutions across the Americas, as key precursors. The data have been challenged: Albouy (2012) called the 18th-century mortality figures sketchy, and Glaeser and co-authors (2004) noted that migrants brought not just institutions but themselves; the committee adds that know-how may have travelled too. Its verdict: the core result stands, but the exact size of the effect should be taken "with a grain of salt".

After the prize, the scholar Yuen Yuen Ang argued that the framework cannot account for China, which became the world's second-largest economy in a generation without Western-style democracy. In August 2026, The Economist called Acemoglu "oddly unconvincing"; he replied that what was really unconvincing was the critique.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

This work sells no product; it changed how international institutions talk about development. The World Bank's World Development Report 2017 put governance and institutions at the centre of its agenda, with "credible commitments" among its core ideas, and the UN's 2030 Sustainable Development Goals include developing "effective, accountable, and transparent institutions at all levels". The committee is careful to say the laureates' direct influence on such policy cannot be proven. Acemoglu and Robinson's bestseller Why Nations Fail took the whole argument to a mass audience.

What may change next

In Power and Progress (2023), Acemoglu and Johnson aim the same question at AI: the direction of technology is a choice, not destiny, and whoever holds that choice decides who benefits. Whether the gains from AI are captured by a few firms or broadly shared over the next decade or so is exactly where this framework will be tested.

What it does not mean

It does not mean colonialism was good. The reversal is about relative prosperity between regions, not about indigenous people thriving; in the sparsely populated colonies, a large share of the native population did not survive the colonial era. It does not mean geography or culture are irrelevant, only that they cannot explain the reversal. And it does not mean "add democracy, get rich": the effect takes around twenty years, and the commitment problem itself explains why institutions cannot be copy-pasted like software.

A way of thinking to borrow

Find the case where rival explanations predict opposite things. Geography says rich places stay rich; institutions say a wholesale change of rules can flip the ranking. Instead of arguing which story sounds better, the laureates went looking for the stretch of history that could tell them apart.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · who holds write access: a leader that can unilaterally rewrite the log promising not to do so is worth nothing; the only way to make the promise credible is to change who may write, for instance by moving decisions to a majority quorum. Extending the vote is that move: not a promise about future allocations, but a change in who decides them.
  • AI · the instrument in causal inference: settler mortality is an instrumental variable. It pushes on institutions and is assumed to affect today's income only through them. That is the same worry as leakage in model evaluation: any side channel around the main path (settlers' schooling, say) biases the estimate, and critics still argue about how wide that door is.
  • Economics · Ulysses and the mast: the committee traces commitment problems to the 2004 laureates Kydland and Prescott, for whom an independent central bank is a mast that ties the future self. The site's 2026 economics prize is about a central bank's promises; this one is about a ruler's. Same puzzle, higher stakes.
  • Economics · incumbents in the way: in the 2025 economics prize, Mokyr documents machine-breaking and machine bans, and Aghion and Howitt model incumbents lobbying against entrants. The political replacement effect adds a motive: technology may be blocked for the sake of power, not money.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Nogales is split by a fence into a US half and a Mexican half with the same climate and shared ancestors, yet very different incomes.
  2. The richest fifth of countries are about 30 times richer than the poorest fifth, and the gap is not closing.
  3. Rich countries might have better institutions because they got rich first, so proving that institutions cause prosperity needs a natural experiment.
  4. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson used mortality among soldiers, bishops and sailors to track where Europeans could settle.
  5. Where Europeans settled they built inclusive institutions; where they could not, extractive institutions took hold and persisted to this day.
  6. Reversal of fortune: colonies that were urban and dense in 1500 are poorer today, and the reversal coincides with the industrial revolution.
  7. Extractive institutions persist because of the commitment problem: rulers' promises are not credible, and neither is compensation for rulers who step down.
  8. When the threat of revolution is real, extending the vote is the elite's only credible promise, as British and Swedish suffrage history suggests.
  9. The work does not say colonialism was good, nor that democracy makes a country rich overnight; the effect takes about twenty years.
  10. Credible commitment comes from changing who holds the decision, the same move as transferring write access in a distributed system.

// Think further

If settlers brought schools and skills as well as rules, does "institutions decide prosperity" still hold?
This is the soft spot of the instrument. Try separating rules from people: when the same population ends up under different rules, do outcomes diverge? The two halves of Nogales suggest one answer: shared ancestors, different rules, different results. In practice the two are probably entangled, and the real question is which moved first and which locked in the other.
Is China's decades of rapid growth a counterexample, or a variant the framework misreads?
As critics summarise it, the laureates point to catch-up room, a critical juncture and luck, and predict a slowdown. Critics reply that autocracies come in many kinds, some with partial checks and local experimentation and no elections. The question worth pressing is whether "inclusive" can be measured with a finer ruler than "are there elections?"
Where in your own organisation are promises cheap?
Management says "this is the last round of layoffs". Why does nobody believe it? The commitment problem's answer: credibility does not come from a more sincere tone but from putting the decision where the promiser cannot change it alone, in a contract, with a third party, or behind a process that needs both signatures.
