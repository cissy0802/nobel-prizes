To photograph electrons, let the electrons fire the flash

A strong laser rips an electron out of an atom and flings it back; as it crashes home it emits a flash only a few hundred attoseconds long. For the first time, we had a shutter fast enough to watch electrons move in matter

The shortest flash of light was not made by building a faster laser. It is what an electron spits out when it slams back into its atom.

Take a phone photo of a running desk fan and the blades are a grey smear. Turn on the flash and they hang frozen in mid-air. The rule is simple: your shutter has to be faster than the motion, or everything blurs into an average.

Electrons inside atoms move on a scale of attoseconds. An attosecond is a billionth of a billionth of a second: there are about as many attoseconds in one second as there have been seconds since the universe began, 13.8 billion years ago.

Before 2001, humanity's fastest “flash” was still a few femtoseconds, that is, a few thousand attoseconds. That year, labs near Paris and in Vienna measured flashes of a few hundred attoseconds. Both light sources traced back to a 1987 experiment that had been looking for something else.

The femtosecond wall: the shortest a laser can do is still too long

From the laser's invention in 1960, physicists kept making light pulses shorter. By the 1980s they had reached femtoseconds (a millionth of a billionth of a second), and that was treated as a hard limit. It was enough to watch atoms shift inside molecules; the 1999 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Ahmed Zewail for using it to watch chemical reactions.

Electrons were another matter. The committee's scientific background sets out the mismatch: the natural unit of time inside an atom is about 24 attoseconds, while a light pulse has to contain at least one full oscillation of the wave. For the workhorse titanium-sapphire laser, at around 800 nanometres, one oscillation takes about 2.6 femtoseconds. It is like trying to draw a hairline with a thick brush: the tip is wider than the line.

Going shorter means using light of much shorter wavelength, such as extreme ultraviolet (XUV), and many wavelengths at once. That is a basic property of waves: the more frequencies you stack, and the wider their spread, the narrower the pulse they make. An ordinary laser is essentially one colour. The committee writes that better engineering was no longer enough to break this wall; it took a change of track in fundamental physics.

Saclay, 1987: looking for fluorescence, finding a row of overtones

In 1987 L’Huillier was at the French Atomic Energy Commission's laboratory in Saclay, south of Paris. Her team focused a powerful infrared laser, wavelength about one micrometre, into a jet of noble gas, hoping to see what fluorescence the excited atoms would give off.

They saw little fluorescence. What they saw instead were high-order harmonics: light at exact odd multiples of the laser's frequency. In argon they ran from the 13th to the 31st, and across a long stretch they were all about equally bright, a “plateau” that ended in a sudden drop. Theory at the time said each higher harmonic should be weaker than the last.

This result was very unexpected! We did not anticipate seeing so many high-order harmonics, with comparable intensities.— Anne L’Huillier · Nobel Lecture · 2023

She compares it to a musical instrument: a bow pulled hard across a string brings out a whole series of overtones, and an intense laser “bowing” an atom does the same. Several groups realised that such a broad set of overtones could in principle add up to an extremely short pulse. Nobody yet knew where the plateau came from.

The answer came in pieces. In 1991, L’Huillier, Kenneth Schafer and Kenneth Kulander of Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory solved the equations numerically and got the plateau's shape right. In 1993, Kulander's group and, independently, Paul Corkum in Canada proposed a vivid “three-step model”:

First, the laser's electric field is strong enough to bend the wall that holds the electron in, and the electron tunnels through it, a purely quantum move. Second, the free electron is pushed away by the field; half a cycle later the field reverses and drags it back, and along the way it gathers energy. Third, it smashes back into its ion and releases the surplus in one go as an extreme-ultraviolet photon.

The picture also explains why Saclay saw a long plateau. Infrared light has a long wavelength, so the field reverses slowly and the electron travels far and gains a lot. A Chicago group the same year used 248-nanometre ultraviolet light; the electron was yanked back too soon, and no plateau appeared. In 1994, L’Huillier, Corkum and others published a full quantum theory confirming the picture.

2001: a train, and a single carriage uncoupled

Having harmonics was not proof of attosecond pulses. Whether they add up to sharp spikes or a mess depends on whether their peaks line up, their “phase”; an ordinary detector only tells you how bright each colour is, not where its peaks fall. In his Nobel lecture Agostini recalls that it took almost ten years to go from the idea to a phase measurement, and that one reason for the delay was perhaps a 1996 calculation by L’Huillier herself, which seemed to show the phases jumping at random.

Agostini was a veteran of the field: in 1979 his team found that atoms in a strong laser swallow more photons than they need to ionise. His method was to send the harmonics, together with part of the original infrared beam, into argon and measure the electrons knocked out. The infrared light works like a ruler: nudge its delay relative to the harmonics step by step, the electron signal rises and falls, and the rhythm gives away the phases. The method was later named RABBITT. In June 2001 the team reported in Science that the harmonics were phase-locked and formed a train of pulses, each just 250 attoseconds long.

That November, Krausz's group in Vienna reported in Nature a single pulse. Working with Mauro Nisoli's group in Milan, they had compressed their laser to about 5 femtoseconds, only a few oscillations long; theory had predicted that such a pulse would make the highest-energy harmonics at the end of the plateau form one isolated burst. They picked out that part with a multilayer mirror, then used the infrared field to “push” the electrons it knocked loose, working back from the electrons' energy to time. The pulse lasted about 650 attoseconds.

An “instant” turns out to have a length

Among the first questions the new shutter could answer, one of the prettiest was about the photoelectric effect, which Einstein explained in 1905: light hits an atom and an electron flies out. It had long been treated as instantaneous, because nobody could measure otherwise. In 2010 Krausz's group found that when the same attosecond pulse hits a neon atom, electrons from the 2p orbital leave 21 attoseconds later than those from 2s. Several theory groups, though, calculated only about half that.

The mismatch stood for seven years. In 2017 L’Huillier's team in Lund re-measured it with a method that resolves time and energy together, and found that the 2010 signal had been contaminated by another process: a photon ejecting one electron while kicking a second one into a higher orbit, only 7.4 electronvolts away and impossible to separate with the earlier method. With the two pulled apart, experiment and theory agreed. That is what a new instrument is worth: not just a number for an old question, but sharper questions and better methods.

Since then the targets have spread from single atoms to liquids and solids. A 2020 experiment found that electrons leaving liquid water emerge 50 to 70 attoseconds later than those from water vapour, held back mainly by the neighbouring molecules. The pulses keep shrinking too: Agostini's lecture cites two 2017 records, 53 and 43 attoseconds.

◐ Off the podium

A Nobel can go to at most three people, and the name most often mentioned this time was Canada's Paul Corkum, one of the two independent authors of the three-step model. On announcement day The Globe and Mail wrote of disappointment among Canadian researchers: Corkum had been seen as one of the country's top contenders, and in 2022 he shared the Wolf Prize in Physics with Krausz and L’Huillier. The committee's background also singles out important work by Margaret Murnane, Henry Kapteyn and Ursula Keller.

L’Huillier is the fifth woman to win the physics prize. When the news came she was teaching. Asked by phone that day how her students took it, she said they were very happy, and: “I continued my lecture.”

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Honestly, almost nothing: attosecond pulses are still a laboratory tool. The most direct “products” are big facilities. Europe's attosecond source ELI-ALPS, in Szeged, Hungary, welcomed its first foreign users in January 2018. On 10 January 2025 China broke ground on its Advanced Attosecond Laser Facility in Dongguan, Guangdong: ten beamlines, four of them in Xi'an, due in five years; its chief engineer called it the second attosecond laser facility to start construction anywhere. The barrier is also dropping: in 2020 a University of Central Florida team made attosecond pulses with commercially available industrial lasers.

What may change next

Two lines are concrete, and neither has left the prototype stage. One is chip inspection: features on today's chips are a few nanometres across, and L’Huillier's Lund group runs a harmonic source based on an industrial laser, developed with a Dutch semiconductor company, meant as a diagnostic beam to check that everything on a chip is where it should be. It is, she says, still a prototype. The other is blood testing: Krausz's group uses ultrashort infrared pulses to record “molecular fingerprints” of blood plasma, has seen differences between samples from 470 lung-cancer patients and 470 healthy controls, and has launched a long-term study of 15,000 volunteers in Hungary. That is a long way from an approved test; whether it can beat existing methods on earliness or accuracy is still open.

What it does not mean

It does not mean we have “seen electrons orbit the nucleus.” The committee's background says plainly that, strictly speaking, the position and revolution of an electron are still not observable; what can be measured are timing differences as electrons leave atoms, and how the electron cloud as a whole sloshes. Nor is attosecond light just a faster laser pointer: the pulses are extreme ultraviolet, do not travel far in air, and need vacuum, steel tubing and pumps.

A way of thinking to borrow

When a process is faster than every ruler you own, stop only sharpening the ruler and let the process make the ruler. The attosecond flash comes from the electron's own round trip, and measuring it borrows the electric field of the very laser that made it as a clock.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Performance profiling · sampling interval: a profiler that samples every 10 ms cannot see a 1 ms lock contention; it gives you an average. Femtosecond lasers left physicists in the same place, measuring an averaged electron cloud. Attosecond pulses push the sampling interval below the event. And the probe disturbs the target here too: L’Huillier says in her lecture that the neon delay difference she measured comes essentially from the infrared probe light itself.
  • Distributed tracing · equivalent-time sampling: an attosecond experiment does not film an electron in one take. The same event is repeated many times, the delay between two beams shifted a little each time, and the results stitched into a timeline. That is the structure of an oscilloscope's equivalent-time sampling, or of stitching spans from countless requests into one trace: it needs a repeatable event and a shared clock, and here the clock is the infrared laser's field. Trigger with light, then look after a delay, is also the skeleton of this site's 2026 medicine prize (optogenetics).
  • Quantum mechanics · tunnelling: step one of the three-step model is tunnelling. In this site's 2025 physics prize, tunnelling by a whole circuit is the phenomenon being measured; here, tunnelling by a single electron is the engine that makes the light.
  • Philosophy · Heisenberg's observables: the committee's background opens in 1925, with Heisenberg arguing that the new theory should rest only on observables, and treating the position and orbital period of an electron as unobservable in principle. A century on, part of that has entered the lab. “Observable” turns out to be a border that moves with our instruments.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. A shutter must be faster than the motion; electrons in atoms move on attosecond scales, a billionth of a billionth of a second.
  2. A light pulse must hold at least one full oscillation, and one infrared cycle lasts about 2.6 femtoseconds: the femtosecond wall.
  3. In 1987 at Saclay, L’Huillier shone infrared laser light into noble gas and unexpectedly saw a long row of equally bright high harmonics.
  4. The three-step model: the electron tunnels out, is flung away and pulled back by the laser field, and emits its energy as XUV light.
  5. Many high harmonics with aligned peaks add up to a flash a few hundred attoseconds long every half laser cycle.
  6. In 2001 Agostini used RABBITT to measure a train of 250-attosecond pulses, and Krausz isolated a single pulse of about 650 attoseconds.
  7. Attosecond shutters showed the photoelectric effect is not instantaneous: electrons from different neon orbitals leave tens of attoseconds apart.
  8. The 2010 neon delay disagreed with theory until a 2017 Lund experiment separated out a second, hidden process.
  9. Attosecond science is still in labs and big facilities; chip inspection and blood fingerprinting are prototypes, and nobody has seen electrons orbit.
  10. Attosecond experiments build timelines from repeated events and scanned delays, the same structure as equivalent-time sampling and distributed tracing.

// Think further

Corkum co-authored the three-step model but was not included. When the prize honours “experimental methods,” where does theory go?
The wording of the citation itself draws the boundary. Yet the three-step model was not just an after-the-fact explanation: it told experimenters to pick the harmonics at the end of the plateau, and Krausz's isolated pulse followed a theoretical prediction. How do you weigh the person who says where to dig against the person who digs? Is the three-person cap flattening a collaboration network into a list of names?
High harmonics turned up in a search for fluorescence. What kind of lab culture treats a surprise as signal rather than noise?
The Saclay team was measuring something else, and the plateau contradicted theory. They could have filed it as an instrument quirk; they followed it, for thirty years. Is there a dashboard line in your own systems that “shouldn't be there” and is quietly filtered out as noise?
Measuring disturbs the target: the neon delay difference comes mainly from the probe light. How do we separate what is measured from what the measurement adds?
It resembles a Heisenbug in software: add logging and the bug disappears. Attosecond physics works out the probe's effect and subtracts it. But when the probe is as fast as the thing it probes, does that subtraction still have a clean meaning?
