LITERATURE · 2024

Turn the question around: can the dead save the living?

A novelist born in Gwangju writes about bodies, massacres and people who refuse to say goodbye, so that readers neither forget nor are left with nothing but hatred

Han KangSouth Korea · 1/1
for her intense poetic prose that confronts historical traumas and exposes the fragility of human life
2026-10-26 · BigCat

For years she wrote two questions on the first page of every new diary. To write Human Acts, she had to flip them: not whether the living can save the dead, but whether the dead can save the living.

A sudden downpour, a crowd of strangers pressed under an awning, and across the street another crowd under another awning. Most of us have stood there.

At eight, a Korean girl coming out of an afternoon abacus lesson stood exactly there. Two dozen children huddled under the eaves; across the street a small group mirrored them. With the rain soaking her arms and calves she suddenly understood that every one of those people was living as an "I" in their own right, seeing this same rain, feeling this same damp on their faces.

That was the story Han Kang told at the Nobel banquet in Stockholm on 10 December 2024. Decades of reading and writing, she said, had been a way of reliving that moment of wonder: following the thread of language into the depths of another heart.

A city, and a photo book she wasn't meant to see

She was born in 1970 in Gwangju, in the south of South Korea. Her father, Han Seung-won, is a novelist; the family had no proper furniture, she recalls, just "windows, doors and books". She moved house and school often, and in the month before new friends arrived it was the books that protected her. One winter at fourteen she read a short story in which a young man throws sawdust into a stove and the flame lights up his eyes. She felt the heat and the red on her own skin, and decided to become a writer.

In January 1980, when she was nine, the family moved to Seoul. Roughly four months later came the mass killings in Gwangju: students and residents resisting the military powers behind a coup were killed with clubs, bayonets and guns. At twelve she found a "Gwangju Photo Book", printed in secret by survivors and bereaved families, shelved upside down at home, and looked through it when no adults were around. The ruined faces raised one question: is this the act of one human towards another? Then a photo of an endless queue of people waiting to give blood outside a hospital raised the same question again. The two answers would not fit together. It became a knot she could not undo.

She debuted as a poet in 1993, published her first short story the following year, and has written fiction in Korean ever since. In her Nobel lecture on 7 December 2024 she described finding a "Book of Poems" she had stapled together at eight. What is love? "It is the gold thread connecting between our hearts."

Reading the citation: white and red

"Intense poetic prose": she began as a poet, and her novels keep a poem's density. The presentation speech, given by the author and Academy member Ellen Mattson, opens by saying that two colours meet in Han's writing. White is snow, sorrow and death; red is life, and also pain, blood and the cut of a knife.

"Confronts historical traumas": two episodes of state violence. Human Acts (2014) is set in Gwangju in 1980; We Do Not Part (2021) on Jeju Island in the late 1940s, where tens of thousands of people, children and the elderly among them, were shot on suspicion of being collaborators.

"Exposes the fragility of human life": the body is the landing point. In his bio-bibliography Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, calls her work a "double exposure of pain", mental and physical torment mirroring each other. She lets the souls of the dead separate from their bodies and witness their own annihilation; the unidentified, unburied corpses recall Sophocles's Antigone.

What actually happens in the books: a refusal, and a "you"

In The Vegetarian (2007), an ordinary wife named Yeong-hye stops eating meat after a nightmare. Her husband and her authoritarian father push back hard; her brother-in-law, a video artist, becomes obsessed with her passive body; she ends up in a psychiatric clinic while her sister In-hye tries to bring her back to "normal" life. Han said in her lecture that she was living inside a few questions: can a person ever be completely innocent? To what depths can we reject violence? Refusing violence, Yeong-hye ends up drinking only water, convinced she is becoming a plant, and so speeds towards death in her bid to save herself. Han set the last scene in an ambulance because she wanted Yeong-hye to stay alive:

"In-hye stares fiercely at the trees. As if waiting for an answer."— The Vegetarian, closing pages · tr. Deborah Smith · Nobel Prize excerpt

Olsson points to a key choice of method: the book offers no simple explanations. The refusal comes suddenly, and Yeong-hye barely speaks.

Human Acts was harder. She first imagined Gwangju as one layer of a novel, then decided to face it squarely: nine hours a day for a month she read more than 900 testimonies, then other cases of state violence and mass killing worldwide, until her remaining faith in people shattered and she nearly gave up. Then she read the diary of Park Yong-jun, a night-school teacher who chose to stay in the YWCA building near the provincial headquarters knowing soldiers would return before dawn. On that last night he wrote: "Why, God, must I have a conscience that pricks and pains me so? I wish to live." She knew "with the clarity of lightning" that her diary questions had to be reversed: Can the past help the present? Can the dead save the living?

The result is the book's most distinctive move: the first chapter addresses fifteen-year-old Dong-ho as "you". In the excerpt on the Nobel site, "you" sit on the gym steps logging coffin numbers in a ledger, then replace the candles beside the sheeted bodies:

"the tiny blue-tinged core that clasps the wick, its trembling shape recalling that of a heart, or perhaps an apple seed."— Human Acts, chapter 1 · tr. Deborah Smith · Nobel Prize excerpt

The Korean title, Sonyeon-i onda, ends in the present tense of "to come". The moment the boy is addressed as you, she said, he wakes in the dim light and walks into the present. Gwangju stops being one city's proper noun and becomes a common noun for any time and place where human cruelty and dignity exist in extreme parallel.

Her working notebook for the book held these lines: life seeks to live, life is warm; to die is to grow cold, to have snow settle on one's face rather than melt. "To kill is to make cold."

Why her: a writer who is "hiding very well"

She is the first South Korean literature laureate, and AFP reported her as the first Asian woman writer to win. Her path to world readers ran largely through one translator: Deborah Smith, a British woman who only began learning Korean in 2009, published The Vegetarian in English in 2015, and shared the 2016 International Booker Prize with Han. The White Book (2016) was shortlisted for the same prize in 2018, and the French translation of We Do Not Part won the Prix Médicis étranger in 2023.

She herself stayed cool about the noise. When the call came she had just finished dinner with her son, and planned to celebrate "quietly" with tea; she doesn't drink. Asked in December by the Nobel site about all the attention, she replied: "Well, I'm hiding very well."

◐ Off the podium

She held no press conference after the announcement. Her father, speaking to reporters in Jangheung, relayed her reasoning: with the wars in Ukraine and Gaza bringing out bodies every day, what was there to celebrate?

The fame had another side. AFP counted at least 1.06 million copies sold in South Korea within a week. Almost at once, a parliamentary audit revealed that schools in Gyeonggi Province, prompted by conservative groups, had discarded more than 2,500 books over sexual content, The Vegetarian among them, and a national parents' federation launched a petition to have it classed as harmful to minors. The English translation had its own fight: in 2017 the Seoul-based academic Charse Yun summarised Korean criticism of its omissions, embellishments and errors, quoting one translator who called it an adaptation. Smith replied that there can be no translation that is not "creative".

◎ What it means for the rest of us

What reading her can give you

A different tense for trauma. We tend to file pain in the past tense: it's over. Han insists on the present: the boy is coming, and Gwangju recurs wherever cruelty and dignity stand side by side. That isn't wallowing. She wants remembrance without turning the rememberer into a vessel of hatred. Afterwards you understand better why some families spend decades searching for a fragment of bone.

Practice in softening the edges of the self. Reading literature, she said in her December interview, means entering another person's interior and coming back to yourself, over and over, until "the boundary of yourself becomes flexible". She writes cold, pain and hunger so precisely that your body reacts before your judgement does.

Where to start

On the prize-day phone call Han herself suggested her latest novel, We Do Not Part (English translation by e. yaewon and Paige Aniyah Morris, 2025), adding that it connects directly with Human Acts; it is long and heavy. For a gentler entry, try The White Book (tr. Deborah Smith, 2017): short pieces about white things, addressed to an older sister who died two hours after birth. The best-known, The Vegetarian (tr. Deborah Smith, 2015), is the easiest read and the most unsettling.

What it does not mean

The Vegetarian is not a book about diet, nor a simple feminist manifesto: the committee stresses it offers no simple explanation, and Han says her question was whether a person can wholly refuse violence. "Trauma literature" is not the same as accusation: Human Acts holds both the massacre and the blood-donation queue, two extremes in one species. Nor is the prize a political verdict on Korean history: of the citation's three phrases, two point at language and at life itself.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · no global view: the moment under the eaves is lesson one of distributed systems. Each node has only its local view; there is no god-node that sees everyone, and every "I" is watching the same rain. Literature works like a message between nodes, carrying another node's internal state across. The difference is that messages sync data, while she hopes to send body heat; she calls it "an electric current".
  • Buddhism · the paradox of non-harm: Yeong-hye pushes "do no harm" to the limit, stops feeding even her own body, and moves towards death. Olsson notes her work's close ties to Eastern thought. Buddhism teaches non-killing, and also the middle way; enforce any constraint as an absolute and the system loses the room it needs to run. The novel stages that paradox without resolving it.
  • Psychology · trauma in the present tense: survivors often describe certain memories not as remembered but as happening again. The present-tense grammar of Human Acts shares that structure. The difference is that she turns involuntary recurrence into a chosen refusal to say goodbye.
  • Philosophy · Antigone and the bones: the state forbids burial; one person insists on it. Olsson links the unclaimed bodies of Human Acts to Antigone, and Inseon's mother in We Do Not Part, searching for decades for her brother's bones, has the same shape: mourning itself as resistance to violence.
  • Literature · fragments and long sentences: The White Book mourns in short fragments, the same gesture as Anne Carson keeping Sappho's gaps on the page. Krasznahorkai says there is no hope between good and evil; Han flips the question and lets the dead rescue the living. Two laureates in neighbouring years, both writing on with no guarantee.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Sheltering from rain at eight, Han Kang realised every person under the eaves was a separate "I" watching it fall.
  2. A secretly printed Gwangju photo book showed Han Kang both massacre and a blood-donation queue: two irreconcilable questions.
  3. The "poetic prose" in the citation has a history: Han Kang debuted as a poet in 1993.
  4. The presentation speech says only two colours meet in her work: white for snow and grief, red for life and wounds.
  5. In The Vegetarian, Yeong-hye refuses meat to refuse violence and speeds towards death trying to save herself.
  6. A dead teacher's diary reversed the question: not whether the living can save the dead, but whether the dead can save the living.
  7. Human Acts uses "you" and the present tense, turning Gwangju into a common noun for every site of violence.
  8. We Do Not Part moves horizontally through snow, vertically down into 1948, and ends with a candle on the seabed.
  9. Start with We Do Not Part or the fragmentary White Book; The Vegetarian is not a book about diet.
  10. Each node has only a local view; literature is the message that carries another person's inner state, and warmth, across.

// Think further

In what sense can the dead save the living?
The dead can do nothing. Yet Park Yong-jun's diary got a writer who had lost faith in people writing again, and her book makes later readers rethink dignity. Perhaps "saving" means that someone's choice in their last hours becomes a reference point others calibrate against. Do you have such a reference point?
Does addressing a dead boy as "you" pull us closer or push us away?
NPR's reviewer felt the second person created distance: I don't need to be Dong-ho to feel with him. Han's reason lies in Korean tense: once addressed as you, he arrives in the present. Can both be right? Notice which kinds of writing make you lean back, and which make you stop.
When a book is pulled from a school library, who is being protected?
The Vegetarian contains sex and violence; the parents' worry isn't invented. But the novel is about a woman whose body everyone around her controls "for her own good". How does pulling the book relate to what the book is about? If it were your call, where would you set the line?
How "faithful" can a translation be?
Smith's version adds and omits, and it is also the version that brought Han to the world. Translation is lossy compression: lossless isn't possible, so the question is what to drop and what to keep. If you can't read Korean, whose Han Kang are you reading?
