LITERATURE · 2025

A four-hundred-page sentence, because the things that matter won't fit in short ones

A Hungarian novelist whose sentences barely stop writes ruined farms, a town visited by a dead whale, ordinary people at the edge of the end, and a heron standing in a Kyoto river: the world is coming apart, and art is still worth it

László KrasznahorkaiHungary · 1/1
for his compelling and visionary oeuvre that, in the midst of apocalyptic terror, reaffirms the power of art
2026-10-22 · BigCat

He opened his Nobel lecture by saying he had meant to talk about hope, "but as my stores of hope have definitively come to an end, I will now speak about angels."

Kyoto, the Kamo River, early morning. A great snow-white heron stands in the shallows, perfectly still, staring into the foam. On the embankment people stroll, someone rides past on a battered bicycle, the northbound number 3 bus goes by. Nobody looks.

The passage that describes this bird is a single sentence that runs on for most of a page. Then it adds a twist: even if some passenger did happen to glance over, he would probably see nothing. Human attention rises and falls in waves; it cannot stay at its peak. The heron's strike, fast and exact, lands in the trough of the watcher's attention. He sees only a bird leaning forward, doing nothing, and misses the one thing that might have given his life meaning.

This is the opening of Seiobo There Below (2008) by the Hungarian writer László Krasznahorkai. Reading him feels a lot like being that passenger. The sentence keeps flowing; let your mind drift for a second and the moment that mattered has already passed.

A small town, and a copy of The Castle he couldn't understand

He was born in 1954 in Gyula, a small town in southeast Hungary near the Romanian border, and lived there until he was 18. His brother, six years older, ran with a crowd of big boys who talked about Kafka and Dostoyevsky. At eleven or twelve, wanting in, he pulled a book off his brother's shelf one night. It happened to be Kafka's The Castle. He read every page and never worked out what the castle was. It remains, he says, "the most beautiful secret in literature," and without it he would never have become a writer.

As a teenager he played piano and listened to classical music, beat music, jazz and free jazz; writing wasn't the plan. In his late twenties, unable to leave Soviet-occupied Hungary, he drifted around the country. Under Dostoyevsky's spell he went to live among the poorest people, took a job among miners, and practised writing good sentences in his head. His first novel, Satantango, came out in 1985 and was a literary sensation at home. He crossed the border for the first time only in 1987 and found, he says, that the sky really was blue and the grass green. "Under the Soviet time, we lost the colours."

He writes in Hungarian, a language that stacks meaning into suffixes. A London Review of Books critic recalls trying to buy a train ticket in Budapest and saying szeretlek (I love you) instead of szeretnék (I would like). Hungarian also splits "you" into an intimate and a formal form, and it lends itself to irony and meanings tucked inside meanings. When the Nobel committee called, he said the prize gave him the strength to keep using "this little language."

Reading the citation: terror is the setting, art is the subject

"Apocalyptic terror." In his novels the end of the world doesn't arrive from outer space. It's a small place slowly going wrong. Satantango is set on an all-but-abandoned collective farm just before the fall of communism, where a handful of poor families wait through the long autumn rains for something to happen, until Irimiás, whom everyone believed dead, walks back in. In The Melancholy of Resistance (1989) a ghostly circus rolls into a town in a Carpathian valley with the carcass of a giant whale as its star attraction, and riot follows. After reading it, Susan Sontag called him contemporary literature's "master of the apocalypse."

"Compelling." This is about sentences that pull you under. His translator, the poet George Szirtes, described the experience as "the slow lava flow of narrative … the vast black river of type." You can't skim it. You slow down and let its rhythm carry you.

"Reaffirms the power of art." This is where the citation lands. In his presentation speech the committee chair, Anders Olsson, said Krasznahorkai's greatness lies in holding two things at once: a gaze "entirely free of illusion" that sees how fragile human orders are, and "an unwavering faith in the power of literature." The seventeen stories of Seiobo There Below are arranged in a Fibonacci sequence. They go from the Kyoto heron to an unfinished painting by Perugino being hauled back to his birthplace by mostly drunken assistants. The artist is usually offstage. Instead we get janitors, onlookers and craftsmen, standing beside a work they don't understand.

What actually happens on the page: a tango that walks back to where it began

The plot of Satantango is simple enough. Irimiás and his sidekick Petrina could be saviours or the last judgement. They talk the villagers into handing over all their savings, lead them to an estate even more derelict than the one they had, then pack them off one by one in different directions. The prophet is a police informer. In the second-to-last chapter, clerks are ordered to turn his foul-mouthed report into "a usable document," so that "that foul old bag of poisonous gossip" becomes "a transmitter of unreliable information."

The form is the point. The book has twelve chapters laid out like tango steps: six forward, six back. In the last chapter the estate's reclusive doctor picks up his pencil and writes, word for word, the novel's opening lines. The epigraph is from Kafka:

"In that case, I'll miss the thing by waiting for it."— epigraph to Satantango · quoted in the Nobel bio-bibliography

It isn't a stunt. The novel is about people going round in circles while convinced they are moving forward, so the book itself is built as a circle.

Herscht 07769 (2021) goes further. It is set in a present-day small town in Thuringia, Germany, amid social breakdown, arson and murder, against the backdrop of Bach's legacy. Its hero, Florian, is a huge, kind-hearted young man of limited intellect, a Dostoyevskian holy fool. Olsson calls the whole novel "a single sentence extending to four hundred pages." In the excerpt on the Nobel site, dialogue runs straight into narration with no quotation marks; swearing, the price of pork liver and Bach's shifts from major to minor all share one breath. An amateur orchestra that is good at the Beatles has been rehearsing Bach in a school gym for nearly three years without being ready to perform, and its leader keeps the principal at bay with:

"Johann Sebastian doesn't give himself up so easily."— Herscht 07769 · tr. Ottilie Mulzet · excerpt on NobelPrize.org

Why not use full stops? In a December 2025 interview in Stockholm he gave an example. Suppose you need to tell someone that for twenty years you have loved them and never had the courage to say so. "You cannot say that in a beautiful way in short sentences." A long sentence still needs structure, he added, and his comes from music.

Why him: a writer out of hope, talking about angels, dignity and ten metres

The English-speaking world found him slowly. The Melancholy of Resistance appeared in English only in 1998, and it was Sontag who recommended him to the American publisher New Directions. Many people first met his work through the films of the Hungarian director Béla Tarr: the film of Satantango runs seven and a half hours and opens with a nine-minute take of a herd of cows. He won the Man Booker International Prize in 2015. The previous Hungarian-born winner of the literature prize was Imre Kertész, in 2002.

His Nobel lecture on 7 December 2025 is a piece of his fiction in its own right: long breathless sentences in three movements. First, the "new angels," who have no wings and bring no message. They walk among us in street clothes, and their gaze begs us for a message, which we cannot give. Second, human dignity: we invented fire, language, science and Bach, and then, "thanks to the devices that you yourself invented, destroying imagination, you are left with only short-term memory now." Third, rebellion. On a muggy afternoon in early-1990s Berlin, a homeless man stepped past the yellow line at the end of a U-Bahn platform and, in obvious pain, tried to urinate onto the tracks. A policeman shouted from the opposite platform, but to reach him he had to run up the stairs and across the walkway. Between them lay a trench about ten metres wide. The moment has stayed frozen in Krasznahorkai's mind ever since:

"the Good will never catch flailing Evil, because between Good and Evil there is no hope, none whatsoever."

The committee didn't read this as despair. In his presentation speech Olsson suggested there may be "a hidden, Heraclitian order in the disorder, amidst the wars that tear our poor world apart." Hope isn't lost, he said; we just don't know how or when it will emerge.

◐ Off the podium

When the Nobel call came he was at a sick friend's flat in Frankfurt. His first words were "This is more than a catastrophe," a nod to Samuel Beckett's reaction to his own prize, before he explained that he meant happiness and pride. His plan for the day had been to visit the German registration office to report a new address: "I didn't count on these fantastic news, and that's why I cannot make changes."

The prize also landed on his publishers. In Korea, Alma Books, his only publisher there, had been selling one or two copies a day of its six titles. On the night of the announcement the bookseller Aladdin sold 1,800 copies, far beyond the 1,000 it sold when Annie Ernaux won in 2022.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

What reading him can give you

A workout for attention. His sentences give you no paragraph breaks and no full stops, so you have to follow them in one go. After the prize, Alma's CEO said we live in a time when people burn themselves out on short-form content, and she hoped readers would get lost in his long, meandering sentences. Read a page of him and you can feel exactly where your attention rises and where it drops into a trough.

A way of living with bad news. He doesn't console you or urge optimism. His characters still carry a painting home, rehearse Bach, offer a stranger some warmth. Acting without hope can be more honest, and more workable, than "staying positive."

Where to start

Satantango (tr. George Szirtes, New Directions, 2012). Swedish Academy member Steve Sem-Sandberg recommends it: bleak on the surface, he writes, but shot through with the author's characteristic humour. The sentences are long, but each one eventually lands, and the book isn't long (288 pages in English). Moderately hard. For something gentler, try the stories in The World Goes On (2017); for his contemplative side, Olsson's pick, Seiobo There Below, tr. Ottilie Mulzet.

What it does not mean

Not "apocalyptic literature equals literature of despair." The subject of the citation is art, not the apocalypse, and the academicians keep mentioning his humour. Not "long sentences are just stream of consciousness." He insists a long sentence needs structure, and his comes from a feel for music rather than wherever the mind wanders. And not "if you've seen the Béla Tarr film you've read the book." The long takes are a translation with their own rhythm; Werckmeister Harmonies, made from The Melancholy of Resistance, ends differently from the novel.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Neuroscience · fluctuating attention: the heron passage is about attention that can't hold its peak and keeps dropping into troughs. That has the same structure as sampling: an event shorter than your sampling interval goes unseen, and a polling loop misses an interrupt. The heron's strike is an event that falls between two samples. The difference is that he writes the miss as fate, where an engineer would add an interrupt.
  • Distributed systems · the transaction that never commits: a full stop is a commit point; once you write it, the sentence is final. His sentences are full of "namely," revising what was just said while the sentence is still going, like a long-running transaction that keeps amending itself instead of committing. He calls himself, over his whole life, "a corrector of myself." The reader has to behave like the database and hold every uncommitted state in memory.
  • Buddhism · emptiness as possibility: in the excerpt the motionless heron is described as "nothingness", yet a nothingness "clearly identical with anything that can happen." That is the structure of śūnyatā: emptiness isn't absence but what hasn't yet collapsed into any one particular thing. His banquet thanks included the city of Kyoto and "Prince Siddhartha."
  • Literature · punctuation as meaning: Anne Carson, the 2026 laureate, keeps Sappho's missing text visible as square brackets; Krasznahorkai takes the full stops out so the sentence never breaks. Both make the same move: punctuation is not typesetting but the structure of the work. One preserves the break, one refuses it, and each forces the reader to look at the shape of the words themselves.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Seiobo There Below opens with a motionless heron in Kyoto's Kamo River, whose strike always lands in the trough of a passer-by's attention.
  2. At eleven or twelve László Krasznahorkai read Kafka's The Castle, didn't understand it, and still calls it literature's most beautiful secret.
  3. Unable to leave Soviet-era Hungary, he lived among the rural poor and practised writing good sentences in his head.
  4. He has always written in Hungarian, a "little language" that stacks meaning into suffixes and lends itself to irony.
  5. In the citation, apocalyptic terror is only the setting; the real subject is the power of art, and the academicians stress his humour.
  6. Satantango has twelve chapters, six steps forward and six back, and its last chapter ends with the doctor writing the opening lines.
  7. Herscht 07769 is a single four-hundred-page sentence that sets arson and social breakdown against Bach's legacy.
  8. Things that truly matter can't be said beautifully in short sentences, Krasznahorkai argues, but a long sentence still needs musical structure.
  9. The easiest way into Krasznahorkai is Satantango; the stories in The World Goes On are gentler still.
  10. A sentence without full stops behaves like a transaction that never commits, revising itself as it goes while the reader holds all the state.

// Think further

If "between Good and Evil there is no hope," why write at all?
His answer doesn't seem to be "because there is hope." It's that art doesn't need hope as a precondition. The janitors and craftsmen in Seiobo There Below don't know what the work they serve means; they just do their part well. How much of what you do would still be worth doing without the promise that it will succeed?
Are long sentences a torment for the reader, or protection for things that can only be said that way?
Short sentences travel well and are easy to quote out of context. His example is a confession twenty years late: the cause, the hesitation and the consequence all crowded into one breath. Break it up and it becomes a notification. But the reverse question matters too: which information really should be chopped short? Does an incident report need the same shape as a confession?
The "new angels" bring no message and wait for us to give them one. What kind of situation is that?
The old angels brought commands from above; the new ones hand the burden of the question back to us. You can read this as religion's exit, or as a heavier kind of freedom: nobody will say the meaning for you. In the lecture we have nothing to give. Is that despair, or a seat not yet filled?
