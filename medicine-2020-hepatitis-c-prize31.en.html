A disease that spent fourteen years being called “not”

Hepatitis after transfusion that was neither A nor B. Three people, three steps: prove it exists, fish it out of a million clones, then show it needs nothing else

Harvey J. AlterNational Institutes of Health · 1/3Michael HoughtonUniversity of Alberta · 1/3Charles M. RiceRockefeller University · 1/3
for the discovery of Hepatitis C virus
2026-11-12 · BigCat

It was first recognised by patients’ own antibodies. Today a couple of months of pills cure it, and there is still no vaccine.

Give blood, or have bloodwork before an operation, and somewhere on the list of tests is a line for hepatitis C antibodies (anti-HCV). For almost everyone it comes back negative, and nobody gives it a second look.

Before 1989 that line did not exist. Someone could come through major surgery, receive a few units of blood, and weeks later feel worn out, with liver enzymes climbing. Test for hepatitis A: negative. Hepatitis B: negative. The chart could only say “non-A, non-B hepatitis”, a name that described the disease only by what it wasn’t. Worse, most people carrying it felt fine and kept donating blood.

That “not” lasted fourteen years. Turning it into a virus with a name, a sequence, a test and eventually a cure took three people and three steps.

Hepatitis B could be screened out. Transfusion hepatitis barely moved

Until the 1960s, people who had surgery or repeated transfusions faced up to a 30% risk of chronic hepatitis. In the 1960s Baruch Blumberg identified the antigen of hepatitis B virus (it earned him the 1976 Nobel Prize), and blood banks began excluding donors who carried it. As a young doctor, Alter had worked with Blumberg on that very antigen.

Alter then moved to the blood bank at the US National Institutes of Health (NIH), and stayed with the problem for his whole career. His method was patient bookkeeping: follow transfused patients forward in time and freeze their serum, tube after tube. The numbers from the early 1970s were alarming. Excluding hepatitis B carriers prevented only about 20% of post-transfusion hepatitis; the other 80% had nothing to do with hepatitis B.

In 1973 Stephen Feinstone and Robert Purcell at NIH found the hepatitis A virus, and a test for it followed. Alter went back to his freezer. In 1975 they reported together on 22 patients with transfusion hepatitis who were negative for hepatitis B: not one showed signs of hepatitis A. The label “non-A, non-B” was coined then, as a working name everyone expected to retire soon.

In 1978 Alter took the decisive step. He inoculated five chimpanzees, the only animal besides humans that can be infected, with serum from four patients and one suspect donor, keeping a sixth animal as a control. All five developed hepatitis; the control did not. Serum from chronic patients transmitted the disease too, so the agent could persist in a person and stay infectious for a long time. Later experiments showed it had a lipid envelope and measured roughly 30 to 60 nanometres across. Unseen, uncultured, but with an outline.

Then every classical virus-hunting technique was thrown at it, and for more than ten years nothing worked.

Don’t look for what it looks like. Look for what the body recognises

In 1982 Houghton, with a PhD from King’s College London, joined Chiron, a biotech company in California, and began the hunt that same year. Beside him were two colleagues, Qui-Lim Choo and George Kuo.

The first plan was straightforward. Extract nucleic acids from infected chimpanzees, build a huge library of DNA fragments (a cDNA library: every bit of genetic material in a sample, chopped up and filed piece by piece), and search it for the viral pieces. The trouble was that nearly every fragment was chimpanzee. They tried subtracting sequences also found in a healthy liver. That failed too.

So they tried an approach no one had proven. They packaged the fragments into a phage and delivered them into bacteria, so that each bacterial colony translated its own small stretch of genes into protein. Then, with no knowledge of the virus at all, they washed the colonies with serum from a patient with severe non-A, non-B hepatitis. The reasoning: this person’s immune system had been fighting the virus for a long time, so the blood must contain antibodies that recognise viral proteins.

They screened about a million colonies, and one lit up. Its DNA was neither chimpanzee nor human. Clone 5-1-1 matched an RNA of about 10,000 bases, distantly related to known RNA viruses; the virus was placed in the Flaviviridae family and named hepatitis C virus (HCV). In April 1989 two papers appeared together in Science: one reported the clone, the other used it straight away to build an antibody test, which found the virus in about 80% of chronic post-transfusion non-A, non-B patients from Italy and Japan.

Correlation is not causation: the last piece was RNA written in a test tube

By 1989 the evidence was strong but not closed. Patients had antibodies to the virus, and blood transmitted the disease, but could something else in that blood be helping? To show the virus alone was enough, you needed something clean, containing only the viral genome, and you needed it to cause the disease by itself.

Rice, then at Washington University in St. Louis, worked on yellow fever virus and already knew how to transcribe infectious viral RNA from a DNA template in a test tube. In his autobiography he recalls that, for a new assistant professor, taking on a virus nobody could grow in the lab didn’t make much sense. A phone call from Feinstone, by then at the FDA, changed his mind.

Their first HCV genome, injected into a chimpanzee’s liver, did nothing. Kunitada Shimotohno’s group in Tokyo and Rice’s group, in close succession, found an overlooked conserved stretch at the very end of the genome. Adding it still didn’t work.

The problem was that RNA viruses copy themselves sloppily, so every sequence cloned from a patient might carry a few fatal typos. Kolykhalov, in Rice’s lab, assembled and checked hundreds of full-length clones, compared them position by position, and built a consensus sequence by majority. This time two chimpanzees developed virus in the blood and liver inflammation, and both went on to chronic infection, just like the human disease. The 1997 paper’s abstract ends by saying it proves that HCV alone is sufficient to cause disease.

After eight years of working on HCV, we finally had an infectious clone.— Charles M. Rice · Nobel autobiography · 2020

From screening blood to curing with pills: another twenty-odd years

The first thing the discovery changed was the blood supply. With an antibody test, blood banks could exclude donors who had no symptoms at all, and in many parts of the world post-transfusion hepatitis essentially disappeared.

Treatment came far more slowly. For years there was only interferon plus ribavirin, weak and full of side effects; long-acting interferon in the late 1990s helped somewhat. The bottleneck: Rice’s clone infected chimpanzees but wouldn’t grow in a dish, so drugs couldn’t be tested efficiently. In 1999 Ralf Bartenschlager’s lab in Germany built a “replicon” that copied itself in liver cancer cells, giving drug makers a firing range. Only when Takaji Wakita in Japan found an unusually vigorous strain, more than fifteen years after the discovery, was there a complete cell-culture system.

Then came direct-acting antivirals (DAAs): sofosbuvir, which jams the viral copying enzyme, ledipasvir, which blocks another viral protein called NS5A, and others, used in combination. In December 2013 the US FDA approved sofosbuvir (sold as Sovaldi), among the first oral treatments that did not need interferon injections. Today a short course of pills cures more than 95% of those infected.

◐ Off the podium

Choo and Kuo, who found the virus alongside Houghton, were not included, and their names do not appear in the Nobel press release (the scientific background does mention them). Back in 2013, Canada’s Gairdner Foundation chose Alter, Houghton and Daniel Bradley of the US CDC, a co-author on the 1989 cloning paper. Houghton turned down the $100,000 prize because Choo and Kuo were left out. “The three of us worked closely together for almost seven years to discover this very elusive and challenging virus using a novel approach in the field of infectious disease,” he wrote. The committee’s scientific background, for its part, credits Shimotohno, Jens Bukh, Bartenschlager and others at each later step.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Donated blood is safe. The hepatitis C line on a pre-op blood test and the screening of every donated unit come straight from the 1989 test paper. Hepatitis C is curable. The World Health Organization says direct-acting antivirals cure more than 95% of people infected, and the widely used generic combination of sofosbuvir and daclatasvir costs under US$50 for a full course in many low- and middle-income countries. When Sovaldi launched in the US in 2013, a 12-week course was listed at $84,000, around $1,000 a day.

The most persuasive case is Egypt, once among the countries hit hardest. From 2014 it ran free testing and treatment; its “100 million healthy lives” campaign tested over 60 million people and treated more than 4.1 million, and prevalence fell from 10% to 0.38%. In October 2023 Egypt became the first country to reach the WHO’s “gold tier” on the path to eliminating hepatitis C.

What may change next

The drugs exist; the missing piece is finding people. WHO figures: about 47 million people live with chronic hepatitis C, about 0.9 million are newly infected each year, and some 239,000 died of it in 2024. Of everyone infected between 2015 and 2024, only 36% knew it, and about a fifth of those diagnosed had been treated. The real variable for the next decade is whether testing reaches primary care, harm-reduction services and prisons. Vaccines are still in the lab and in early trials; there is no effective hepatitis C vaccine today.

What it does not mean

A, B and C are not one family. The letters for the five hepatitis viruses are just labels; the viruses belong to entirely different families. Hepatitis B is a DNA virus, C is an RNA virus, and the hepatitis B vaccine does nothing against C. A positive antibody test doesn’t mean a current infection. About 30% of people clear the virus on their own but stay antibody-positive for life; a test for viral RNA is needed to tell. And a Nobel doesn’t mean hepatitis C is gone: between “curable” and “cured for everyone” stand diagnosis rates and drug prices.

A way of thinking to borrow

When you can’t find something, look for whatever already recognises it. Houghton didn’t know what the virus looked like, but he knew the patient’s immune system did.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · majority reads: Rice’s consensus sequence is a majority vote across replicas. Every clone is a copy with random corruption and none can be trusted alone, but take the majority at each position and you recover a working original. Like a quorum read, it relies on errors being independent and rare. The twist is that no single virus in the population may ever have carried exactly that consensus; Rice assembled a version that never existed yet best represented them all.
  • Machine learning · borrowing a trained classifier: Houghton never learned to recognise the virus himself. He borrowed a detector the patient’s immune system had spent years training and used it to label a million unlabelled samples. Structurally this is weak supervision with an off-the-shelf model: the discriminative power comes from elsewhere; only the search space is yours.
  • Causal inference · necessary and sufficient: antibodies appearing with disease is correlation; a tube containing only the viral genome causing disease on its own is intervention. It is the same step the 2021 economics prize keeps pressing on: is some other variable behind the association you observed?
  • Ancient genomes and mRNA · the same family of technical problems: Houghton had to pick a few viral fragments out of a flood of host DNA, much as Pääbo in the 2022 medicine prize had to pick Neanderthal DNA out of contamination: signal drowned in host noise. And Rice showed that RNA transcribed in a test tube is executed by the body as if it were real, a fact later exploited in the other direction by the mRNA vaccines of the 2023 medicine prize.
  • Buddhism and epistemology · definition by negation: Buddhist logic has a way of approaching what can’t be named by saying what it is not. “Non-A, non-B” was medicine’s version: before any positive description, an entity is fenced off by exclusion, then confirmed to be really there by the chimpanzee experiments. What Alter did for over a decade was hold that empty slot open, so no other explanation could fill it.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Before hepatitis C virus was found, excluding hepatitis B carriers prevented only about 20% of post-transfusion hepatitis; the other 80% was unexplained.
  2. In 1975 Alter, Feinstone and colleagues showed these patients had neither hepatitis A nor B, and the stopgap name “non-A, non-B hepatitis” lasted until 1989.
  3. In 1978 Alter gave hepatitis to five chimpanzees with patient serum, proving a transmissible agent that could persist for years.
  4. For more than a decade, classical virology failed to find the non-A, non-B agent, which could be neither seen nor grown.
  5. Houghton, Choo and Kuo used a patient’s antibodies to screen about a million bacterial colonies and found a single viral clone, 5-1-1.
  6. The key to the immune screen was not knowing what the virus looked like, but borrowing what the patient’s immune system had already learned to recognise.
  7. Rice built a majority-vote consensus from hundreds of error-laden clones and in 1997 showed that hepatitis C RNA alone is enough to cause disease.
  8. Antibody testing for hepatitis C largely ended transfusion hepatitis in many countries, and oral direct-acting antivirals now cure over 95% of patients.
  9. There is still no hepatitis C vaccine, and most of the roughly 47 million chronically infected people are undiagnosed; the bottlenecks are testing and price.
  10. A consensus sequence works like a majority read in distributed systems: no single replica is trustworthy, yet a per-position majority recovers a usable original.

// Think further

What had to be true for Houghton’s immune screen to work, and where would it fail for a different disease?
The patient had to have made antibodies to the agent, and those antibodies had to recognise protein fragments made by bacteria. A pathogen that hides from the immune system, or whose key proteins only become recognisable when properly folded and modified, would slip through. The borrowed classifier decides what you can find, and also what you can’t.
Rice’s consensus sequence may never have existed in any real virus. Is it still “the virus”?
Think of the virus as a constantly mutating crowd rather than a fixed individual. The consensus is the crowd’s centre of mass, and the fact that it works shows the fatal errors are in the minority. It’s the same question as defining a species, or the “main line” of an open-source project: identity lives in the statistics of the group, not in any one member.
Is leaving out Choo and Kuo just the Nobel’s three-person rule, or a bias in how we tell science stories?
The rule caps the number, but who gets picked reflects a narrative: a collective effort told as three milestones, each with one name. Alter had an NIH team behind him; Rice had Kolykhalov and Feinstone. Worth asking: if you wrote this history, what unit would you give credit to — people, labs, or the one crucial idea?
If pills cure over 95%, why does hepatitis C still kill over two hundred thousand people a year?
Most infected people have no symptoms and don’t know; many who do know can’t get or afford treatment. Egypt shows the way through is bringing tests to people and making drugs cheap. Once the science is solved, what remains is a problem of distribution and trust.
