Winning the auction may be the bad news

A teacher and his former student, neighbours on the same street, worked out why smart bidders go wrong, then built a new kind of auction to sell the airwaves

Paul R. MilgromStanford University · 1/2Robert B. WilsonStanford University · 1/2
for improvements to auction theory and inventions of new auction formats
2026-11-16 · BigCat

Put a room of clever bidders together, and the winner is often the one who guessed worst.

You are bidding on a used camera online. The clock runs down, you add one last bid in the final seconds, and you win. The glow lasts about a minute. Then a small doubt arrives: why would none of the others go that high? Did they see something you missed?

Most people have felt that doubt and shrugged it off. The 2020 prize in economics went to two people who followed it all the way down: where it comes from, how a sensible bidder should respond, and finally how to build those answers into a working auction machine that sold tens of billions of dollars of radio spectrum.

An oil field nobody can see into

Auction theory starts in 1961, with William Vickrey (economics laureate, 1996). He studied a tidy special case, private values: what the item is worth to you is your own business, like a charity dinner with a celebrity. In that world he showed that the rising-price English auction, the falling-price Dutch auction and the two kinds of sealed bid all bring the seller the same expected revenue.

Plenty of valuable things are not like that. How much oil lies under an offshore tract is the same for every company; nobody just knows the number. That is a common value. Each company hires geologists, runs seismic tests and gets its own noisy estimate. Reading a case study of offshore oil-lease sales, Wilson saw the trap. Bids follow estimates, so the most optimistic company bids highest, which means the winner is usually the one who overestimated most. Winning tells you that nobody else was as hopeful as you, and that is bad news.

The effect got its name in 1971, when E. Capen and colleagues, writing in the Journal of Petroleum Technology, used simulations to explain why companies that won leases so often failed to profit, and called it the winner's curse. Wilson had already treated the situation as a game in a three-page note in 1969: sealed bidding by people holding different information. His answer was that a rational bidder deliberately bids below their own best estimate; the more uncertainty, the deeper the shading; and whoever knows less than the others bids lower still, or stays home.

The student fills in the middle

Most real auctions mix the two. Buying a flat, you care whether you love the layout (private) and what you could resell it for (common). That mixed case is far harder to analyse. The person who cracked it was Milgrom, who did his doctorate at Stanford in the 1970s with Wilson as his advisor.

In 1982 Milgrom and Robert Weber published a long paper in Econometrica that ranked the standard formats. On average the English auction raises the most, the sealed second-price auction comes next, and the Dutch and sealed first-price auctions come last. The reason condenses into the linkage principle: the more of the bidders' private information a format's price absorbs, the more it raises. In an English auction you watch where rivals drop out and learn roughly what they thought, so you can bid closer to your own estimate. In a Dutch auction the first person to shout stop takes the item, and nobody learns anything along the way.

The same paper gave sellers a counterintuitive tip: tell the buyers what you know. Commission an independent appraisal, publish the survey. Hiding information feels like it should help, but in this model more disclosure weakens the curse and bidders dare to bid higher. Authenticity certificates at art sales and published test-drilling results on oil tracts rest on the same logic.

From a charity dinner to a spectrum machine

In 1993, Milgrom recalls, he and Wilson were both still mostly interested in theory. That year Congress told the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) to stop handing out mobile licences through "beauty contests", where firms argued their merits, or through lotteries. The lotteries had gone badly: winners needed no qualifications, and some flipped their licences to real phone companies while others sat on them for years, leaving whole areas without service.

Spectrum cannot be sold lot by lot like antiques. For a company building a national network, a Chicago licence is only worth much if it can also get New York and Boston, so if Chicago goes first, it has no idea what to bid. In his Nobel lecture Milgrom says the inspiration came from silent auctions at charity dinners: everything on the tables at once, a sheet of paper in front of each item, people writing higher bids and drifting to other items when one gets too pricey. The flaw was sniping, bidders waiting until the final seconds so nobody could answer.

His and Wilson's Simultaneous Multiple Round Auction (SMRA) kept the simultaneous rising prices and changed three things. Bidding runs in discrete rounds, with results posted only between rounds. The auction ends only after a round with no new bid on any licence, so there is no last second to snipe in. And Milgrom invented the activity rule: you may not bid for more spectrum in a round than you did in the round before, so you cannot sit back, watch, and pounce at the end. To convince the FCC it was workable, he had the whole thing coded up in Excel spreadsheets.

“well, I may not know the best way to do it, but I can do better than that”— Milgrom, recalling his reaction to a proposal he was shown in 1993 · telephone interview on the day of the announcement · 2020

The FCC first used the format in July 1994: 10 licences, 47 rounds, 617 million dollars, for airwaves the government had previously given away practically for free.

From millions to eighty billion

SMRA became the standard way to sell spectrum, used in Finland, India, Canada, Norway, Poland, Spain, the UK, Sweden and Germany among others. According to the committee, FCC auctions in this format alone brought in more than 120 billion dollars from 1994 to 2014. Britain's 2000 3G sale, designed with an academic team led by Ken Binmore and Paul Klemperer, raised £22.5 billion and was widely called the biggest auction ever. The Dutch auction right after it used an off-the-shelf design that did little to attract newcomers, and drew little competition and low prices.

SMRA has a weak spot: no package bids. A firm can win half the licences its plan needs and lose the other half, and the half it won becomes a burden. Milgrom and colleagues went on to design the Combinatorial Clock Auction, which accepts bids on bundles; after the UK adopted it in 2008, about nine more countries followed.

The hardest job was the 2017 Incentive Auction: buy spectrum back from TV broadcasters, then sell it to mobile carriers. About 2,400 stations had to be repacked so that no two stations too close together share a channel, which is the graph-colouring problem, NP-complete. Kevin Leyton-Brown's group at the University of British Columbia wrote custom algorithms for the feasibility checks that ran about a thousand times faster than the best commercial ones. The reverse auction cleared 14 channels for 10.1 billion dollars; the forward auction sold licences for 19.8 billion. Prices kept climbing: in January 2021 the first phase of the FCC's C-band auction closed with gross proceeds above 80.9 billion dollars.

◐ Off the podium

When the news came, nobody could reach Milgrom: he had switched his phone off to sleep. Wilson and his wife walked about 40 metres across the street and rang his doorbell. Wilson also recalls that as an MBA student in 1959 he got a failing grade on a competitive-bidding case because he used mathematics instead of the mandatory "administrative point of view". In 2020 the economist Glen Weyl and Stefano Feltri criticised the 2017 incentive auction, arguing that the small circle of designers sat too close to consultants advising private-equity bidders. Milgrom replied at length; the critics stressed they had alleged nothing illegal. The committee's scientific background mentions the dispute.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

In dozens of countries the frequencies your phone uses were sold this way. Governments now borrow by auctioning bonds; in parts of Europe electricity prices are set daily in regional power auctions; the EU auctions carbon emission allowances; search engines auction ad slots against your keywords. When a house seller attaches an inspection report, that is the "disclosure raises the price" result at work.

What may change next

The incentive auction showed that a public resource already handed out can be bought back by market means and passed to someone who will use it better. Milgrom has pointed to environmental resources as another place where careful design can help. Keep expectations in check, though: on the day of the prize he said the biggest problems of the pandemic, vaccines and care for displaced people, were not ones auction theory directly solves. His Nobel lecture ends by saying simulation and computation will matter more and more, but theory is still needed to understand the problem.

What it does not mean

It does not mean "highest bidder wins, and higher is always better". US law made efficient and intensive use of spectrum the primary goal, with revenue secondary; chasing revenue alone could hand everything to one firm and create a monopoly. It does not mean the winner's curse proves people are irrational: in Wilson's theory rational bidders shade their bids, and the curse catches those who fail to, in experiments mostly inexperienced bidders. And there is no perfect auction for every setting. The committee writes that there is no silver bullet, and Binmore and Klemperer's review records weak competition and low prices in the Dutch, Italian and Swiss 3G sales.

A way of thinking to borrow

When you win, ask one more question: "What does my winning tell me about what the others knew?" A job offer, a tender, the hot flat everyone wanted: same structure. Treat the win itself as a piece of evidence, and revise your estimate.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Machine learning · the "best" model on the validation set: run a hundred hyperparameter settings, keep the top validation score, and it nearly always drops on fresh data. Same structure as the oil tract: take the maximum of many noisy estimates and the maximum is biased upward. Wilson's remedy is to discount before you believe; the ML version is a held-out test set you never touched.
  • Distributed systems · rounds, termination detection, liveness: SMRA is a round-synchronous protocol that collects all bids and then broadcasts results. "End only after a round with no new bid anywhere" is termination detection: you must confirm the whole system is quiet, not one corner of it. The activity rule is a liveness constraint that stops nodes from waiting forever for someone else to move first.
  • Hayek · prices as an information system: in his Nobel lecture Wilson says auctions fit Friedrich Hayek's view of markets as information systems. The linkage principle is a precise version of that idea: the more dispersed information a price absorbs, the sharper everyone's estimate. Bidders dropping out one by one in an English auction resemble weak learners being averaged into an ensemble.
  • Elsewhere on this site: moving spectrum from fading over-the-air TV to mobile data is a concrete case of the creative destruction in the 2025 economics prize. The committee notes that Paul Klemperer designed the product-mix auction in response to the 2007 Northern Rock bank run, and the Bank of England has kept using it; bank runs are the subject of the 2022 economics prize.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Vickrey showed in 1961 that with purely private values, English, Dutch and sealed-bid auctions give the seller the same expected revenue.
  2. With a common value such as oil reserves, every bidder's estimate is noisy, and the most optimistic estimate produces the highest bid.
  3. The winner's curse: winning a common-value auction often means you overestimated, because nobody else was as optimistic.
  4. Wilson showed in 1969 that rational bidders shade bids below their estimate, and shade more as uncertainty grows.
  5. Milgrom and Weber proved in 1982 that English auctions raise more on average than sealed bids, because bidding reveals information.
  6. The linkage principle: the more private information the price absorbs, the weaker the curse, so disclosing appraisals raises prices.
  7. Milgrom and Wilson's Simultaneous Multiple Round Auction used a closing rule against sniping and an activity rule against waiting.
  8. The FCC's first spectrum auction in July 1994 sold 10 licences in 47 rounds for 617 million dollars.
  9. The 2017 Incentive Auction bought spectrum back from about 2,400 TV stations, a hard graph-colouring problem, and resold it.
  10. Spectrum auctions aim first at efficient use rather than maximum revenue, and no single auction design fits every market.

// Think further

Is the company that offers a candidate the highest salary suffering the winner's curse?
It depends how much of the candidate's value is common. If ability matters about equally to every employer and interviews are noisy measurements, the top offer probably overestimated. If the person fits your team unusually well (a private value), paying more is sensible. Separating those two is exactly what Milgrom's mixed model is built for.
If disclosure raises prices, why do so many sellers still keep things hidden?
The theorem has conditions: symmetric, risk-neutral bidders, and disclosure that is credible and not cherry-picked. If buyers know you only reveal good news, silence itself becomes bad news. Real sellers also often sit in long relationships rather than one auction, where squeezing out a bit more today may not pay.
Auction designers know the rules and also know the consultants on every side. How should that be governed?
The 2017 dispute was not about lawbreaking but about one small expert community sitting on both sides of the table, a bit like a problem-setter also entering the contest. Firewalls, disclosure and rotation help without removing it. The harder question is whether the alternatives without those experts, administrative allocation or lotteries, would be worse.
