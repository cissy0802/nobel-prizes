Your birth month ran an experiment for economists

How a social science that cannot run experiments learned to borrow the accidental lotteries of real life to answer questions of cause and effect, and to say exactly whom each answer is about

David CardUC Berkeley · 1/2Joshua D. AngristMIT · 1/4Guido W. ImbensStanford University · 1/4
for his empirical contributions to labour economicsfor their methodological contributions to the analysis of causal relationships

The most honest form of a causal claim begins by saying whom it does not cover.

You have heard it a hundred times: people with more schooling earn more, kids from top schools do better, richer people live longer. In the data, all of it is true. And you sense the catch: people who are good at school may simply be good at earning, too.

The clean way to separate the two would be a lottery: randomly bar half the children from finishing high school and check their pay thirty years later. Nobody would do that.

American law did it by accident. Children start school by calendar year of birth, but may leave school once they reach a certain age. So a child born in January can legally walk out almost a year earlier than a classmate born in December. Birthdays are handed out at random. The lottery had already been run on a whole generation; nobody had read the results.

Everyone in the data walked only one road

Causal questions are hard for one plain reason: the same person cannot both finish high school and not finish it. You only see the road they took. The version of them on the other road, the counterfactual, is always missing.

Among American men born in the 1930s, as recorded in the 1980 census, each extra year of schooling goes with about 7 percent higher earnings. But that 7 percent mixes two things: what schooling does, and the fact that people who stay longer were different to begin with. The minimum wage is worse. When the economy sours, pay at the bottom stagnates, and calls for a higher minimum grow. A correlation between minimum wages and unemployment may run the other way round.

By the early 1980s, economists had lost faith in their own data work. The econometrician Edward Leamer wrote in 1983:

Hardly anyone takes data analyses seriously. Or perhaps more accurately, hardly anyone takes anyone else's data analyses seriously.— Edward Leamer · 1983 · quoted in Imbens's Nobel lecture

In 1986 Robert LaLonde took a job-training programme that had been evaluated by a randomised experiment and re-estimated its effect with standard econometric methods. The answers did not match.

Burger joints on either side of a state line

On 1 April 1992, New Jersey raised its minimum wage from $4.25 to $5.05 an hour. Neighbouring Pennsylvania did not. Card and his colleague Alan Krueger surveyed 410 fast-food restaurants on both sides, before and after. Same weather, same business cycle, much the same customers. The one difference was the law.

The method is called difference-in-differences. Do not ask whether jobs in New Jersey went up or down; ask how New Jersey's change compares with Pennsylvania's. Pennsylvania stands in for "New Jersey, had it done nothing".

The textbook predicted layoffs in New Jersey. Instead, relative to Pennsylvania, employment at New Jersey's restaurants rose by about 13 percent, and stores that already paid above the new minimum moved just like Pennsylvania's.

Earlier, Card had found another accidental experiment. In late April 1980, Fidel Castro announced that Cubans who wished to leave could go from the port of Mariel. About 125,000 left between May and September; half settled in Miami, and within months the local labour force grew by 7 percent, mostly with low-skilled workers. Comparing Miami with four other cities, Card found no sign that wages fell or unemployment rose for less-skilled locals.

That result was later boiled down to "immigration doesn't affect wages". On the morning of the prize, Card corrected it himself:

a cautionary tale is that there could have been small effects because them the ability to precisely measure the effects when it only affects one city is somewhat limited.— David Card · nobelprize.org telephone interview · 11 October 2021

Birthdays, lotteries, and the people they push

Back to birthdays. In 1991 Angrist and Krueger showed that, among men born in the 1930s, those born in the first quarter of the year had on average 0.15 fewer years of schooling and 1.4 percent lower earnings than those born in the fourth. Divide the earnings gap by the schooling gap and an extra year of school is worth about 9 percent.

That was higher than the raw 7 percent, which was odd. If able people both study more and earn more, the raw correlation should overstate the return; stripping out the bias should shrink it. Angrist's 1990 study of the Vietnam-era draft lottery ran into a similar puzzle. In televised draws each birth date got a number from 1 to 365, and men with low numbers could be drafted. But many who served were volunteers who would have gone whatever their number.

Angrist and Imbens were then young assistant professors at Harvard. Their 1994 paper in Econometrica opens bluntly: the existence of valid instruments is not sufficient to identify any meaningful average treatment effect. An instrument here is a random nudge, such as a birthday or a lottery number, that affects the outcome only through the choice it nudges.

Their answer: when effects differ from person to person, a natural experiment can only reveal the average effect for the people it actually moved, the local average treatment effect (LATE). For those who would finish school regardless, or leave regardless, the birthday changes nothing, so there is no comparison group and the data are silent. The conditions are spelled out: the nudge must be as good as random, must actually move people, must work through only one channel, and no one may be pushed the opposite way.

So the 9 percent acquires a precise meaning: it is the return to an extra year for people who would leave school at the first legal chance. Card's reading is that such people tend to be short of money, so borrowing to study costs them more, and an extra year pays them more. Who exactly sits in that group cannot be seen, but its size can be computed.

The committee put the three in one prize because the halves complete each other. Card showed that natural experiments can settle big questions; Angrist and Imbens clarified what they can and cannot settle. Together they underpin what economists now call the design-based approach: first decide what experiment you would run if you could, then go and find it in the world.

Experiments nobody designed, everywhere

Once the method was clear, people saw natural experiments everywhere. Newborns under 1,500 grams are classed as low birth weight and get extra care, so comparing babies just either side of the line shows whether that care lowers mortality. Sweden issued savings bonds that paid part of their interest by lottery, turning winners into a study of what extra wealth does to people.

The minimum-wage argument ran for thirty years. A 2019 study of 138 state-level increases in the US found the number of low-wage jobs essentially unchanged over five years. That November, the UK Treasury published a review of the international evidence it had commissioned from Arindrajit Dube: minimum wages in a range of countries had had a negligible or zero effect on jobs. The work also revived economists' interest in employers' power to hold wages down, known as monopsony.

Medicine is borrowing the framework too. In clinical trials some patients never take the drug they are assigned, and the standard analysis compares groups only by assignment. A review cited by the committee looked at 98 randomised trials published in 2008 in the New England Journal of Medicine and JAMA; half also ran analyses based only on who actually took the treatment, and those analyses are biased in their own right. LATE offers a cleaner alternative.

◐ Off the podium

The missing name is Alan Krueger. He co-wrote the New Jersey study, the quarter-of-birth paper and the school-quality papers. He died in 2019, and the prize is never awarded posthumously. In the acknowledgements of his Nobel lecture Angrist wrote: "We three share a debt to our absent friend, Alan Krueger."

The fights continue. In 2015 George Borjas re-analysed the Mariel data and reported that wages of Miami men without a high-school diploma fell by 10 to 30 percent. In 2017 Michael Clemens and Jennifer Hunt traced the discrepancy to an abrupt change in the make-up of small survey subsamples, unrelated to the boatlift. The New Jersey survey data were challenged as well; in 2000 Card and Krueger redid the analysis with administrative data and again found a positive estimate, though not a statistically significant one.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

How minimum wages are set. On 4 November 2019 the UK government published the Dube review, and the Chancellor pledged to move the National Living Wage towards two-thirds of median earnings by 2024, economic conditions permitting, projected at £10.50 an hour. The review also urged evaluating and recalibrating along the way. Card himself joked that after his book with Krueger the US federal minimum was frozen for a long time: "And so we always assumed that our work had completely stopped all progress for the minimum wage!"

The "elite school" effect. Angrist compared applicants just above and just below the admission cut-offs of exam schools in Boston, New York and Chicago. The causal effect of attending on achievement and college-going came out at zero, maybe negative. Exam-school students do well mostly because the schools admit students who were going to do well. The first paper's title says it: "The Elite Illusion".

What may change next

The pandemic is the next giant natural experiment; Card expects people to still be untangling it twenty or thirty years from now. The method has its own failure modes, though: a 2020 study found more signs of p-hacking in difference-in-differences and instrumental-variable papers than in randomised trials. Most economics journals now require authors to post data and code, and pre-analysis plans are used to limit after-the-fact choices.

What it does not mean

Not that minimum wages can rise without limit. The committee says outright that one cannot conclude increases never cost jobs; the US federal minimum is low by international standards, so the results may not transfer, and tradable sectors that face outside competition show larger effects. Not that immigration affects no one. The later consensus is that earlier immigrants bear the brunt. And not that an extra year of school adds 9 percent for everyone: that number belongs to the people their birthday moved.

A way of thinking to borrow

When you see "people who did X are more Y", ask two things: who chose X, and did anything push some people into X by accident, without their choosing? If you find that push, ask a third: which group of people does the answer belong to?

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Engineering · the "invitation" in an A/B test: show a new-feature prompt to a random half of users, and only some click. Comparing clickers with non-clickers mixes in "early adopters are more active anyway"; comparing the two halves dilutes the effect with people who never clicked. Treat the random invitation as an instrument and you get the effect for exactly the users the invitation moved. When software can force everyone onto the feature you don't need this; when it can't, you do.
  • AI · correlational models and distribution shift: a learned model is good at "people who did X are more Y" and has no idea whether that holds for a different population. LATE writes the scope onto the result, like a model card that says "evaluated on this slice only". Carrying it to another population is a distribution-shift argument you have to make separately.
  • Philosophy · two lives, one lived: the statisticians Jerzy Neyman (1923) and Donald Rubin (1974) defined a causal effect as the difference between two outcomes for the same person, one of which can never be observed. Causal inference doesn't observe the road not taken; it designs a group of people who can testify for it.
  • Elsewhere on this site: Nogales, the town split by a fence in the 2024 economics prize, uses the same design as the New Jersey–Pennsylvania line: a border manufactures the other road. The vaccines in the 2023 medicine prize were proven in genuine randomised trials; this prize is about what to do when you can't draw lots.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Causal questions are hard because the counterfactual is always missing: one person cannot both finish and not finish school.
  2. By the early 1980s economists distrusted observational data analysis; Leamer and LaLonde exposed the weakness of standard methods.
  3. Card and Krueger compared fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania around New Jersey's 1992 minimum-wage rise and found no fall in employment.
  4. Difference-in-differences uses a control group's change as the road not taken, then asks how much more the treated group changed.
  5. Card found that the 1980 Mariel boatlift raised Miami's labour force by 7 percent without visibly hurting less-skilled locals' wages or jobs.
  6. Angrist and Krueger used birth quarter and school-leaving age rules to estimate a return of about 9 percent per extra year of school.
  7. Angrist and Imbens showed in 1994 that with varying effects a natural experiment reveals only the average effect for the people it moves.
  8. The local average treatment effect needs a nudge that is near-random, actually moves people, has one channel, and pushes no one the other way.
  9. Minimum-wage findings do not mean any increase is harmless, and immigration findings do not mean nobody is affected.
  10. A random invitation in an A/B test is an instrument, and it measures the effect on the users the invitation moved to click.

// Think further

Is an answer that holds only for "the people who were moved" useful for policy?
It depends on whom the policy will move. If the policy aims to keep would-be dropouts in school one more year, the people moved by their birthday are exactly its target, and LATE hits the bullseye. If the policy is to send everyone to graduate school, the number may not travel. Deaton and Heckman criticised the approach on precisely this point; Imbens's 2009 reply was titled "Better LATE Than Nothing".
Card has said that crazy political regimes have many disadvantages but create very good conditions for causal analysis. Does that sit uneasily with you?
Natural experiments often come from other people's misfortune: drafts, refugee waves, pandemics. Researchers did not cause them but do benefit. One view: the price has already been paid, and not reading the results would be the real waste. The caution is that the more dramatic the event, the more things it changes at once, and the harder it is to defend "only one difference".
Is there a "state line" in your own work?
A feature launched in one city first, a team that switched process early, a release pushed only to Android. Each could be a natural experiment, provided you can say why the two sides are alike in everything except that one difference. Whatever you cannot explain is the risk carried by your conclusion.
