LITERATURE · 2022

She wrote an autobiography without a single “I”

A grocer’s daughter from Normandy who chose a deliberately flat language to turn her father, her mother, her abortion and her shame into the memory of a generation

Annie ErnauxFrance · 1/1
for the courage and clinical acuity with which she uncovers the roots, estrangements and collective restraints of personal memory
2026-11-05 · BigCat

The thing she refused to write was a beautiful sentence. That, she said, was what she taught her students.

Maybe you know the feeling. You walk into a restaurant a notch too smart for you, the waiters drift past your table, and suddenly your clothes, your accent, the way you read the menu all announce that you do not belong.

Tours, France: a brightly lit dining room lined with mirrors and a sophisticated clientele. A girl in spectacles sits with her father at the far end of a long table set for their tour group, waiting a long time between courses. At a small table nearby, a suntanned girl of fourteen or fifteen in a low-cut dress laughs freely with her father, dipping into a glass of some thick milky stuff. Years later she will learn it was yogurt, something “people like us” had never heard of. She catches herself in the mirror: pale, sad-looking, silent beside a father staring into the distance.

For weeks afterwards the father rails against the place and its mashed potatoes, “pig slop”. He never says what he means, but the daughter understands: he was treated with contempt, and this is how he says so. It was probably then, she would write, that she began decoding his speech. The girl was Annie Ernaux, and the scene is from Shame (La honte, 1996).

The café-grocery, and a vow to avenge her people

Ernaux was born in 1940 in Lillebonne, a small working-class town in Normandy, and grew up in Yvetot, where her parents, both former factory workers, ran a combined café and grocery. Her childhood swung between two opposite worlds: the shop on the edge of town and the private Catholic school in the centre that her parents paid for at great sacrifice. Before she was born they had lost a six-year-old daughter, a sister nobody spoke about.

She was a gifted pupil and went on to study literature at Rouen, among the sons and daughters of the local bourgeoisie. At twenty-two she wrote a sentence in her diary that she read aloud, sixty years later, in her Nobel lecture:

“I will write to avenge my people, j’écrirai pour venger ma race.”— Ernaux · Nobel lecture · 7 December 2022

She was proud and naive, she said, believing that if the last in a line of landless labourers, factory workers and shopkeepers became a writer, that would be enough to redress the injustice of birth. Then life intervened. At twenty-three, in a France where abortion was a crime, she turned to a backstreet abortionist. She married, had two sons, taught school and carried the whole household, drifting further from writing every day. In June 1967 she went home to see her parents; the next morning her father had a heart attack and died three days later. Her first book, Cleaned Out (Les armoires vides), was written in secret on afternoons without classes and published by Gallimard in 1974. She was thirty-three.

Her language problem was not a choice between two national tongues but between two class dialects. Flaubert, Proust and Woolf, the writers she revered, were no help at all, she said, once she set out to write about where she came from. She had to break with “writing well” and with beautiful sentences, the very kind she taught her students to write.

The citation, word by word

“Courage”: Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, quotes her wish to write the kind of book that makes it impossible to talk about afterwards, impossible to withstand the gaze of others. Happening (L’événement, 2000) is that book, the account of her illegal abortion. Anyone involved at the time, the pregnant woman included, risked prison; while she waited for her period, her diary entries were a single word: RIEN, nothing. Parentheses keep breaking into the story, the voice of the woman writing it decades later.

“Clinical acuity”: from her fourth book on, the one about her father, she adopted a neutral, objective écriture plate, flat writing, with no metaphors and no signs of emotion. The violence, she said, would come from the facts, not from the prose. She calls language “a knife” for cutting through the veils of imagination.

“Roots”: A Man’s Place (La place, 1983), her breakthrough, is a scant hundred pages on her father and the whole milieu that formed him, with his plain phrases set into the text like engravings. Its epigraph is from Jean Genet: “to write is the last resort / having committed treason.”

“Estrangements”: having left her class through school, she calls herself “an immigrant of the interior”, and writing about her family always carries a whiff of betrayal.

“Collective restraints”: the Swedish citation says kollektiva ramar, literally “collective frames”. Personal memory is never wholly private; it is framed by the songs, adverts, laws and taboos of its time.

Inside the books: from “I” to “we”

A Man’s Place has a double vision: details only a daughter could know, seen across the distance of a class barrier. It still says “I”. In her lecture she explains what kind of “I” she wanted: a transparent one the reader can step into, an “I” that becomes transpersonal.

The Years (Les années, 2008) takes that road to its end. Covering 1941 to 2006, it never uses the first person singular. It speaks through the French impersonal on, rendered “we” in English, and calls the woman in the photographs “she”. There is no Proustian affective memory to carry her straight back to childhood, Olsson notes. What strings the decades together are photographs and public events. One British reviewer observes that moments such as the 1998 World Cup, the 1999 eclipse and the falling towers are ones “by which multitudes can index their own lives against each other’s.”

The book opens: “All the images will disappear.” A long run of fragments follows: a woman squatting to urinate behind a shack at the edge of the ruins in Yvetot after the war, Alida Valli’s tearful face on screen, a shoe turning on a shop pedestal with the same slogan scrolling round it. At the end of the run she writes:

“Like sexual desire, memory never stops. It pairs the dead with the living, real with imaginary beings, dreams with history.”— The Years, opening pages · tr. Alison L. Strayer · Fitzcarraldo excerpt

A review on the University of Exeter’s Translating Women blog notes how the book refuses to make 1968 as glorious as the recurring camera images, and how, in its words, “the struggle of women sank into oblivion.” Ernaux puts that forgotten struggle back into collective memory. Her Chinese translator, Wu Yuetian, says she began mulling the book in the mid-1980s and finished it only after retiring.

Why her, and why that year

She was the first Frenchwoman to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. When The Years came out in English, a British reviewer was still saying she was long overdue to be recognised in Britain as one of contemporary France’s most important writers; a year later the book was shortlisted for the 2019 Man Booker International Prize. On 6 October 2022 she learned of the Nobel around one o’clock, alone in her kitchen with the radio on. It was, she said, like being “in the desert and there is a call that is coming from the sky.”

She called the prize a collective victory. In her lecture she spoke of women rising up in Iran, and of men in Western intellectual circles who simply never cite books by women; the Academy’s recognition, she said, was a sign of hope for all female writers. Later, in an interview, she pointed to US states and Poland restricting abortion. The citation says nothing about politics, yet the forbidden experience she wrote about had come back into the news.

◐ Off the podium

After the award, her past signatures came under scrutiny, mainly in Germany: petitions calling for boycotts of Israel, support for the BDS (Boycott, Divestment and Sanctions) movement, a call to boycott the Eurovision Song Contest held in Israel. Swiss broadcaster SRF interviewed Jürgen Ritte, a literature professor at the Sorbonne. He found nothing antisemitic in her books, but called the apartheid-regime charge she had co-signed “crude and negligent nonsense”, adding that a good author need not be a wise citizen. Ernaux draws a line of her own in her lecture: she does not confuse the political action of literary writing with the positions she takes on events.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

What reading her can change

A new way to look at your own memories. Your most private recollections, the first time someone looked down on you, the first song you loved, come in a period frame. After The Years it is hard not to ask of a phrase in your head: which decade, which advert, which grown-up did that come from?

A name for people who crossed a class line. Zhao Wei, her editor at Shanghai People’s Publishing House, notes that her idea of being “an ethnologist of herself” echoes a phrase popular among Chinese readers, “taking oneself as method”. Anyone who left a small town through education may recognise the mix of pride and betrayal.

Written down, it is no longer carried alone. After Simple Passion, readers wrote to her about their own secret affairs; Happening is now on the French school syllabus. She believes a book can break the loneliness of experiences endured in silence.

Where to start

On the day of the prize she recommended The Years as the book that might bring everyone together (tr. Alison L. Strayer; Fitzcarraldo in the UK, Seven Stories in the US). It is dense with French pop-culture references. For something thinner, take Olsson’s pick, A Man’s Place, about a hundred pages. Committee member Ellen Mattson’s favourite is Happening. Anne Swärd, also on the committee, advises leaving I Remain in Darkness, about her mother’s dementia, until last.

What it does not mean

Writing “I” is not narcissism. Her “I” is an empty seat for the reader. Flat is not simple. Dropping metaphor was a choice, made so that culturally privileged readers could not look down on her father. Nor is she settling old scores. In A Girl’s Story she deliberately leaves out today’s word, rape, wanting only to describe exactly what happened and how it felt then.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · no global clock, so sync on shared events: in a distributed system no machine’s clock can be trusted; order is recovered from messages everyone has seen. The Years does the same. Each private life keeps its own time, while World Cups, eclipses and TV shows are broadcasts everyone received, and people align their lives against them. The difference: for humans, the syncing is itself the content of memory.
  • AI · which corpus your sentences came from: in Olsson’s reading, our lives are formed by the stories told, the songs sung, the trends of the moment. Every output of a language model traces back to its training data; Ernaux performs data provenance on herself, chasing each sentence in her head back to a class and a decade.
  • Buddhism · non-self: Olsson writes that Ernaux has great difficulty recognising herself in the person she once was; the “she” of The Years is a series of photographs and states produced by conditions. Buddhism likewise treats the self as a bundle of conditions with no fixed core. Buddhism wants to let it go; she wants to write it down and hand it on.
  • Economics · the gap opens with the first child: marriage, two children, a teaching post and all the housework took her further from writing every day, she says in the lecture; A Frozen Woman is about that double burden. It is what Claudia Goldin measured across two centuries of data: the gap does not open on the first day of work but in the year the first child arrives.
  • Literature · history landing on a body: Han Kang, laureate two years later, also writes how history lands on a body. Han Kang approaches trauma through poetic prose; Ernaux uses unemotional fact, letting the marks that law and taboo left on a twenty-three-year-old body speak for themselves.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Annie Ernaux, born in 1940, grew up in her parents’ café-grocery in Yvetot, and has spent her life writing the gaps of class, gender and language.
  2. At twenty-two Ernaux wrote in her diary “I will write to avenge my people”, and read it out sixty years later in her Nobel lecture.
  3. To write about a working-class origin, Flaubert and Proust were no help: she had to break with “writing well” and beautiful sentences.
  4. Flat writing uses no metaphors and no signs of emotion, so the violence comes from the facts rather than the prose.
  5. Happening recounts an illegal abortion in early-1960s France, when everyone involved, the pregnant woman included, risked prison.
  6. A Man’s Place, about a hundred pages on her father and his class, restores his dignity while being written in the shadow of betrayal.
  7. The Years covers 1941 to 2006 without the first person singular, fusing personal and collective memory.
  8. The citation’s “collective restraints” is “collective frames” in Swedish: private memory is always framed by its era’s songs, adverts and laws.
  9. Ernaux herself suggests starting with The Years; for a shorter entry point, try A Man’s Place.
  10. Private lives keep separate time, and public events like World Cups and eclipses work like messages everyone received in a distributed system.

// Think further

She tells young writers to write honestly rather than well. Are the two really at odds?
Beautiful sentences carry a class accent; they quietly decide what is worth writing and how to write it respectably. Yet flatness is itself a carefully chosen style. Perhaps the real difference is not how much rhetoric you use but whether you write to be admired or to get the thing said. In your own status reports and post-mortems, which is more common?
Is an autobiography without “I” still an autobiography?
In The Years she writes herself as “we” and “she”, and few readers doubt it is her life. Maybe the core of autobiography is not the pronoun but the promise that this is true. Turn it round: when you tell your own story as “back then, everyone…”, are you dodging, or saying something truer?
Where is the line when you write about your family?
Reading the book on her mother’s dementia, Anne Swärd kept asking herself, “Is this really okay?” Her answer: Ernaux always lands on the right side of the edge, because of love. Ernaux’s own mother, when she moved in, left her daughter’s diaries behind, presumably having read them and decided they should be destroyed. The same truth can free the one who writes and expose the one written about. How would you weigh it?
