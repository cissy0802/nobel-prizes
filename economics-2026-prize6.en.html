What moves you is not today's interest rate, but your guess about next year's

One economist turned "you can't predict spending" into evidence; the other used it to build central banks a simulator

Robert E. HallStanford University · 1/2Michael WoodfordColumbia University · 1/2
for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation
2026-10-16 · BigCat

Hall proved his theory right by showing that nobody could forecast next quarter's consumption.

Your employer tells you your job ends in three months. Your paycheck stays exactly the same until then, yet you probably start eating out less tonight and shelve the new phone. When the paychecks finally stop in month three, your spending barely moves. You already adjusted.

Everyone understands that reaction, yet the older macro models had no room for it: there, what you spend depends on what you earned this month. This year's prize goes to the two people who built "people look ahead" into macroeconomics in a form that can be tested, computed, and used to set policy.

A recipe that worked for decades, then broke in the oil crises

After the Great Depression, John Maynard Keynes left governments a recipe. Slumps happen because people buy too little, so cut rates, spend, and send out transfers to push demand back up; when the economy overheats, do the reverse. Under the recipe sat a few statistical relationships measured from data. One was the consumption function: hand a household an extra 100 dollars of disposable income and it spends about 67. Run that ratio through the economy and each dollar of government spending lifts output by roughly three. Another was the Phillips curve, drawn by A. W. Phillips in 1958: years of high unemployment had low inflation and vice versa, so it looked as if a little inflation could buy jobs.

It worked until the oil crises of the 1970s. The West got high unemployment and high inflation at once (stagflation). Stimulus by the recipe did not bring unemployment down; it let inflation run away, and a decade of low growth followed. Plenty of people concluded that stabilisation policy did more harm than good.

Robert E. Lucas, Jr. (laureate in 1995) named the problem in 1976, in what became the Lucas critique. Those statistical relationships are shadows cast by millions of decisions. Change the policy and the decisions change, and the shadows move with them. Steering new policy by old correlations is like navigating with a map that redraws itself whenever you move. His prescription was to build models up from how households and firms decide, with microfoundations.

Building one was another matter. In 1982 Kydland and Prescott (laureates in 2004) produced a microfounded model, but in it markets were perfect and prices adjusted instantly, so government had nothing useful to do. On one side was a broken recipe, on the other a kitchen with no need for a cook.

Hall flipped the question: if you can't predict it, I'm right

Milton Friedman and Franco Modigliani had argued that spending depends on what people expect to earn over their lives, not just this month's wage: the permanent income hypothesis. Nearly everyone accepted it in theory, but nobody could test it. Expectations are invisible, and a researcher could always invent expectations to fit whatever the data showed.

Robert E. Hall's insight was this: if people really look ahead, today's consumption already contains everything they know about the future. How consumption changes next quarter can only depend on news that arrives in between, and news, by definition, cannot be predicted, or it would not be news. So the theory implies that nothing visible today should help forecast the change in consumption.

That turned research around. Everyone else was hunting for indicators that predict consumption; Hall set out to test that none would. On quarterly postwar US data, past disposable income had essentially no predictive power, and only the previous quarter's change in stock prices had a little. The paper appeared in the Journal of Political Economy in 1978. Its abstract says:

No variable apart from current consumption should be of any value in predicting future consumption.— Robert E. Hall · Journal of Political Economy · 1978

The committee's scientific background calls it a "Eureka" moment for macroeconomics. Unpredictable stock prices are less surprising, because traders arbitrage away anything forecastable. No one arbitrages consumption. Millions of households living their own lives somehow added up to something that behaved like one forward-looking person.

The benchmark had a second use: once you know what "unpredictable" looks like, you can measure how far reality departs from it. In 1982 Hall and Frederic Mishkin took food spending by 2,309 families from Michigan's Panel Study of Income Dynamics, 1969–1975, and estimated that about 80 percent behaved as the theory says while about 20 percent lived hand-to-mouth: no savings, no access to credit, spending whatever came in. That split between planners and hand-to-mouth households is still part of the models central banks use.

Woodford built a simulator with three gears

Michael Woodford started out studying law before moving to economics. He did not start from scratch. He took the perfect-market Kydland–Prescott model and changed one crucial thing: prices are sticky. Firms have pricing power, but changing prices is costly, so they cannot do it every day. A standard way to write this comes from Guillermo Calvo (1983): each period, a firm gets to reset its price only with some probability, as if waiting for a randomly visiting "Calvo fairy."

Once prices stick, demand matters again. If demand falls and prices don't, goods go unsold and factories cut output. That was Keynes's intuition, but now it was derived from microeconomic behaviour. Through the 1990s Woodford and his co-author Julio Rotemberg (who died in 2017) built the parts paper by paper, and in 2003 Woodford put the whole framework into the book Interest and Prices. The simplest version has just three interlocking relationships:

The first is Hall's consumption theory with the interest rate added: when rates are low, saving pays less, so people spend more now. The second is about firms: when your rare chance to reset a price arrives, you must guess costs and rivals' prices for a good while ahead, so pricing is forward-looking too. The third describes how the central bank sets its rate in response to inflation and how hot the economy runs, usually called a Taylor rule.

Two features are distinctly Woodford's. First, there is no money stock in the model at all. Textbooks used to say central banks control how much money exists; Woodford argued that setting the interest rate directly is enough, following the "natural rate of interest" introduced by the Swedish economist Knut Wicksell in 1898. Second, the output gap: actual output minus the output the economy would produce if prices could adjust freely. Stabilisation policy's job is to push that gap back to zero.

That definition produces a counterintuitive result. When energy prices spike, output falls, but the output the economy "should" have falls even further. So the gap is actually positive and the economy is running hot, and the right move is to raise rates even as output slides. Answering the 1970s oil shocks with stimulus was exactly backwards.

From the textbook into every rate meeting

Because everyone looks ahead, what a central bank says matters as much as what it does. In his book Woodford showed that in this model aggregate demand depends on the whole expected path of future short rates, not just today's. In a 2005 Jackson Hole paper on central-bank communication he argued that sharing the bank's own assumptions about future rates makes policy more predictable and more effective.

The idea was put to use. US rates sat near zero from 2008 to 2015, with no room to cut. In August 2011 the Federal Reserve said rates would likely stay exceptionally low "at least through mid-2013." In December 2012 it switched to conditions: no hike at least while unemployment stayed above 6.5 percent, projected inflation stayed within half a point of target, and long-run expectations stayed anchored. That was the form Woodford had argued for in a Kansas City Fed paper earlier that year: state the criterion, not the date. This is forward guidance.

The models kept growing. Sweden's Riksbank now uses MAJA, which adds foreign trade and union wage-setting and has 29 kinds of shocks in its core version. The newest branch, HANK (heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian) models, brings in households of every kind. In doing so it returns to the split Hall and Mishkin measured: some households plan ahead, some live hand-to-mouth, and the two respond very differently to the same check.

◐ Off the podium

The call from Stockholm woke Woodford at 4:30 a.m. He said he had assumed the prize usually went to older people, and that he still had an exam to give that morning. He also gently disagreed with the committee: its announcement traced stabilisation policy to Keynes, while he would have credited the Swede Wicksell, since Keynes claimed never to have read Wicksell. "But we know who wrote first." This year marks the centenary of Wicksell's death.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Central banks now often explain their reasoning and sketch the likely path ahead when they announce a rate decision. Woodford singles out Sweden's Riksbank for doing this well. The habit of saying it clearly first and then delivering rests heavily on this work. In the committee's words, when your central bank sets its rate, one input almost certainly came from a New Keynesian model. Long-term borrowing costs reflect expectations of a long string of future short rates, which is why a central banker's sentence can move them before any rate changes.

The question of who should get relief money draws on the same line of research. Of the 2001 US tax rebates, households spent roughly 20–40 percent within three months; of the 2008 stimulus payments, 12–30 percent. A randomized experiment in France, published in 2025, found 23 percent spent within a month. The tighter a household's budget, the faster the money goes out.

What may change next

Models that tell different households and sectors apart (HANK, production-network versions) are already in use. Still open: how often firms actually change prices (the standard stickiness assumptions are admittedly ad hoc), what happens far from the linear approximation, and when inflation expectations come unanchored. The committee concedes that the current framework struggles to capture how black swans such as wars and pandemics change expectations.

What it does not mean

It does not mean economists can predict recessions. The framework is about responding to shocks, not preventing them. Nobody could have stopped the pandemic, and preventing 2008 was a job for financial regulation, not interest rates. Nor does it claim everyone is a perfect calculator. Hall himself measured a fifth of households living hand-to-mouth, and one meta-analysis, after correcting for publication bias, puts the average share of an extra dollar spent at about 11 cents. Criticism never went away either. Paul Romer's 2016 "The Trouble with Macroeconomics" accused this family of models of explaining fluctuations with "imaginary causal forces," shocks that are really just names for the unexplained part.

A way of thinking to borrow

When the variable that matters is invisible, look for the unpredictability it must leave behind. If someone really uses all their information, nothing old should forecast their next move. Whatever you can forecast is exactly what they missed.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Control theory · the Kalman filter innovation test: a well-tuned filter's prediction errors should be white noise; structure left in them means the model is missing information. Hall's test has the same shape: the change in consumption is the household filter's innovation, and if past income predicts it, households are not using everything they know. The difference is that an engineer can retune the filter, while the economist can only read the residuals to infer constraints such as being unable to borrow.
  • Machine learning · distribution shift and Goodhart's law: in today's language, the Lucas critique says a model trained on history changes the behaviour of the people generating the data once deployed, and the training distribution expires. Treat the Phillips curve as a lever, as policymakers did in the 1970s, and it stops being the same curve. Microfoundations are the equivalent of modelling the users' decision process instead of fitting input–output correlations, so the model survives a policy change.
  • Physics · thermodynamics vs statistical mechanics: the Keynesian consumption function is like an equation of state fitted to bulk measurements; microfoundations are like deriving pressure from molecular motion. The analogy breaks at one point: molecules do not read central-bank statements, and households do.
  • Philosophy · Ulysses and the Sirens: Kydland and Prescott showed in 1977 that the best plan today usually gives you a reason to deviate tomorrow. Ulysses had himself tied to the mast to guard against his future self. Woodford's case for a public rule stated as conditions rather than dates works the same way: tie tomorrow's hands so today's promise is believed.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Older macro models assumed people spend according to this month's income, and the stagflation of the 1970s broke that Keynesian recipe.
  2. The Lucas critique: statistical relationships are shadows of millions of decisions, so when policy changes, behavior and the relationships change with it.
  3. Robert Hall's 1978 implication: if people look ahead, nothing visible today should predict how their consumption changes.
  4. Hall and Mishkin estimated that about 80% of households look ahead as the theory says, while about 20% live hand to mouth.
  5. Michael Woodford added sticky prices to the perfect-market model, which made demand matter again and gave Keynes's intuition a micro derivation.
  6. The simplest New Keynesian model has just three relationships: household spending, firms' price setting and the central bank's interest-rate rule.
  7. The output gap is actual output minus output with fully flexible prices; after an energy price spike, raising rates may be right even as output falls.
  8. Aggregate demand depends on expectations of a long string of future short-term rates, so what a central bank says matters as much as what it does.
  9. In 2012 the Fed gave forward guidance in the form Woodford advocated: conditions for raising rates rather than a specific date.
  10. Hall's test has the same structure as the innovation test for a Kalman filter: if prediction errors still show a pattern, information went unused.

// Think further

When is "we can't predict it" evidence for a theory, and when is it just ignorance?
Hall's test carries weight because the theory made a sharp prediction in advance that could have failed: no old information should have predictive power. A theory that merely said "consumption is complicated" would prove nothing. The difference is whether the test could have failed. Compare the efficient-markets hypothesis: both treat unpredictability as evidence, but stock markets have arbitrageurs erasing patterns and consumption has none.
If a central bank's promises gain power with credibility, should it spend that credibility all at once?
In the model, promises about the distant future are implausibly powerful; this is the forward guidance puzzle. Stanley Fischer's view in 2016 was that any central bank's ability to commit shrinks with the horizon. Think of credibility as a resource that grows with each promise kept and is marked down with each one broken.
If a fifth of households live hand-to-mouth, should relief be universal or targeted?
Hand-to-mouth households spend almost immediately, so money given to them stimulates most and is often most needed. But targeting has administrative costs and misses people. HANK models turn this into a question you can compute, though the answer depends on how many kinds of households you put in.
