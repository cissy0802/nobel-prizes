Progress always used to stall. Two centuries ago, it stopped stalling.

An economic historian explains why growth stopped burning out after the Industrial Revolution; two theorists turned "the new kills the old" into a growth engine you can actually compute

The 1992 paper has an awkward conclusion: left to itself, a market may innovate too little, or too much.

Open the junk drawer at home. Barrel plugs, flat plugs, Micro-USB cables, an old MP3 player, a memory card nobody can read any more. Each was once some company's pride. Now each is worth nothing.

That drawer is a very recent invention. Between 1300 and 1700, income per person in England rose and fell but showed almost no trend at all, even though the printing press, windmills and Newton's mechanics all arrived in that window. A great-grandchild lived much as the great-grandparent had. After the early nineteenth century, income per person in Britain and Sweden grew about one and a half percent a year, and kept doing it for two hundred years. Close to two percent a year means income doubles over a single working life.

Good ideas turned up in every age. So why did progress always stall before, and why did it stop stalling?

Never short of ideas, always short of a sequel

History has plenty of bright spells. The Middle Ages brought the heavy plough, windmills and three-field rotation. Renaissance Italy, Sweden in its seventeenth-century Great Power Era and the Dutch Golden Age (roughly 1588 to 1672) each had a real growth spurt, and each one fizzled out. China led Europe in technology until the fourteenth century; after 1430, a decision of the imperial court ended its voyages of exploration altogether. Leonardo da Vinci drew thousands of machines, and most were never built in his lifetime.

By the late twentieth century, some historians doubted even the Industrial Revolution. New statistics showed British growth in the late 1700s was feeble by modern standards, and one pair of scholars declared the revolution "dethroned".

Joel Mokyr, born in Leiden in 1946, asked a different question: not which country had coal or high wages, but why technology had never before turned into lasting growth. He split inventions in two. Macro inventions, like the printing press or the weight-driven clock, are sudden leaps. Micro inventions are the small, steady tweaks that live close to everyday production. Before industrialisation the two never connected: a big leap appeared now and then with no stream of tweaks behind it, and tweaking on its own soon hit diminishing returns. Nobody knew why things worked, so money flowed into perpetual-motion machines and artificial gold as readily as into anything else.

a world of engineering without mechanics, iron-making without metallurgy, farming without soil science, mining without geology…— Mokyr, 2005, quoted in the committee's Scientific Background

Mokyr: progress compounds once someone knows why

In The Gifts of Athena (2002), Mokyr split "useful knowledge" into two kinds. Prescriptive knowledge is the know-how: recipes, drawings, craft, telling you what to do. Propositional knowledge is the know-why: regularities of nature that explain why it works. For most of history the two lived apart. Philosophers reasoned in the study, artisans tinkered in the workshop, and neither fed the other.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries that changed. Precise measurement, controlled experiments and reproducible results took hold; learned societies, journals and the long-distance Republic of Letters made knowledge cheaper and more open. Mokyr called it the Industrial Enlightenment. Steam engines improved on the back of new ideas about atmospheric pressure and vacuums; once people understood that oxygen draws carbon out of molten pig iron, steelmaking had a direction. With the loop closed, each success paved the way for the next, and growth no longer burned out.

The failures make the point sharper. In the 1840s, Ignaz Semmelweis and Oliver Wendell Holmes both argued that doctors were carrying disease to their patients. Neither could say why, and their colleagues took offence. Holmes dropped the idea; Semmelweis left Vienna in disgrace. Only in the 1860s, after Pasteur and Lister, was it picked up again. A remedy without a reason can be buried for decades.

Science alone was not enough, though. France is often called the epicentre of the Enlightenment, yet Britain took off first. Mokyr and a colleague assembled records on 759 people born before 1830 who tweaked and implemented new technology in early British industry. More than half belonged to, or published in, scientific or technical societies; about two-thirds had served apprenticeships. A separate study of 41 English counties found that industry took root where wages were low but mechanical skills were plentiful; literacy, banks and nearness to coal explained little.

The third foundation was a society willing to give way. In Danzig in 1579, the inventor of a ribbon loom was reportedly drowned in secret on the city council's orders. Between 1811 and 1816 the Luddites smashed machines across England. In Mokyr's account, the British Parliament gave interest groups a table to bargain at, so the old guard could not simply veto the new.

Aghion and Howitt: a model where the new eats the old

MIT, autumn 1987. Philippe Aghion, fresh from a Harvard PhD, was a first-year assistant professor and a microeconomist; Peter Howitt, a macroeconomist, was visiting for the year from the University of Western Ontario. Aghion had long been drawn to Joseph Schumpeter's creative destruction, described in 1942: a new product arrives and the old one, along with the firm that made it, is swept away. Howitt had ideas about how to model it. Their working paper came out in January 1990 and the article in Econometrica in 1992.

The leading growth models of the day treated a new idea as one more variety added to the shelf, sitting peacefully beside the old ones. Aghion and Howitt used a different picture: every product is a ladder. Build a better rung and you reach the top and earn monopoly profits; whoever stood there before is kicked off. A patent protects you from being copied, not from being surpassed. The loser still holds the patent; it is simply worthless.

The ladder yields some counter-intuitive results. First, more research expected tomorrow means less research today: if everyone expects you to be overtaken soon, your invention will not earn for long. Second, in the simplest version, the climbers are newcomers. An incumbent that improves its own product only cannibalises its own profits; lobbying to keep entrants out pays better. Third, a free market can grow too slowly or too fast. Innovators count only the profits they collect while on top, ignoring that later inventors will build on their work, so they invest too little. Yet a newcomer who reaches the top takes the incumbent's entire profit even if its product is only a shade better, so the private reward can exceed the gain to society, and they may invest too much. In the paper's own words, the growth rate "may be more or less than optimal".

Under the calm surface of aggregate growth, the economy churns. In the US, more than ten percent of firms close each year, and about as many open. This was the first macroeconomic model of creative destruction in general equilibrium, the first in which the micro churn and the macro calm add up in a single set of books. Later extensions found an inverted U between competition and innovation: with too little competition the leader sits and collects rent; with too much, nobody can recoup the cost of research.

One explains how the engine caught; the other, why it keeps running

The two halves of the prize could hardly use more different tools: Mokyr reads letters, technical manuals and biographies; Aghion and Howitt write equations. The committee put them together because they answer two halves of one question: how growth started, and how it keeps going. The machine-breaking and machine-banning that Mokyr documents is the historical face of the incumbent lobbying in the model. The committee's press release states the shared lesson: creative destruction creates conflicts that must be managed constructively, or innovation will be blocked by established firms and interest groups.

The committee notes that productivity growth in rich countries has slowed since the 2000s, while firm entry and exit have both declined. Aghion argues that superstar firms such as Microsoft, Amazon and Facebook lifted US growth between 1995 and 2005, then became so dominant after 2005 that they discouraged new firms from entering.

None of this is settled. The economic historian Robert Allen offers a rival account in which scarce labour and high British wages pushed firms toward labour-saving machines, and the committee concedes that history is rarely monocausal. Critics also argue that Mokyr puts too much weight on a few elite thinkers and that a "culture of growth" is hard to turn into a testable variable, while the Aghion–Howitt model squeezes innovation into a single dimension and says little about who wins and who loses.

◐ Off the podium

When the call came, Mokyr said he had a list of likely winners in his head, "and I wasn't on it", adding: "I'm an economic historian. We don't win Nobel Prizes." Howitt, retired in North Carolina, heard the news first from journalists, because Stockholm could not reach him; on another line, Aghion could be heard urging him to pick up the phone. Howitt predicted a future with "less golf and more economics" than he had planned.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

The language of policy. The committee writes that the link between competition policy and innovation policy is now "deeply engrained" in antitrust thinking. In his prize-day call, Aghion said the EU's Draghi report and the World Bank's World Development Report on the middle-income trap draw directly on "our approach". On 13 March 2024 he and Anne Bouverot handed President Macron the report of France's AI commission, with 25 recommendations, including a 10-billion-euro "France & AI" fund.

How unemployment insurance is designed. Denmark and the Netherlands introduced "flexicurity" in the 1990s: firms can lay people off easily, and those laid off get generous benefits and retraining. Research by Aghion and colleagues found that, holding unemployment constant, US metro areas with faster job turnover report higher well-being, and that generous benefits soften the sting of job loss. Protect people, not jobs.

What may change next

The committee suggests AI could speed up Mokyr's loop, growing the know-why faster and making it cheaper for practitioners to look up. It also warns that this wave will create many "losers", at least in the short run. Some economists argue that history's greatest inventions are already behind us. The theory makes no forecast; it tells you what to watch: can new firms still get in, can displaced workers move to better jobs, does science stay open?

What it does not mean

Not that growth is a law of nature. Two centuries is a sliver of human history, and stagnation was the norm. Not that markets should simply be left alone. The model shows markets can invest the wrong amount and incumbents will block the road. Not that more growth is always better. The committee spells it out: sustained growth is not the same as sustainable growth, and climate, pollution and antibiotic resistance need policy. At the banquet, Mokyr said of the advocates of "de-growth": "They have a point."

A way of thinking to borrow

Run Mokyr's test on your own skills: do you hold a recipe, or do you know why it works? A recipe breaks when the setting changes; knowing why lets you keep improving it yourself.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • AI · scaling recipes: much recent progress in large models comes from training recipes found by trial and error, with theory explaining them only afterwards. That is Mokyr's know-how running ahead of know-why. His framework makes a testable bet: while interpretability and theory lag, progress is prone to plateaus; once the two feed each other, it can compound the way the Industrial Enlightenment did.
  • Statistical physics · annealing temperature: the inverted U between competition and innovation is the same trade-off as searching an energy landscape in the 2024 Physics prize. Too cold, and the system freezes in one valley, like a monopolist collecting rent. Too hot, and no structure survives, like competition so fierce nobody recovers their research costs. The sweet spot is in between. The difference: annealing temperature is set from outside, while incumbents can lobby to turn the market's temperature down.
  • Buddhism · impermanence and continuity: a river looks permanent, yet the water is never the same. Creative destruction brings this into economics: prosperity is not a state you can hold but a process of constant replacement, and freezing a product or a job in place is asking the river to stop. Where the analogy parts ways: Buddhism teaches letting go of attachment, while economics has to design institutions for the people being replaced.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. English income per person showed almost no trend from 1300 to 1700; sustained growth began only in the nineteenth century and has lasted two hundred years.
  2. Before industrialisation, great inventions appeared now and then but never triggered a stream of improvements, so every boom fizzled.
  3. Mokyr splits useful knowledge into prescriptive know-how and propositional know-why.
  4. When know-how and know-why feed each other, progress compounds; Mokyr argues the Industrial Enlightenment first closed that loop.
  5. Britain took off first thanks to a deep pool of skilled mechanics and a Parliament where the old guard could not simply veto the new.
  6. In 1992 Aghion and Howitt built the first general-equilibrium macroeconomic model of Schumpeter's creative destruction.
  7. On the quality ladder, a patent stops copying but not a better product, and a surpassed patent becomes worthless.
  8. A free market can do too little or too much research, because knowledge spillovers and business stealing pull in opposite directions.
  9. Flexicurity, protecting people rather than jobs, is one way to make creative destruction less painful.
  10. Competition and innovation form an inverted U, the same trade-off as an annealing temperature too cold to move or too hot to hold structure.

// Think further

Is deep learning today closer to before or after the Industrial Revolution?
By Mokyr's yardstick, it depends on whether there is a loop between the two kinds of knowledge. Training recipes come largely from trial and error, which looks pre-industrial. But experimental results are driving theory about representations and optimisation, and theory sometimes feeds back into recipes. Ask which advances were predicted by theory and which were only explained afterwards; the trend in that ratio is itself a signal.
If business stealing can mean too much research, where might that be happening?
The model's test: a new product only slightly better than the old one, yet able to capture all of its profit. Think of markets where marketing drives the race, features barely differ and the fight is over share. The committee's reading is that research is too low overall but may be too high in some industries, which is why one-size-fits-all R&D subsidies are a poor fit.
If AI displaces work at scale, is "protect people, not jobs" enough?
As Aghion describes it, flexicurity gives a laid-off worker about two years of generous support and retraining, on the assumption that a new job can be found in that time. If displacement comes too fast or across too many skills, retraining may not keep pace. Here Aghion's other thread matters: displaced people need somewhere to go, which means high social mobility and room for new firms to enter.
