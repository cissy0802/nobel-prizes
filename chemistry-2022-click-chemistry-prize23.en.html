The best reactions don't need to be clever

One chemist argued for giving up on copying nature and snapping molecules together with a handful of reactions that almost never fail; another carried that snap into living cells, tagging the sugars on their surface without disturbing anything else

Carolyn R. BertozziUSA · Stanford University · 1/3Morten MeldalDenmark · University of Copenhagen · 1/3K. Barry SharplessUSA · Scripps Research · 1/3
for the development of click chemistry and bioorthogonal chemistry
2026-11-04 · BigCat

The prize did not reward building ever more complex molecules. It rewarded deliberately giving up "we can make anything" in exchange for "whatever we make, it almost never goes wrong."

You go to plug in a USB stick. Wrong way up. You flip it. Still wrong. Two parts that clearly belong together, two ways to join them, and half the attempts fail.

One chemical reaction had been stuck on exactly that for over a century. Take a molecule carrying an "azide" group and another carrying an "alkyne". Brought together, they fuse into a sturdy five-membered ring, but in two orientations, often roughly half and half, and only after long heating. Chemists either put up with separating the mess or avoided the reaction altogether.

Around 2001, a lab in Copenhagen and a lab in California each found that a pinch of copper leaves only one way in: the parts click together in hours, at room temperature, even in water. Three years later a third chemist found a way to make them click without copper, and on living cells.

Two centuries of tracing over nature

Plants, microbes and animals make exquisite molecules, and chemistry's traditional ambition has been to rebuild them in a flask; many drugs began that way. The catch is that complex molecules are assembled step by step, every step leaves by-products that must be cleaned away, and over a long route the material dwindles until little is left.

In 2001 K. Barry Sharpless was about to receive his first Nobel Prize in Chemistry for other work. That same year he, Hartmuth Kolb and M.G. Finn published a contrarian paper: stop obsessing over copies of natural molecules. His example was the antibiotic meropenem, where just finding a way to make it at scale took six years of development.

The worst trouble, he argued, comes from forcing carbon atoms on two different molecules to bond: they have little drive to do so, and activating them invites side reactions. So go around it. Start from small pieces that already have their carbon frame, bridge them with nitrogen or oxygen, and pick reactions that run like a rock rolling downhill, with only one path to the bottom.

He set a strict entry test: broad scope, very high yield, works in water and in air, gives essentially one product, and any by-products are harmless and easy to remove without elaborate purification. He called chemistry that passes it click chemistry. He was also clear about the price: what you build this way will not be an exact copy of a natural molecule, but you can find something that does the same job.

The reaction that went the wrong way

Morten Meldal was at the Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen, searching for drug candidates by building molecular libraries of hundreds of thousands of compounds and screening them. One day his group ran a routine reaction in which an alkyne was supposed to bond to a molecule called an acyl halide, with copper ions as the usual catalyst. The analysis looked wrong. The alkyne had grabbed the other end of the molecule, an azide, and formed a five-membered ring called a triazole. The acyl halide, the intended partner, sat there more or less untouched.

Triazoles are stable and turn up in drugs, dyes and agrochemicals. The azide-alkyne reaction that makes them had been mapped out around 1960 by the German chemist Rolf Huisgen, but it was slow, needed heat, and gave two orientations of product, sometimes close to one-to-one. Meldal saw that copper made it take essentially a single path.

He first presented it at a symposium in San Diego in June 2001 and published in 2002: on the solid supports used to make peptides, all sorts of azides snapped onto alkynes, with conversion and purity above 95% in most cases. The same year Sharpless, with Valery Fokin and colleagues, independently published the same copper-catalysed reaction, showed it was reliable in water, and called it an "ideal" click reaction. According to the committee's scientific background, copper speeds the reaction up by as much as ten million times. From then on, to join two things you put an azide on one, an alkyne on the other, and add copper.

sometimes you just by serendipity have an orange falling into your turban and you have a very nice idea— Morten Meldal · telephone interview on the day of the announcement · 2022

Speaking to one partner in a crowded room

The second thread starts in the 1990s. Genes and proteins were being mapped at a furious pace, but one class of molecules on the cell surface was largely ignored: glycans, complex chains of sugars hanging off proteins and cells. They matter when viruses infect cells and when the immune system switches on, yet the new molecular-biology tools could not touch them.

In the early 1990s Carolyn Bertozzi studied a glycan that draws immune cells to lymph nodes, and it took four years just to work out how it functioned. At a seminar she heard a German scientist describe coaxing cells into taking up an unnatural version of sialic acid, one of the sugars glycans are built from; the cells built it into their own chains anyway. Her idea: feed cells a sugar that carries a chemical handle, let the cell's own machinery install the handle across its glycans, then attach a fluorescent molecule to it and watch where the glycans are.

The hard part was the handle. It and its partner had to recognise each other instantly and ignore everything else in the cell, like two people in a crowded station speaking a dialect nobody else knows. She gave this requirement a name: bioorthogonal, meaning a reaction that runs inside living systems without interfering with, or being disturbed by, the biology around it. In 1997 she showed the idea worked. In 2000 she found the ideal handle, the azide, and ingeniously reworked an old reaction from 1919, the Staudinger reaction, to hook a fluorescent tag onto azides on cell surfaces.

By then the copper-catalysed click reaction was the talk of chemistry, and Bertozzi knew azides would snap onto alkynes given copper. But copper is toxic to living cells. Back in the literature she found a 1961 report: bend an alkyne into a ring and it reacts with azides almost explosively, no copper needed. Think of a bamboo strip forced into a curve, straining to spring free. In 2004 she published this copper-free version, strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition, and demonstrated it on human cells with no apparent toxicity. That same year her group, using the Staudinger approach, labelled cells inside living mice.

A concept, an accident, a shared handle

The committee's scientific background describes this prize as largely about conceptualisation: Sharpless laid down what a good reaction should look like, Meldal and Sharpless found the reaction that fit it best, and Bertozzi reshaped the same family of reactions into a tool that works in living things. The point where the three threads meet is the azide, indifferent to water, oxygen and nearly everything in biology, interested only in alkynes.

A rough timeline afterwards: chemical suppliers quickly filled their catalogues with azide and alkyne building blocks; in 2008 a faster class of bioorthogonal reactions appeared (tetrazines with strained alkenes); in 2010 researchers let antibodies find tumours in mice first and then clicked a radioactive probe onto them for imaging; in October 2020 a company announced the first use of click chemistry inside the human body.

◐ Off the podium

The Nobel stops at three people. Of the two 2002 copper papers, Meldal's had Christian Tornøe as first author, and Sharpless's included Valery Fokin; the key observation behind Bertozzi's copper-free trick came from Georg Wittig and Adolf Krebs in 1961. This was also Sharpless's second Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In his phone interview he said that by the time he won the first one, he was already trying to do click chemistry.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Mostly indirectly. The committee lists uses ranging from drug discovery, herbicides and diagnostic sensors to all kinds of polymer materials; a manufacturer can build azides into a plastic and later click on something conductive or antibacterial. In the lab, the 2008 EdU method uses a click reaction to label freshly copied DNA, detectable within minutes, and has become a standard way to watch cells divide. Faster, cheaper drug and materials research eventually shows up in what you buy, but you would be hard pressed to point at a pill and say "that's click chemistry."

What may change next

The headline idea is "aim first, then load" cancer treatment: send something with a handle to the tumour, then inject a drug or radioactive molecule that clicks onto it, so the toxicity concentrates where it is wanted. All of this is in early trials. The pioneer, SQ3370, injects a polymer into the tumour and then infuses a "locked" form of doxorubicin. In phase 1, 39 patients received up to 15 times the standard dose with no dose-limiting toxicity; but the 2025 report found a 14.3% response rate in 14 patients with soft tissue sarcoma, no better than ordinary doxorubicin, and the study was stopped under its prespecified rule. A second line grows from Bertozzi's work on tumour glycans, some of which appear to switch immune cells off. A company she co-founded tested E-602, an enzyme that trims those glycans: of 21 patients reported in 2024, one had a confirmed partial response and six had stable disease, but with weekly dosing the trimming lasted only two to three days. Shown to be safe and to work as designed is still a long way from shown to be better than what exists.

What it does not mean

Click chemistry is not a button that synthesises any molecule: Sharpless said from the start it cannot make exact copies of natural products, only functional stand-ins. Bioorthogonal does not mean you can do any chemistry you like inside a body; it means a small set of reactions are selective for each other, and in living animals they still face hurdles of speed, dose and clearance, as the committee itself notes. And many organic azides are explosive, which makes large-scale use something to handle with care.

A way of thinking to borrow

Trade a little expressiveness for a small set of joins that almost never fail, and you earn the freedom to combine at scale. None of Sharpless's criteria asks for cleverness; all of them ask for reliability. When every step works, steps can be repeated by other people, by machines, thousands of times over.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Software engineering · the Unix philosophy and interface contracts: Sharpless's entry test reads like an interface spec. Liberal in what it accepts (water, air, all sorts of substituents), one well-defined output, negligible side effects, and irreversible: once committed, no rollback. Unix tools compose into complex pipelines only because each tool behaves predictably. Both make the same bet: composition gets its power from dull, dependable parts, not from any single clever one.
  • Distributed systems · tracing: Bertozzi does not re-engineer the cell. She slips a tagged part into the cell's own production line, lets normal operation carry the tag everywhere, and later queries with a probe that recognises nothing but that tag. Injecting a trace ID at the edge, letting it propagate through the call graph and aggregating by ID afterwards has the same shape. In both, the tag must be orthogonal: it must not change the system's behaviour or collide with any field already in use.
  • Biology · the cell's tolerance loophole: Bertozzi relies on the glycan assembly line accepting a slightly altered raw material. The mRNA vaccine page on this site tells how Karikó swapped one letter in RNA, exploiting the same willingness of cells to accept a part that is almost, but not quite, the usual one; the optogenetics page installs a foreign switch in neurons that answers only to light. All three insert a private control channel without rewriting the whole system.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. The 2022 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was shared equally by Sharpless, Meldal and Bertozzi for click chemistry and bioorthogonal chemistry.
  2. Traditional synthesis rebuilds natural molecules step by step with by-products at every stage; scaling up the antibiotic meropenem alone took six years.
  3. Sharpless proposed click chemistry in 2001: build molecules only with a few high-yield reactions that work in water and give essentially one product.
  4. Click chemistry deliberately gives up exact copies of natural molecules in favour of reliable modules that achieve the same function.
  5. The azide-alkyne ring-forming reaction was long known, but with heat alone it was slow and gave a mixture of two orientations.
  6. Meldal found in a routine reaction that "went wrong" that copper makes azides and alkynes form a triazole ring in essentially one orientation; Sharpless's team found the same independently.
  7. A bioorthogonal reaction pairs two chemical handles that recognise only each other inside living systems, neither disturbing biology nor disturbed by it.
  8. Bertozzi fed cells azide-tagged sugars and then used a strained ring alkyne, reported in 1961, to light up cell-surface glycans without toxic copper.
  9. In-body click therapies such as SQ3370 have proved safe in humans, but have not yet beaten standard chemotherapy and remain in early trials.
  10. Bertozzi's metabolic labelling mirrors distributed tracing: let the system carry an orthogonal tag everywhere, then query by the tag afterwards.

// Think further

The committee says this prize is largely about a concept. Why should a set of criteria and a name rank alongside an experimental discovery?
The azide-alkyne reaction existed for over a century before anyone called it click chemistry. What a concept changes is what people look for. It handed the field a filter, so that when Meldal's accident happened it was recognised at once as "the thing", and suppliers were willing to stock building blocks by the catalogue. Without the concept, the accident might have stayed a modest paper.
Does restricting yourself to a few reliable reactions mean missing genuinely novel molecules?
Sharpless admitted that cost. The useful question is when to pay it. In exploration, where you need huge numbers of cheap, fast attempts, reliability beats elegance; once you know the direction, you return to painstaking classical synthesis. Software has the same division of labour: prototype with glue scripts, then rewrite the performance-critical core once the requirements settle.
SQ3370 was safe and worked as designed, yet did not beat ordinary chemotherapy. Is that a failure?
Separate "the platform works inside a human" from "this particular drug is worth using". The first project to do click chemistry in people mostly answered the first question; successors can change the drug, the handle or the disease. But beware reading every early result as "just a matter of time". Plenty of platforms never produce a good medicine.
