MEDICINE · 2023

Change one letter, and the alarm goes quiet

Synthetic mRNA was treated as an invader because it lacked the chemical marks natural RNA carries. Add the marks, and it stops causing inflammation and makes far more protein

Katalin KarikóHungary · Szeged University / University of Pennsylvania · 1/2Drew WeissmanUSA · University of Pennsylvania · 1/2
for their discoveries concerning nucleoside base modifications that enabled the development of effective mRNA vaccines against COVID-19
2026-10-28 · BigCat

The key step in making mRNA vaccines work was getting the immune system not to notice them.

After a COVID mRNA shot your arm ached for a day or two, and maybe you ran a slight fever. It is tempting to read that as "the vaccine working."

But that shot was already the tamed version. The mRNA in it is made in a test tube, and in its original form it sets off the immune system's sentries the moment they touch it: they take it for a virus's genetic material. In 2005 Karikó and Weissman found out why that alarm rings and how to silence it: swap one of mRNA's four letters for a natural variant of itself.

Without that step it would have been hard to pack enough mRNA into a single dose, and there would have been no pair of vaccines with efficacy above 90% in December 2020.

An instruction sheet the body reads as an intruder

First, what mRNA is. A cell's DNA is the master blueprint, locked in the nucleus. To build a protein, the cell copies a temporary work order and sends it out to the ribosomes, which build to spec. That work order is messenger RNA. It was discovered in 1961, and it is broken down soon after use.

The idea came early: if mRNA tells cells what to make, why not inject a written-to-order mRNA and let a person's own cells make a viral protein (a vaccine) or a missing one (a drug), with no factory in between? In 1984 scientists at Harvard used an enzyme from a bacterial virus to make mRNA in a test tube, a process called in vitro transcription. In 1990 a team injected mRNA into mouse muscle, and the muscle duly made protein.

Over the next decade most groups quietly walked away. Karikó's Nobel lecture gives the reasons: the mRNA was unstable, and it made little protein for a short time. The committee's scientific background adds a third: test-tube mRNA triggered inflammation. Many bet on DNA vaccines instead, because DNA is sturdier.

Karikó was one of the few who stayed. She had trained in an RNA laboratory in Szeged, Hungary, and then the money ran out:

I lost my job in 1985 when the pharmaceutical company stopped sponsoring the project. I was 30 years old.— Katalin Karikó · Nobel Lecture · 2023

She moved to Philadelphia, joined the University of Pennsylvania in 1989, and spent 17 years in a small lab in the neurosurgery department trying to use mRNA to treat stroke. Inflammation is exactly what a stroke patient does not need: it makes the brain injury worse.

A photocopier, and a question about tRNA

In 1997, at a photocopier in a Penn medical school hallway, Karikó met Drew Weissman, an immunologist just arrived from the National Institutes of Health. Both read a lot and copied a lot of papers. Weissman wanted an HIV vaccine and was curious about mRNA, so they began working together.

Weissman knew how to grow dendritic cells, the immune system's sentries, which spot intruders and then wake up the rest of the defence. Fed mRNA, the dendritic cells did make the HIV protein, but they also poured out inflammatory signals. At first this looked like a bonus, since an activated sentry is a better trainer. For Karikó, with stroke patients in mind, it was bad news.

They asked the decisive question: cells are full of RNA, so why doesn't the body inflame itself over its own? They fed dendritic cells one kind of natural RNA after another. Test-tube mRNA provoked the strongest reaction, RNA from mammalian cells much less, and transfer RNA (tRNA) none at all. tRNA is famous for one thing: it is studded with chemically modified bases.

The letters of natural RNA, A, U, G and C, are often reworked by the cell's enzymes; more than a hundred such modifications are known. Test-tube mRNA has none. Nobody knew which one mattered. So they put modified building blocks straight into the synthesis reaction and hoped the enzyme would accept them. It did. Every mRNA in which U had been swapped for a modified version left the dendritic cells almost silent, including pseudouridine (Ψ), a natural variant of U first described in 1951. The alarms that had been ringing were two sensors inside the cell, TLR7 and TLR8.

They published in the journal Immunity in 2005. The paper also offered an evolutionary reading: bacterial and mitochondrial RNA strongly set off the sensors while total mammalian RNA does not, so the innate immune system may use "unmodified" as its cue for bacteria or dying tissue. The committee notes that the paper drew little attention at the time. Weissman later recalled that back then "nobody would publish it."

Why one letter changed the game

The modification did more than stop the inflammation. Follow-up work in 2008 and 2010 showed pseudouridine mRNA made roughly ten times more protein. Unmodified mRNA trips an antiviral switch called PKR, which slams the brakes on all protein production in the cell; modified mRNA leaves the switch alone and is also harder for the cell to shred. Add a purification step that strips out double-stranded RNA left over from synthesis, and in human dendritic cells protein output rose by up to a thousandfold.

Don't mistake it for a master key, though. The committee lists several pieces that had to fall into place: modified bases; lipid nanoparticles that carry mRNA safely into cells; a redesign that locks the coronavirus spike protein in its "pre-fusion" shape; and unprecedented money from governments and industry. The prize went to the first piece only.

Why that piece mattered, the committee shows with a comparison. Both approved COVID mRNA vaccines replaced every U with an improved variant, N1-methylpseudouridine. CureVac's vaccine used no modified bases and a much lower dose, and it raised lower levels of neutralizing antibodies. The committee's reading: modification let each shot carry enough mRNA without setting the recipient on fire.

From an ignored paper to the shot in your arm

Industry caught up slowly. CureVac was founded in 2000, BioNTech in 2008, Moderna in 2010. In 2013 Karikó was pushed out of Penn and went to BioNTech in Germany.

When the pandemic struck in 2020, BioNTech with Pfizer, and Moderna with the US NIH, built vaccines on base-modified mRNA. Phase 3 trials showed 95% and 94% protection, and both were approved in December 2020. Counting every kind of COVID vaccine, the committee writes, more than 13 billion doses were given worldwide, saving millions of lives.

Then the road forked. In May 2024 Moderna's respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccine, mRESVIA, was approved in the US, the first mRNA vaccine for a disease other than COVID. In August 2025 the US health department cancelled 22 mRNA vaccine projects worth nearly $500 million. In October 2026 Merck and Moderna presented phase 3 data on a personalized mRNA melanoma therapy: added to a standard immunotherapy, it cut the risk of recurrence or death by 42%.

◐ Off the podium

A Nobel can be shared by at most three people. In a 2024 interview Weissman said that he and Karikó had raised other names with the Nobel side: The person that I thought should have been recognized along with us was Pieter Cullis, who invented the lipid nanoparticle. The answer they got: two papers had described mRNA at the same time in 1961, followed by a long line of further work. There were simply too many people.

On the day of the announcement Karikó told the Nobel website that ten years earlier, in October, she had been kicked out of Penn and forced to retire. For the next nine years she commuted to Germany, and at 58 was still culturing plasmids and feeding cells with her own hands.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

COVID vaccines: the Pfizer/BioNTech and Moderna shots approved in December 2020 both swap U for a modified base. An RSV vaccine: mRESVIA, approved in May 2024 for people 60 and over, was tested on about 37,000 people in 22 countries and was 83.7% effective against lower respiratory tract disease with two or more symptoms. A less visible change is speed: the committee notes that when new variants appear, no other platform currently matches mRNA for how fast a vaccine can be updated.

What may change next

Personalized cancer vaccines are closest: sequence the mutations peculiar to your tumour, then write an mRNA that teaches the immune system to recognize them. The phase 3 melanoma trial INTerpath-001 enrolled 1,137 patients and was positive, but the therapy was given alongside a standard immunotherapy, overall survival data are not yet in, and it is not approved. Having patients make their own drugs from mRNA, in place of expensive protein medicines, is still in animal studies and early trials. Policy is another variable: after the 2025 US cuts, progress over the next few years may not be decided by science alone.

What it does not mean

It does not mean mRNA rewrites your genes: it works in the cytoplasm, never reaches the nucleus, and the committee states plainly that it cannot integrate into the genome. It does not mean modified mRNA causes no reaction: sore arms and fatigue remain common, and myocarditis and pericarditis have been reported, at what the US CDC describes as low rates. It does not mean Karikó alone invented mRNA vaccines: her Nobel lecture goes out of its way to thank the many scientists who laid the groundwork over decades. Nor does every mRNA therapy need modified bases: the committee points out that some cancer vaccine trials use unmodified mRNA.

A way of thinking to borrow

When the system keeps rejecting what you built, stop tuning your own version and go find the natural sample the system never rejects, then ask what it has that yours lacks. The turn in Karikó and Weissman's story came from asking why tRNA stayed quiet.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Security · signatures and spoofing: innate immunity checks RNA much like an authentication layer that only asks "is it signed?" Modified means insider; bare means outsider. Karikó and Weissman stamped an external message with the house seal, so it ran as an internal instruction. The 2005 paper also suggests where the rule came from: bacterial and mitochondrial RNA carry few modifications, so "unsigned" is a cheap proxy for "from bacteria." Unlike a forgery attack, nothing was cracked; the seal they used is one nature already makes.
  • AI · shortcut features: classifiers often learn a cheap surface cue in place of the real judgment, calling anything on grass a cow. TLR7 and TLR8 use "is the U modified?" to decide "is this a virus?", a shortcut accurate enough in the environment it evolved in. Know which feature it reads and you can route an input around it. This time the input belonged to us.
  • Distributed systems · pushing compute to the edge: classic vaccines and protein drugs are made as finished products in central bioreactors and shipped out. mRNA ships only the instructions and lets each person's cells build the protein locally. Karikó's lecture makes exactly this case: protein drugs are expensive, while mRNA is easy and cheap to make. The cost is the familiar edge-computing one: you don't control every client's runtime, so doses and reactions vary more from person to person.
  • Immunology · two layers of "don't attack yourself": the 2025 Medicine prize on this site is about the brake in adaptive immunity, regulatory T cells. This one is about how older innate immunity tells self from foreign by chemical marks. Same problem, solved separately at two layers. For another side of RNA, see the 2024 Medicine prize: a class of tiny RNAs that turn down the "volume" of genes.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. mRNA is the temporary work order a cell copies from DNA; an mRNA vaccine has your own cells build a viral protein.
  2. Early test-tube mRNA had three problems: it was unstable, made little protein and caused inflammation, so most groups gave up.
  3. Katalin Karikó lost her job in 1985 when funding ended, then kept working on mRNA therapy in a small lab at Penn.
  4. Karikó and Drew Weissman met at a Penn photocopier in 1997 and began testing mRNA vaccines on dendritic cells.
  5. The key clue was tRNA: rich in modified bases, it caused no inflammation at all when given to dendritic cells.
  6. Swapping U in mRNA for pseudouridine or other modified versions all but silenced the TLR7 and TLR8 sensors, published in 2005.
  7. Modified bases also dodge the PKR brake, so the same mRNA makes about ten times more protein and lasts longer.
  8. COVID mRNA vaccines also needed lipid nanoparticles, a stabilized spike and huge funding; the Nobel rewarded only modified bases.
  9. mRNA never enters the nucleus or integrates into the genome, and modified vaccines still cause reactions such as sore arms.
  10. Innate immunity tells self from foreign by chemical marks, like signature-only authentication or a shortcut feature learned by an AI.

// Think further

The 2005 paper went almost unnoticed and became pivotal fifteen years later. How should research funding treat discoveries with no visible use yet?
Think of basic research as buying options: most expire worthless, and a few pay for all of them. The catch is that someone has to hold the option until it pays. Karikó lost her position twice, and this line nearly broke. Worth asking whether peer review could reserve small, long-term grants specifically for unfashionable directions backed by solid evidence.
The committee honoured modified bases, not lipid nanoparticles. Does the three-person limit still make sense for today's big science?
mRNA vaccines are a chain: discovering mRNA, in vitro transcription, delivery, antigen design, clinical trials. Break any link and there is no vaccine. A three-person cap forces the committee to pick one "decisive breakthrough" to tell the story through, and every other link becomes background. Weissman himself said Cullis should have shared it. Which link would you name as the one without which nothing works?
The immune system tells friend from foe by "modified or not," and humans exploited that rule. Could viruses learn the same disguise?
This is an evolutionary arms-race question. If a chemical mark is the credential for "self," a virus that can mimic it gains an edge. The 2005 paper only discusses why bacterial and mitochondrial RNA get flagged; it does not address the viral side. The open question is how long a recognition system that checks a single feature can stay stable under long-term adversarial pressure.
