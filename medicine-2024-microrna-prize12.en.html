MEDICINE · 2024

Life's volume knob is 22 letters long

Two labs each held half an answer about a millimetre-long worm. Put together, the halves revealed a class of tiny RNAs whose whole job is to tell other genes to keep it down

Victor AmbrosUMass Chan Medical School · USA · 1/2Gary RuvkunMassachusetts General Hospital / Harvard Medical School · USA · 1/2
for the discovery of microRNA and its role in post-transcriptional gene regulation
Announced 2024-10-07 · 2026-10-24 · BigCat

This prize is not for a switch. It is for subtraction: complex life got complex not only by saying more, but by making other things say less.

Twenty-two letters. That is shorter than this sentence, and it is the full length of the molecule this prize is about.

A neuron in your brain and a cell lining your gut carry exactly the same set of roughly twenty thousand genes. One sends signals, the other absorbs food. The whole difference is which pages of the shared manual each one reads, and when it turns the page: things a body does in childhood must not be done again in adulthood.

Before 1993, biologists thought they understood page-turning. Then a worm produced an RNA just 22 nucleotides long. It made no protein. It did not "do" anything. Its only talent was making another gene go quiet.

A worm stuck in its own childhood

The big answer to "how does a cell pick its pages?" came in the 1960s. François Jacob and Jacques Monod showed that proteins called transcription factors sit on DNA and decide which genes get copied out; they shared the 1965 Nobel Prize. Thousands more transcription factors followed, and, in the committee's words, for a long time it was believed that the main principles of gene regulation had been solved.

The crack appeared in the roundworm C. elegans: one millimetre long, transparent, fast-breeding, brought into the lab by Sydney Brenner more than five decades ago. Mutant screens in Brenner's lab in the 1970s turned up an odd animal, with a broken gene called lin-4. It kept re-running its larval programmes, never built the structure it needed to lay eggs, and filled up with eggs inside. Another mutant, lin-14, did the opposite: it skipped its larval stages and became a tiny "adult". One could not leave childhood; the other never had one.

In the 1980s Victor Ambros and Gary Ruvkun were both postdocs in Robert Horvitz's lab at MIT, and both were hooked on this pair. Ambros later showed that lin-4 is the one holding lin-14 down. But how? Getting from a mutant to the stretch of DNA behind it was years of grind back then; cloning lin-14 alone took the two of them years.

The evening of 11 June 1992

Each then set up his own lab around Harvard: Ambros chased lin-4, Ruvkun chased lin-14.

The Ambros lab walked along the chromosome until lin-4 was pinned to a 693-letter piece of DNA. Then the strange part: there was no sensible protein recipe in it. They deliberately scrambled the reading frame, and lin-4 still worked. The only conclusion: this gene makes no protein at all. In 1991 they caught its products, two very short RNAs, one 61 letters long and one just 22. To be sure, they screened more than 20,000 mutagenised chromosomes to find a second lin-4 mutant. It differed by a single letter.

Ruvkun was looking at lin-14's messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that gets turned into protein). Some mutant worms were missing a piece of its tail, the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), a kind of endnote that is read but never made into protein. Without it, the worms had 4 to 7 times more LIN-14 protein, yet the same amount of mRNA. So the brake was not on copying the gene, but on using the copy. When he grafted that tail onto another gene, the other gene fell under the same control.

On the evening of 11 June 1992, the two swapped sequences. Both saw it at once: lin-4's 22 letters could partly zip onto several repeated stretches in lin-14's tail. In December 1993 their papers appeared back to back in Cell: a new kind of RNA that base-pairs with another gene's mRNA tail and cuts how much protein it makes.

at that moment it was just the quirky, what we were working on, it was really interesting.— Gary Ruvkun · phone interview with nobelprize.org on the day of the announcement, asked whether it had seemed Nobel-worthy at the time

Seven quiet years, then let-7

The committee describes the response to the 1993 papers as "almost deafening silence". The reason was concrete: searched against every sequence then on file, lin-4 matched only other worms of the same genus. The verdict was a worm quirk, irrelevant to us. Ruvkun later told the Harvard Gazette the two of them were "fine" and "weren't terrible", but nobody thought they were walking on water.

The turn took seven years. In 2000 Ruvkun's lab found a second small RNA of this kind, let-7, 21 letters long. This time the database returned hits in the fruit fly and in humans. They went on to detect let-7 in vertebrates, sea squirts, molluscs, annelids and arthropods, and in each it switched on relatively late in development. In his Nobel lecture Ambros lined up the let-7 sequences of a fish, a fly and the worm: across 22 letters, fish and worm differ by one.

Nobody could call that a quirk. In 2001, the labs of Thomas Tuschl, David Bartel and Ambros each pulled a large batch of similar small RNAs out of humans, flies and worms, in the same issue of Science. MicroRNAs were no longer two oddities; they were a whole class of molecules. Another thread joined at the same time: in 1998 Andrew Fire and Craig Mello showed that double-stranded RNA silences genes (Nobel Prize 2006), and in 1999 David Baulcombe found small RNAs of similar length in plants. It turned out they share the same cellular machinery.

The difference is in how they use it. Foreign small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) tend to match their target perfectly and have it cut. A microRNA often pairs over only a short stretch, its "seed region", with the rest loosely matched. The committee reads this partial matching as something evolution kept on purpose, to tune each target rather than destroy it. And because only a short stretch must match, one microRNA can regulate many genes, and one gene can be regulated by many microRNAs.

From two worm genes to a thousand knobs

Today the human genome is known to hold more than a thousand microRNA genes. The miRBase database lists over 48,000 mature microRNA sequences from 271 organisms. Even viruses carry some.

How much they matter shows when you take them away. Cutting a mature microRNA out of its precursor takes an enzyme called Dicer; mice and zebrafish without Dicer die as embryos. In people, mutations in the seed region of one microRNA (miR-96) cause progressive hearing loss, and in DICER1 syndrome, people born with one broken copy of the Dicer gene often develop tumours in childhood, in kidney, lung, thyroid and elsewhere.

Zoom out to evolution and the committee's background paper draws a line: animal microRNAs arose about 600 million years ago, when cells began organising into complex bodies, and their number grew as cell types multiplied. The oldest microRNAs act early in the embryo; the newer ones more often work in adult cells. What was rewarded is a layer of control that multicellular life built for itself, not a worm's home remedy.

◐ Off the podium

The first author of Ambros's paper, Rosalind Lee, is his colleague and his wife; she and Rhonda Feinbaum share first authorship, and they did the hands-on work of cloning lin-4. On announcement day the Nobel Prize's social account posted a photo of the couple and noted her role. According to El País, the post drew more than 2.2 million views and many people asked why she had not shared the prize. A microRNA researcher the paper interviewed argued that the corresponding author is the one who designs the research; the paper also noted that the two 1993 studies already had six hands-on authors, over the three-laureate limit. Bruce Wightman, first author of the other paper, pointed out that the discovery itself rested on earlier work by Chalfie, Horvitz and Sulston.

Ambros never expected the call. He had assumed the 2006 prize for RNA interference "encompassed microRNAs", and one of that year's winners, Craig Mello, had been his own PhD student.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Honestly: this page found no approved therapy that uses a microRNA itself as the drug. A 2017 review in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery put the furthest candidates at phase II. What did reach the pharmacy is a close cousin. In 2018 the US approved the first RNA interference drug, patisiran, for a hereditary form of amyloidosis. It runs on the same cellular machinery as microRNAs, and it aims at the very same spot: the 3′ tail of its target mRNA. Ruvkun points out that Alnylam, the company behind such drugs, did not exist twenty years ago and is now one of the ten biggest companies in Massachusetts. A quieter change is in the clinic: families with miR-96 hearing loss or DICER1 syndrome now have a gene to test for.

What may change next

Two clinical attempts are worth remembering. In 2013 miravirsen, which blocks the liver microRNA miR-122, cut virus levels in a dose-dependent way in a phase II trial of 36 hepatitis C patients; in the 7 mg group, four patients had virus fall below detection at some point. The other is a cautionary tale: MRX34, a mimic of the tumour-suppressing microRNA miR-34a, was given to 85 patients with advanced solid tumours. Three had partial responses, but serious immune reactions led to four deaths and the trial was closed early (published 2020). A 2022 review names the core difficulty: a microRNA's strength is that it steers a whole set of genes at once, which is exactly why off-target effects are so hard to avoid. The Harvard Gazette reported in 2025 that trials are running in heart disease, cancer, Crohn's disease and Alzheimer's, all still at the trial stage.

What it does not mean

This is not gene editing: a microRNA never changes a letter of DNA, only how much protein gets made. It is not the 2023 mRNA-vaccine prize either; both names contain "RNA", but one is a protein recipe delivered from outside and the other is the body's own volume knob. Most microRNAs are not on/off switches; for most targets they turn output down a notch. And "a blood test for microRNAs will catch cancer early" is not where things stand: the committee only says diagnostics are being developed.

A way of thinking to borrow

To tell a quirk from a rule, lin-4 and let-7 suggest a concrete test: look for it in other species. Something found in one species may be a local oddity. Something nearly letter-for-letter identical in fish, flies and humans, untouched by evolution for hundreds of millions of years, is doing an important job.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • AI · content-addressable lookup: a microRNA finds its targets not by address but by partial match on a short sequence, pressing down harder the more sites match. The seven sites in lin-14's tail act like seven summed weights. That is the same structure as the content-addressable memory of Hopfield networks in our 2024 Physics page: give part of the content, retrieve the whole record. It also resembles the soft query–key matching of attention. The difference: the cell only subtracts.
  • Distributed systems · pub/sub and rate limiting: a microRNA is like a broadcast "slow down" on a topic; every mRNA whose 3′ tail subscribes to that sequence gets throttled, with no central scheduler. Redundancy works the same way: several microRNAs sharing a seed act like replicas, so losing one does not break the system. The committee notes that this redundancy also makes the system hard for viruses to hijack.
  • Software engineering · protocol ossification: fish and worm let-7 differ by one letter after hundreds of millions of years. It is the same reason old protocols cannot be changed: one seed is "called" by many downstream mRNAs, so changing one letter breaks every interface at once. The more clients, the less you can touch it.
  • Complexity science · regulation by subtraction: microRNA numbers grew with the number of cell types. Complex systems often get complex not by adding more doers but by adding finer-grained inhibition. It is the structure of the regulatory T cells in our 2025 Medicine page: a few dedicated restrainers keep a high-gain system from running away.
  • Philosophy of science · anomalies and sample size: lin-4 was ignored for seven years not because it was wrong, but because an N=1 anomaly can be filed as a local quirk without disturbing theory. Kuhn said anomalies must pile up before a paradigm shifts; here one more example, let-7, plus its conservation across species, was enough to tip the scale.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Every cell in the body carries the same genes; cells specialise through gene regulation, reading the right pages in the right cell at the right time.
  2. The worm mutant lin-4 keeps repeating larval development while lin-14 skips it, and Victor Ambros showed lin-4 holds lin-14 down.
  3. The Ambros lab found that lin-4 encodes no protein, only a tiny RNA just 22 nucleotides long.
  4. Gary Ruvkun found lin-14 is controlled through its mRNA's 3′ untranslated region: less protein, unchanged mRNA.
  5. Swapping sequences on 11 June 1992 revealed partial matches between lin-4 and lin-14's tail, published back to back in Cell in 1993.
  6. MicroRNA was written off as a worm quirk for seven years until Ruvkun's lab found let-7, conserved in humans and many animals, in 2000.
  7. MicroRNAs tune output by partial seed pairing rather than cutting, so one microRNA can regulate many genes at once.
  8. Humans have over a thousand microRNA genes; losing Dicer kills mouse embryos, and DICER1 syndrome brings childhood tumours.
  9. Drugs using microRNAs themselves remain in trials, MRX34 was halted after immune deaths, and the approved cousin is RNA interference.
  10. let-7 barely changed over hundreds of millions of years, like a heavily called old protocol: the more users, the less it can change.

// Think further

If the two had not swapped sequences that evening in 1992, how much later would microRNAs have been found?
Each held half the picture. Ambros knew lin-4 was a tiny RNA but not where it landed; Ruvkun knew lin-14's tail held control sites but not what landed there. Discoveries that only appear when two halves meet depend on whether data can flow before publication. Would two young labs today, with fiercer competition and faster preprints, show each other their cards before publishing?
A drug that touches hundreds of genes at once: bug or feature?
Classic drug design wants one target, the more specific the better. A microRNA is one-to-many by nature, and that is part of why MRX34 failed. But many diseases, cancer among them, are whole networks out of balance. Perhaps the problem is not having many targets but not yet being able to predict which way the whole set will tip. It is like bumping the version of a low-level library: you can never list everyone it will affect.
lin-4 seemed worm-only; let-7 is everywhere in animals. How do we know whether the next "quirk" is a quirk or a rule?
Sequence comparison is one test, but it has blind spots: things with the same function and different sequences won't match, and results depend on what is in the database. In his banquet speech Ruvkun noted that sequencing centres have read the genomes of 30,000 species over the past 30 years; in 1993 there was far less to compare against. Before dismissing a result, ask whether we have shown it is not general, or only failed to find its relatives yet.
