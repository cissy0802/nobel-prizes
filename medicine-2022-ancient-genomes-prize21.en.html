PHYSIOLOGY OR MEDICINE · 2022

Extinct relatives live on in your genome

Reading the whole Neanderthal genome out of 40,000-year-old bone, then finding an unknown kind of human in a single finger bone

Svante PääboMax Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology · 1/1
for his discoveries concerning the genomes of extinct hominins and human evolution
2026-11-02 · BigCat

For most of human history, several kinds of humans walked the Earth at once. Being the only one is a 40,000-year-old novelty.

In 2020 a lot of households saw the same thing happen: everyone caught the same virus, one person had a sore throat for a few days, and another ended up in intensive care.

Part of that difference is written in DNA. That autumn, two scientists showed that the single biggest genetic risk factor for severe COVID-19 was a stretch of about 50,000 DNA letters on chromosome 3. It came from Neanderthals, a kind of human that vanished around 40,000 years ago. Today roughly half of people in South Asia and about 16% of Europeans carry it.

That conclusion was only possible because someone had first read the genome of an extinct human. Thirty years earlier, almost everyone thought that couldn't be done.

Bones tell you shapes, not who had children with whom

The first skeleton recognised as Neanderthal was dug up in 1856 in the Neander Valley near Düsseldorf. More turned up across Europe and western Asia. In the committee's account, Neanderthals lived in Eurasia from about 400,000 to 30,000 years ago; anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) appeared in Africa around 300,000 years ago and left it 60,000–70,000 years ago. The two shared Eurasia for at least 20,000 years.

What happened between them was debated for a long time. Bones can't answer the most direct question: did they and we have children together? For that you need their DNA.

Three things stand in the way. After death, DNA breaks into ever shorter pieces and its letters get chemically rewritten; the classic damage makes a C read like a T. What little survives is swamped by bacterial DNA. And worst of all, the researcher is human too. Touch the bone or sneeze and modern human DNA gets in, while the thing you're looking for is the DNA of another human, which looks almost identical.

Pääbo learned this the hard way. As an Uppsala PhD student in the early 1980s, officially working on immunology, he ran a secret side project extracting DNA from Egyptian mummies. He cloned human DNA from a 2,400-year-old mummy sample, and the result drew attention worldwide. He later acknowledged that it was probably contaminated with modern human DNA.

So he practised on animals first, mammoths and cave bears, where stray human DNA is easy to spot. He designed dedicated clean rooms and insisted that key results be reproduced independently in another lab.

An upper-arm bone from 1856, and 61 letters

In 1990 Pääbo became a professor in Munich. He went to the Rheinisches Museum in Bonn, which keeps the 1856 "type specimen", and was given a small piece of its right humerus.

His first target wasn't the full genome but mitochondrial DNA. Mitochondria are the cell's power plants and carry their own little genome, about 16,500 letters against three billion in the nucleus. The nuclear genome exists in two copies per cell; mitochondrial DNA in hundreds to thousands, which gives it a far better chance of surviving in bone.

Using PCR, a way to copy a short stretch of DNA millions of times, the team read a first sequence just 61 letters long, with variants never seen in living people. They repeated it from a fresh piece of bone and got the same answer, and sent bone to Mark Stoneking's lab at Penn State for an independent replication. In all, 123 clones covering 13 fragments, each sequenced at least twice, added up to 379 letters.

Compared against 2,051 modern humans and 59 chimpanzees, the Neanderthal sequence fell outside the whole range of living humans. The 1997 paper in Cell estimated that the common ancestor of the two mitochondrial lineages was four times older than the common ancestor of all modern human mitochondria, and concluded that Neanderthals went extinct without leaving their mitochondrial DNA to us.

That conclusion would turn out to be half right. Mitochondrial DNA is passed down only through mothers, and a line vanishes whenever its carriers have only sons. It tells half a family tree.

2010: one genome, and a kind of human known only from DNA

In 1999 Pääbo founded the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig and went after the three-billion-letter nuclear genome. The break came from a new generation of sequencers: instead of fishing out chosen fragments, they read every DNA molecule in the sample, hundreds of thousands per run, each 100–200 letters long, which matched the size of the scraps left in old bone. In 2006 the team published its first million letters and announced a draft within two years.

They screened more than 70 bones and teeth and settled on three from Vindija Cave in Croatia, each about 40,000 years old and each more than 1% Neanderthal DNA. Interpreting the data took population geneticists, so a consortium of about 50 scientists formed, with David Reich of Harvard among the key members. They estimated modern contamination three independent ways, and every estimate came in under 1%. One method is neat: if a bone came from a woman, any Y-chromosome fragment in the data can only have come from a male researcher.

The draft appeared in Science in May 2010, more than four billion letters. The surprise: Neanderthals were equally close to Europeans and East Asians, and closer to both than to Africans. The simplest explanation is interbreeding just after modern humans left Africa, before they spread out, probably in the Middle East. People living outside Africa today carry a percent or two of Neanderthal DNA.

The same year brought something stranger. In 2008, Denisova Cave in Siberia's Altai Mountains yielded the tip of a child's little finger, from a layer dated 48,000–30,000 years ago. Its DNA was remarkably well preserved. Its mitochondria differed from ours at 385 positions on average, against 202 for Neanderthals and 1,462 for a chimpanzee. This was a human no one had seen before, named the Denisovans: the first kind of human ever identified from DNA alone, with no skull and no face. Present-day Melanesians, it turned out, get 4–6% of their genome from them.

From one finger bone to a whole discipline

The new field came to be called paleogenomics, and the milestones kept coming.

2012: a new single-stranded library method gave a high-quality Denisovan genome, read more than 30 times over, from the same finger bone. 2014: a toe bone from the Altai yielded a high-quality Neanderthal genome, and the Neanderthal share in non-Africans was refined to 1.5–2.1%. The same year another team showed that the version of EPAS1 that helps Tibetans cope with thin air at altitude came from Denisovan-like ancestors; it is very rare among Han Chinese. Later, another bone fragment from Denisova Cave turned out to belong to a first-generation child of a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father.

Each of us carries only a percent or two, but different people carry different pieces, and together about 40% of the Neanderthal genome survives scattered across living humans. Some regions carry no archaic ancestry at all; researchers call them "archaic deserts" and suspect that whatever got in there could not be tolerated.

The ancient genomes also work as a mirror. At about 31,000 positions, every living human carries a new letter while Neanderthals and Denisovans keep the old one inherited since the split from chimpanzees. Around 100 of these change proteins; around 3,000 sit in regulatory regions that decide when and how loudly a gene is expressed (compare the 2024 medicine prize on microRNA, another volume knob). "What makes us us" now has a candidate list. A list is not an answer.

In 2025 a team led by Fu Qiaomei at the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology recovered 95 proteins from a nearly complete skull found in Harbin, at least 146,000 years old, and placed it among the Denisovans. The skull had earlier been named a new species, Homo longi. Fifteen years on, the human known only from DNA had a face. Fu was one of the authors of the 2010 Denisovan papers.

The fact that the last 40 thousand years is quite unique in human history, in that we are the only form of humans around.— Svante Pääbo · nobelprize.org telephone interview on the day of the announcement · 2022

◐ Off the podium

Pääbo was the year's only medicine laureate. STAT reported some head-scratching among ancient-DNA researchers about the solo award: David Reich had worked with Pääbo on the Neanderthal data since 2007, and his lab went on to sequence more than 16,000 ancient human genomes. Reich's own reply was unambiguous. He said he was "thrilled" the field had been honoured and that Pääbo "was, more than anyone, the pioneer of this field." At the banquet Pääbo said he accepted the prize as recognition of everyone who had contributed to the field, not just his group, and admitted there had been errors and setbacks along the way.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

The inherited part of disease risk. In 2020 Hugo Zeberg and Pääbo showed that the top genetic risk segment for severe COVID-19 came from Neanderthals; in 2021 they found another Neanderthal segment, on chromosome 12, that lowers the risk and is common everywhere outside Africa. The same ancestors handed down both risk and protection. (For the vaccine story, see the 2023 medicine prize on mRNA.)

The ancestry report you may have seen. The "Neanderthal variants" line in consumer genetic tests depends on these ancient genomes as its reference.

What may change next

The most practical direction is those 31,000 uniquely human positions: working out what each does in a cell is Pääbo's current main line, and it is still basic research. The committee's background lists association and mechanism studies; it does not point to any drug or therapy that has come out of this. Another direction is mapping where and when ancient humans lived. Africa, too hot for old DNA to last, still had no archaic genome sequenced as of the 2022 background, and that gap is the one most likely to be filled.

What it does not mean

It doesn't mean "I'm more Neanderthal than my spouse." Geneticist Iain Mathieson has explained that such reports count how many of roughly 1,400 known Neanderthal variants you carry, which is not an ancestry proportion. By his rough estimate, the real proportion varies by only about a tenth of a percentage point among people of the same ancestry, about the size of the statistical error in estimating it for one person. Nor does it mean Africans are "purer": the scientific background notes inferred "ghost" archaic ancestry in West African populations, with no reference genome found yet. And it doesn't mean we've found the gene for being human: Neanderthals also had big brains, lived in groups and used tools.

A way of thinking to borrow

Treat contamination as the default, and design a way to measure it. Pääbo's team tagged their own libraries with a unique sequence key, looked for Y chromosomes in female bones, and estimated contamination three independent ways. When the signal you want looks exactly like the noise, first prove you didn't bring it in yourself.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · merge history and cherry-picks: a modern genome is a main branch with commits cherry-picked from two deleted branches. The branches are gone; the commits remain. Segment length is a timestamp: recombination chops foreign segments shorter every generation, which is why the 32.7-kilobase length of the Tibetan EPAS1 segment fits the period when the two kinds of humans coexisted. "Archaic deserts" are the files where the merge keeps getting rejected. Where the analogy breaks: nobody chooses what gets merged; survival does the reviewing.
  • AI · benchmark contamination and canary strings: using humans to study another human's DNA is the classic problem of an instrument drawn from the same distribution as its target, like a model that may have seen the test set during training. Pääbo's fixes match the field's: tag genuine samples with a unique key (a canary string), reproduce in an independent lab (a held-out set), and estimate leakage with at least three independent measures.
  • Buddhism · non-self and the edges of "human": there is no pure "us" in the genome, only pieces from different sources temporarily gathered in one body. On the day of the announcement Pääbo mused that had Neanderthals lasted another 40,000 years, we might not draw such a clear line between animals and humans. "Human" looks more like a conventional name than an entity with a sharp boundary.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Svante Pääbo won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for reading the genomes of extinct humans, founding paleogenomics.
  2. Ancient DNA work faces three obstacles: DNA breaks and decays, bacterial DNA swamps it, and researchers' own DNA contaminates it.
  3. Pääbo's early mummy DNA result was, by his own later admission, probably contaminated with modern human DNA.
  4. In 1997 Pääbo's team read 379 letters of mitochondrial DNA from the 1856 Neanderthal type specimen.
  5. Mitochondrial DNA follows only the maternal line, so the 1997 verdict of no Neanderthal legacy was only half right.
  6. The 2010 draft Neanderthal genome showed that people outside Africa carry a percent or two of Neanderthal DNA.
  7. Denisovans were the first kind of human identified from the DNA of a single finger bone; Melanesians carry 4–6% Denisovan DNA.
  8. Tibetans' high-altitude EPAS1 variant came from Denisovan-like ancestors, and the top genetic risk segment for severe COVID-19 came from Neanderthals.
  9. A consumer report's Neanderthal variant count is not an ancestry proportion, and real differences among people of one ancestry are tiny.
  10. Pääbo's contamination controls follow the same logic as canary strings, held-out sets and independent replication in AI evaluation.

// Think further

In 1997 mitochondrial data said "no mixing"; in 2010 the nuclear genome said "mixing". Where have you seen data tell only half the story?
The mitochondrial data weren't wrong; they were structurally blind to the paternal half, and a maternal line can die out by pure chance. The useful question isn't whether data are accurate but what they can and cannot see in principle. Logs that record only successful requests and surveys that reach only people willing to answer have the same blind spot.
If Neanderthals were alive today, should human rights cover them?
Pääbo floated two possibilities on announcement day: even worse racism, or a completely different sense of our place among living things. We can draw a clear line between humans and animals today partly because everyone in between is gone. How much of that line is biology, and how much historical accident?
One kind of human had a genome fifteen years before it had a face. What actually defines a species?
Denisovans began as a sequence; only in 2025 did proteins from the Harbin skull tie sequence to shape, and that same skull had first been named Homo longi from its form. Morphology, genes and proteins don't always agree. Which one decides? Compare identity defined by interface versus identity defined by implementation.
