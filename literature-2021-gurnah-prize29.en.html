LITERATURE · 2021

Before history got simplified, he made it complicated again

A refugee who left Zanzibar at eighteen wrote ten novels in his second language, English: the wounds colonialism left, the lies and silences of the displaced, and how small lives retrieve something from trauma

Abdulrazak GurnahTanzania / UK · 1/1
for his uncompromising and compassionate penetration of the effects of colonialism and the fate of the refugee in the gulf between cultures and continents
2026-11-10 · BigCat

When the Academy called, he assumed it was a cold call and said nothing, waiting for the machine to hang up.

Most of us remember the first world map on a classroom wall. Nobody asks, at that age, who drew it, what sits in the middle, which places come out large and which come out small.

Zanzibar, an island in the Indian Ocean. A seven-year-old's regular teacher is away, and the substitute is a short, plump man in a kanzu and kofia, his face pitted with smallpox scars. He holds up a hen's egg and asks who can make it stand on its end, and that is how Christopher Columbus enters the room. As he talks he draws on the blackboard in one continuous line of chalk: the coast of Europe, Iberia, North Africa, down to the Cape, back up the east coast, round Arabia and India, all the way to China. Then he puts a dot halfway down the East African coast: "This is where we are, a long way from China."

When the boy peeps back in later, the board has been wiped. Decades on, the same boy is an old man who has spent seven months as an asylum seeker in a foggy English seaside town. By the Sea (2001) opens with his voice:

"I speak to maps. And sometimes they say something back to me."— Abdulrazak Gurnah · By the Sea · excerpt on nobelprize.org

It was maps, he goes on, that turned the world into territory, something that could be possessed.

A house by the harbour, and £400 borrowed from a relative

Gurnah was born in Zanzibar in 1948. Talking at the Swedish Academy in 2022, he described the house: from upstairs you could see the harbour and its warehouses, the sea five minutes away. In the monsoon season, sailors from all round the Indian Ocean would "pour" off their boats into the square in front of the house, still smelling of the sea. For the children it was thrilling. His aunt remembered the other side: when the season ended and the wind turned north, parents kept children indoors, because now and then a child disappeared with the departing ships.

The committee's chair, Anders Olsson, calls the island "a cosmopolitan society before globalisation": ruled in turn by Portuguese, Arab, German and British powers, marked by the slave trade, trading with the whole world. Zanzibar left British rule peacefully in December 1963; after the 1964 revolution, citizens of Arab origin were persecuted, and there were massacres. Gurnah belonged to the targeted group. After finishing school, at eighteen, he and his brother left.

In London the immigration officer asked what they were there for. "Just visiting, tourists" was all they could say. A tourist had to show something like £400, so they had borrowed it from a relative. A few questions in a side room, and they were in. Only later did he realise he had landed in one of Britain's periodic panics about immigrants, that time about East African Asians. He could not return to Zanzibar until 1984, in time to see his father shortly before he died.

He began writing at twenty-one. Swahili was his first language, but on the island, he has said, his access to Swahili literature was virtually nil; what fed him early was The Arabian Nights, Arabic and Persian poetry, the surahs of the Quran, and then English from Shakespeare to Naipaul. So English became the tool. The critic Lu Jiande, at the Chinese launch of his novels, set him against Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, who also began in English and later went back to an African language to think through the postcolonial question. Gurnah went on to teach English and postcolonial literatures at the University of Kent until his retirement, writing on Soyinka, Ngũgĩ and Rushdie.

The citation, word by word

"Uncompromising." In his Nobel lecture, simply titled "Writing," he says that a few years into exile he saw "a new, simpler history" being built, transforming and even obliterating what had happened. It was not only the victors' work; it also suited commentators, scholars and writers who wanted "a familiar narrative of racial emancipation and progress." He refused it, and refused the opposite simplification too: Olsson notes that he has always avoided nostalgia for "a more pristine pre-colonial Africa." In Paradise, as the Nanjing scholar Dan Hansong points out, the merchants' caravans trade with one hand and keep guns ready with the other; victim and perpetrator blur.

"Compassionate." Asked what the word means to him, he talked about our enormous capacity for cruelty, and the possibility of kindness that is always there. That is not forgiveness, he said; it is understanding how a person can be so compromised by state or society that even kindness cannot get out.

"The effects of colonialism." His generation, he says, were children of colonialism in a way their parents and children were not, educated at the height of imperial confidence, "when domination disguised its real self in euphemisms and we agreed to the subterfuge." Later corruption and misrule were, in some measure, part of that legacy.

"The fate of the refugee." In her presentation speech, the Academy's Ellen Mattson described one story told again and again: a boy taken, sold, or running for his life, who grows up where he does not belong and gets by on irony, withdrawal, silence and lies. The lies are fiction, a shield, and over time "a Nessus Tunic that destroys its wearer."

"The gulf." His characters, Olsson writes, hang between cultures and continents, between a life that was and a life emerging, a state "that can never be resolved." Gurnah himself called the citation accurate but not the complete account; one novel is hard enough to put in a sentence, let alone ten.

Inside the books: two narrators, neither to be trusted

The breakthrough, Paradise (1994), was shortlisted for that year's Booker. The boy Yusuf is pawned by his father to a merchant to settle a debt and travels with a caravan into the interior. Olsson reads it as a retelling of the Quran's story of Joseph, with an obvious nod to Conrad; but where Joseph is rewarded for his faith, Gurnah's Yusuf abandons the woman he loves to join the German army he had despised. Frustrating the expected ending is a habit.

By the Sea turns that habit on narration itself. Saleh Omar, narrator of the first half, arrives seeking asylum on a visa forged in the name of a bitter enemy. Latif, narrator of the second, happens to be assigned to help him settle, and is the enemy's son. Saleh tries to remember; Latif does everything to forget. The two voices, Olsson says, dissolve the plot's direction and the narrators' authority along with it. Lu Jiande put it bluntly: you assume this refugee narrator will win your sympathy, and then he doesn't.

The prose carries Swahili, Arabic, Hindi and German; Olsson calls it "wonderfully clear and nuanced," and capable of being "sarcastic and hilarious in a deadpan way." In Desertion (2005) the story breaks off after its first part and a narrator steps out to say the tale is not about him:

"It is about how one story contains many and how they belong not to us but are part of the random currents of our time."— Gurnah · Desertion · quoted in Olsson's biobibliography

Afterlives (2020) picks up where Paradise ends, in German East Africa. Reviewing it in the Guardian, Maaza Mengiste, one of the African novelists Gurnah says he admires, noted that talk of European colonialism in Africa had long left Germany out, and that the novel "gathers close all those who were meant to be forgotten." When a young man defends the Germans, his listeners fall silent until one replies: "My friend, they have eaten you."

Why him, and why then

He really was obscure. On announcement day Publishers Weekly reported that his best-selling US title, Desertion, had sold under 2,000 copies through BookScan-reporting outlets since 2005, and that many of his books were out of print. In Chinese there had been just two short stories in one anthology, according to Feng Tao, the Shanghai Translation Publishing House editor who called him the coldest pick of the last ten or even twenty years. Chinese coverage noted that Ngũgĩ had been the hotter name.

On the phone that morning the interviewer mentioned the refugee crisis. Gurnah answered that Europeans streaming out into the world was nothing new, yet Europe now met arrivals with "a kind of miserliness, as if there isn't enough to go around," when many "don't come empty handed."

◐ Off the podium

The presidents of Tanzania and of Zanzibar congratulated him at once. But, Al Jazeera reported, Tanzanian social media erupted over whether he could be claimed as "ours." The journalist Erick Kabendera wrote that one reason the country resists dual citizenship is fear that people who fled persecution in Zanzibar might come back and claim their stolen assets: "And we're shamelessly celebrating his victory?" Gurnah told AFP: "I am from there. In my mind I live there."

◎ What it means for the rest of us

What reading him gives you

Refugees stop being a faceless category. His displaced people lie, look down on others, nurse grudges, and are tender. That complexity is harder than pity, and more respectful.

You start noticing your own silences. On a 2024 visit to China he said silence can be the only way the powerless keep their dignity, can be resistance, and sometimes means defeat. His Chinese editor pitched him to readers who had moved to the big cities as "the most familiar stranger."

Where to start

Gurnah's own advice: begin with Paradise and Afterlives, or with By the Sea. Paradise has the simplest through-line; By the Sea withholds who the protagonist is and asks you to piece together a feud across generations, so it is a little harder. Three Academy members picked favourites: Anne Swärd chose By the Sea, Ellen Mattson Desertion, Anders Olsson Afterlives. All ten novels have since come out in Chinese, and in 2022 Paradise appeared in Swahili as Peponi, translated by the SOAS scholar Ida Hadjivayanis, who had started before the prize.

What it does not mean

It is not indictment literature. Colonisers, merchants and the colonised all carry their own guilt. It is not a report on the "refugee issue." He has said his books are not out to describe the phenomenon of migration; he writes what he knows. And it is not nostalgia. His coast has a history of slavery, and the harbour he remembers had children who vanished at the season's end.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · log compaction: to save space, a system collapses a long event log into a snapshot of current state, and the conflicting, overwritten writes vanish. Gurnah's "simpler history" is that snapshot, compacted by the winners. His novels run the operation in reverse: recover the raw log, let the same house have one version in each family, keep the versions side by side instead of rushing a merge.
  • AI · absence in the training data: Mengiste asks how we remember when one consequence of colonialism is "the deliberate exclusion of an African perspective from the archives." A model trained on the archive inherits the gap; no amount of inference recovers a voice that was never recorded. Adding the missing data is what Gurnah has been doing all along.
  • Economics · the long tail of colonial institutions: the 2024 economics prize showed with cross-country data that what colonisers built, and where, shaped prosperity centuries later. Gurnah's lecture says the same from the other end: postcolonial corruption and misrule were partly colonial legacy. One side is a regression; the other is a family's dinner table.
  • Literature · emigrants outward and "internal migrants": Annie Ernaux left her class through schooling and called herself an internal migrant; Gurnah crossed continents. Writing back toward where they came from, both had to refuse a more respectable, ready-made way of telling it: for Ernaux, "writing well"; for Gurnah, the simpler history.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Abdulrazak Gurnah was born in Zanzibar in 1948 and fled to England at eighteen after the 1964 revolution brought persecution of Arab-origin citizens.
  2. Zanzibar, ruled in turn by Portuguese, Arab, German and British powers, was a cosmopolitan port society long before globalisation.
  3. Swahili is Gurnah's first language, but with almost no Swahili literature to hand, English became his tool as a writer.
  4. In his Nobel lecture Gurnah refuses a "new, simpler history" built to fit a familiar story of emancipation and progress.
  5. Gurnah equally refuses nostalgia for a pristine pre-colonial Africa; merchants, colonisers and colonised all carry guilt.
  6. The refugees in Gurnah's novels protect themselves with silence and lies, and over time the shield becomes a tunic they cannot take off.
  7. Paradise retells the Quran's Joseph story but turns the happy ending into a boy joining the German army he despised.
  8. By the Sea uses two narrators from feuding families so that neither keeps the authority to tell the truth.
  9. Gurnah suggests starting with Paradise and Afterlives, or with By the Sea; all ten novels now exist in Chinese.
  10. A "simpler history" works like log compaction in a distributed system; the novel's job is to recover the overwritten records.

// Think further

Writing the colonised in the coloniser's language: enrichment or assimilation?
Lu Jiande asked it outright at the Chinese launch: does writing English this well enrich you or enslave you? Ngũgĩ went back to an African language; Gurnah says Swahili literature was barely within reach. Maybe the question is less the language than whose story it carries and to whom. The jargon of your own field: does it help you say things, or decide for you what is worth saying?
How does a lie turn from shield into poison?
Mattson treats the characters' lies as fiction: first a kinder version to protect yourself and the people you love, then a version that traps the teller. The narrator of Admiring Silence hides his English family from Zanzibar and his past from England at the same time. How many versions of "your story" do you tell different audiences, and when did they start to conflict?
Can a place that drove someone out claim him back?
The Tanzanian debate was sharp: the state his family fled now takes pride in him. He says, "In my mind I live there." Is belonging decided by passport, by memory, or by whether home is willing to acknowledge what happened? There is no neat answer, and it is the same "gulf" the citation names.
