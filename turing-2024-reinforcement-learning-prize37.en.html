TURING AWARD · 2024

The thrill always arrives before the reward

A professor and his student taught machines to learn from nothing but a score, and the learning rule they wrote down later turned up, almost line for line, in the dopamine neurons of monkeys

Andrew G. BartoUniversity of Massachusetts Amherst · sharedRichard S. SuttonUniversity of Alberta · Amii · shared
For developing the conceptual and algorithmic foundations of reinforcement learning
2026-11-18 · BigCat

Dopamine neurons don't fire for good things. They fire for things that are better than expected, and that rule was written into an algorithm before anyone saw it in a brain.

You order takeaway, and a notification pops up: "Your driver has picked up your order." The little lift you feel at that moment is real, and it's sharper than what you feel twenty minutes later when the bag is in your hands. When the food does arrive, you mostly just open it. But if the estimated time passes and nobody knocks, a small drop in your stomach shows up right on schedule, even though nothing has actually happened.

Those three feelings line up with three firing patterns recorded in the 1990s from dopamine neurons in monkeys' midbrains, in a lab in Switzerland. Stranger still, the same shape had appeared a few years earlier in a piece of mathematics worked out in Amherst, Massachusetts. Its authors were a mathematician who wanted to model the brain with computers and a student who had studied psychology as an undergraduate. All they had set out to build was a machine that wanted something.

A machine that wants something, forgotten for twenty years

Learning by trial and error is one of the oldest ideas in artificial intelligence. In 1911 the psychologist Edward Thorndike stated his Law of Effect: actions followed by satisfaction get strengthened. In a 1948 report, Alan Turing sketched a "pleasure-pain system":

When a pain stimulus occurs all tentative entries are cancelled, and when a pleasure stimulus occurs they are all made permanent.— Alan Turing · 1948 · quoted in Sutton & Barto's textbook

But through the 1960s and 70s the thread nearly snapped. As the two laureates tell it in their textbook, neural-network pioneers like Frank Rosenblatt and Bernard Widrow spoke the language of reward and punishment while actually building supervised learning systems, where every example comes with the right answer attached. Real trial-and-error learning has no answer key, only a verdict after the fact.

A verdict without an answer raises the problem of credit assignment. Marvin Minsky named it in 1961: you won the game, but which of your forty moves deserves the credit? The later the reward, the harder the question. The other tradition, Richard Bellman's dynamic programming from the 1950s, could compute optimal decisions exactly, provided you already knew every rule of the world, and its cost explodes as the number of variables grows.

The person who brought Barto and Sutton together was Harry Klopf, a scientist trained in neurophysiology and attached to the Air Force at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio. He was unhappy with the idea that intelligence is mostly about staying in balance; he thought brains were maximizing something, and he found Air Force money for a project at UMass Amherst to test the idea. Barto joined as a postdoc in 1977. Sutton, then a Stanford undergraduate who had been writing to Klopf about the timing of stimuli in classical conditioning, was recommended to the project as a graduate student. In late 1979 the two realized that the simplest idea in the pile had barely been studied computationally at all: a learning system that wants something, and adapts its behaviour to maximize a special signal from its environment. As Barto later told the Associated Press:

We were kind of in the wilderness.— Andrew Barto · AP interview · 2025

You don't have to wait for Saturday to know Monday's forecast was off

In 1983 Barto, Sutton and Charles Anderson gave a machine a task: keep a pole upright on a moving cart. The only feedback was a single "failure" when the pole fell or the cart hit the end of the track; the rest of the time, silence. Their solution split the job between two roles. An actor pushes the cart. A critic watches and keeps predicting how far away failure is. When the critic's outlook suddenly gets worse, the actor knows that last push was a bad one, without waiting for the pole to drop.

Sutton pulled the critic's learning method out on its own in a 1988 paper, written while he was at GTE Laboratories, and called it temporal-difference (TD) learning. His example is homely. A forecaster predicts every day whether it will rain this Saturday. On Monday the forecast is 50%; on Tuesday it's 75%. The conventional approach waits for Saturday, then corrects the whole week at once. TD corrects Monday as soon as Tuesday arrives, because Tuesday's forecast is itself a test of Monday's.

It sounds like using one guess to fix another, but Sutton proved that in important special cases this converges to the right answer, using less memory and learning faster than waiting for the outcome. So a late reward stops being a problem. Each step only has to reconcile with the next, and credit seeps backwards one step at a time until it reaches the early moment that really mattered. Sutton also argued that most problems then treated as supervised learning were really prediction problems of exactly this kind.

A drop of apple juice, and a light that comes on first

Meanwhile, at the University of Fribourg in Switzerland, Wolfram Schultz was recording dopamine neurons in monkeys. Losing these neurons causes Parkinson's disease, so people expected them to be about movement, yet their link to movement turned out to be weak. In one 1992 series, a monkey saw a light come on, pressed a lever, and got a drop of apple juice.

At first, many dopamine neurons fired when the juice arrived. With training, that response faded and the burst moved to the moment the light came on. Add a second cue one second earlier, and the burst moved forward again, to that earlier cue. The subtlest case was when a monkey pressed the wrong lever and no juice came. Nothing in the room marked the moment juice would normally have arrived, yet right at that moment the neurons' firing dipped below baseline.

Anyone who knew reinforcement learning recognized the shape at once: it is the TD error. An unexpected reward gives a positive error; a well-predicted reward gives zero, and the signal migrates to the earliest predictor; a broken prediction gives a negative one. In 1997 Schultz, Peter Dayan and Read Montague set this out in Science as "a neural substrate of prediction and reward". Sutton and Barto's own word for the match is "uncanny".

This is where the award crosses into another field. A rule designed for machines became a central hypothesis about how brains learn. That the road started from the brain and came back to it is no accident: Barto was a core faculty member of UMass's Neuroscience and Behavior program.

From backgammon to chatbots

In 1989 Chris Watkins introduced Q-learning, which fully joined TD to dynamic programming. In 1992 Gerald Tesauro at IBM had a neural network play backgammon against itself and learn by TD. It started with essentially no backgammon knowledge; after about 300,000 games against itself it matched the best existing programs, including one Tesauro had trained on expert moves. Later versions came close to the world's best players, and top players changed how they played certain openings to match it.

In 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol four games to one; one of its leads, David Silver, was Sutton's doctoral student. Its successor AlphaGo Zero used no human games at all, playing about 4.9 million games against itself in roughly three days. In 2019 Sutton wrote a short essay, "The Bitter Lesson": seventy years of AI research show that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin.

The step closest to everyday life came in 2022. OpenAI fine-tuned GPT-3 with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF): labelers ranked the model's answers, and those rankings became the score it trained against. The resulting 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT was preferred over the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3. In 2025 DeepSeek reported in Nature that pure reinforcement learning, with no human-written worked solutions, could make a large model develop habits like checking itself and switching strategies.

◐ Off the podium

Both editions of the textbook are dedicated to Harry Klopf, who introduced the authors and whose ideas started it all. The book also notes that in 1981 they found a 1977 paper by Ian Witten, apparently the earliest published TD learning rule, first submitted in 1974. And in their joint AP interview the two didn't always agree: Sutton dismissed fears of AI threatening humanity as overblown, while Barto said, "You have to be cognizant of potential unexpected consequences."

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Part of why chatbots answer the way they do comes from RLHF. The US National Science Foundation's tribute also lists chip layout, deciding where items sit in a supply chain, recommendations on Netflix and YouTube, and robots learning intricate motions by trial and error. A quieter layer is in your own head: "prediction errors drive learning" is now a standard frame for thinking about habits and addiction. A 2004 model by A. David Redish used it to explain part of addiction: the dopamine surge from cocaine can't be "predicted away", so the value of the behaviour around it keeps climbing. The textbook is careful to add that this covers only some features of addiction.

What may change next

Already shipping: "reasoning models", large models trained further with reinforcement learning on maths and coding tasks whose answers can be checked automatically. Still in the lab: the direction Sutton is betting on, agents that keep learning from their own experience instead of relying on one big round of pretraining, laid out with colleagues in the "Alberta Plan". As he told the AP: "The big choice is, do you try to learn from people's data, or do you try to learn from an (AI) agent's own life and its own experience?"

What it does not mean

"Dopamine is the pleasure molecule" is wrong. On Schultz's data it looks more like a surprise signal, "how much better than expected", and a fully predicted treat barely moves it. The hypothesis itself is also contested: in 2022 Huijeong Jeong and colleagues argued in Science that dopamine carries causal associations rather than prediction errors, and Harvard's Naoshige Uchida replied that the new model can't yet replace TD. "Reinforcement learning lets machines figure things out like people" is also wrong. It maximizes whatever score you give it. DeepMind has collected around 60 examples of agents gaming their specification; in one boat-racing game, the agent found that circling and hitting the same bonus blocks over and over scored more than finishing the race.

A way of thinking to borrow

Don't wait for the ending to review. Whenever your judgment about something shifts, the shift is itself the signal: use today's updated estimate to correct yesterday's.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · distance-vector routing: each router asks its neighbours "how far are you from the destination?", adds one hop, and updates its own estimate; nobody holds a map of the whole network. That is the same Bellman-style bootstrapping as TD updating this step's estimate from the next step's. Bad news travels slowly in both: routers "count to infinity", and TD values seep backwards one step at a time.
  • Investing · pricing only the surprise: a stock moves on whether earnings beat expectations, not on whether they are good; good news everyone expected is already priced in. Same structure as the fully predicted apple juice that no longer makes dopamine neurons fire: the signal carries only "actual minus expected".
  • Buddhism · craving and hedonic adaptation: satisfaction tracks expectation rather than possession; getting what you expected leaves hardly a trace, while a broken expectation stings right on time. The TD data draw a curve for this old observation. Where the analogy breaks: TD aims to make predictions accurate, not to let go of them.
  • This site · the 2024 Physics prize: Hopfield and Hinton's neural networks answer "how do you learn patterns from examples?"; reinforcement learning answers "how do you learn to act with no answer key?". TD-Gammon and AlphaGo are both hybrids: the network remembers and generalizes, and the TD error tells it which way to change.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Reinforcement learning gives a machine only a score, good or bad, never the right answer, which is what separates it from supervised learning.
  2. The later a reward comes, the harder it is to tell which step earned it: the credit-assignment problem Minsky named in 1961.
  3. Air Force scientist Harry Klopf argued intelligence is about maximizing, not staying in balance, and brought Barto and Sutton together at UMass.
  4. In the 1983 pole-balancing work, a critic predicts how far off failure is and an actor adjusts on that basis, without waiting for the pole to fall.
  5. Sutton's 1988 temporal-difference learning uses the next moment's prediction to correct this moment's, instead of waiting for the final outcome.
  6. Schultz found that once monkeys learned, dopamine bursts moved from the juice to the light predicting it, and dipped below baseline when juice failed to come.
  7. The three dopamine patterns match the positive, zero and negative TD error, turning an engineering rule into a theory of how brains learn.
  8. TD-Gammon matched the best programs after about 300,000 self-play games with almost no human knowledge; AlphaGo Zero used no human games at all.
  9. Dopamine is not a pleasure molecule but closer to a surprise signal, and reinforcement learning maximizes the score it is given, loopholes included.
  10. Distance-vector routing and TD learning share one Bellman-style update: each refines its own estimate from a neighbour's or the next step's.

// Think further

TD uses one guess to correct another. Why doesn't it just drift further and further from the truth?
Because the end of the chain is a real reward. Every reconciliation passes back a little information that is closer to the truth, and it seeps backwards step by step. Sutton proved convergence only for special cases; combine bootstrapping with complex function approximation and it can genuinely diverge, which later algorithms have had to handle explicitly. Which distributed protocols also reach eventual consistency with no global truth, only a fact anchored at one end?
If dopamine fires only for "better than expected", is lasting happiness mechanically impossible?
A prediction error describes a learning signal, which need not be the whole of subjective experience. But the mechanism does explain why the same good thing is less exciting the second time. The question worth sitting with: if every reward eventually gets predicted away, what keeps a system exploring? Should curiosity and novelty be designed into reinforcement learning as rewards of their own?
Sutton wants agents that learn from their own experience; today's large models mostly learn from people's data. Are these two paths opposed, or sequential?
InstructGPT and DeepSeek-R1 both lay a foundation on human data and then push further with reinforcement learning. Sutton is betting the second will eventually swallow the first, an extension of "The Bitter Lesson". The counter-argument is that trial and error in the real world is slow and expensive, and some mistakes can't be made even once. Where would you draw the line between what can be learned from data and what only from experience?
