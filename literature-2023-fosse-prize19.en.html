LITERATURE · 2023

The word he used most in his plays was "pause"

A playwright and novelist writing in Norway's minority written language, who puts what people cannot say into pauses, repetitions and sentences that never reach a full stop

Jon FosseNorway · 1/1
for his innovative plays and prose which give voice to the unsayable
2026-10-31 · BigCat

He says he has never written to express himself. He writes to get away from himself.

The teacher calls your name and asks you to stand up and read the passage aloud. Every face in the room turns toward you, your throat tightens, the words start to swim. Most people know some version of that moment.

A junior-high boy on Norway's west coast got the extreme version. In his Nobel lecture at the Swedish Academy on 7 December 2023, he remembered how fear arrived without warning: "It was like I disappeared into the fear and it was all I was." He stood up and ran out of the classroom, then claimed he had needed the toilet. He could see nobody believed him.

The boy was Jon Fosse. The fear, he said, seemed to take his language from him, and he had to take it back, on his own terms rather than other people's. He began writing short poems and stories and found that writing gave him the opposite of fear. Fifty years on, he says he still writes from that "secret place inside me", a place he knows little about except that it exists.

A guitarist, and a language written by one Norwegian in ten

Fosse was born in 1959 in Haugesund on the Norwegian west coast. As a teenager he lived for music: electric guitar in a band that played local dances when he was fourteen, though you had to be sixteen to get in, and enough classical guitar to manage small pieces of Bach. Then, "for reasons hard to explain", he stopped playing and even stopped listening, sat down at an old typewriter, and tried to recreate the mood he had felt while playing. The repetitions he is "famous or ill-famous for", he says, probably came from there.

He writes in Nynorsk, one of Norway's two written standards, the one closest to the dialects of the west. By his estimate only about 10% of Norwegians write it. He can hear his grandmother's voice in Nynorsk, he says; in Bokmål, the majority standard, she "sounds as a quite different person". When a minority language is attacked, he told an interviewer as a student, you cannot argue for it, any more than you can argue for your own existence: "Fight or leave." He is the first Nobel laureate in Literature to write in Nynorsk.

His debut novel, Raudt, svart (Red, Black, 1983), dealt with suicide and was poorly reviewed. Had he listened to the critics, he said in his lecture, he would have stopped after that first book.

Unpacking the citation: plays, prose, the unsayable

"Innovative plays." He did not want to write for the theatre. A publicly funded scheme to encourage new Norwegian drama offered what was, for "a poor author", a good sum to write the opening scene of a play. He wrote the whole thing: Someone Is Going to Come (1996), still his most performed play. Its 1999 Paris production became his European breakthrough. Writing it was the biggest surprise of his writing life. In prose and poetry he had been trying to say what ordinary speech cannot. On stage, "all I had to do was to write the word pause, and the silent speech was there." Long pause, short pause, or just pause: it is the most-used word in his plays.

"Prose." In the novels, repetition does the work that pauses do on stage. Either way, he says, there is a silent language behind the written one. The committee's phrase is that his simplicity "gains depth and intensity through repetition and variation."

"The unsayable." Fosse thought the citation made complete sense: if something can be said simply, why write a poem or a novel? He likes to twist a line from Jacques Derrida: the most important thing in life cannot be said, only written. In the presentation speech, committee chair Anders Olsson pointed to an early prose piece, I Couldn't Say It to You (1991). An old man cannot shake the memory of his beloved's eyes as she sat alone at a table in the school cafeteria; even at her deathbed, the thing goes unsaid. Fosse, Olsson added, avoids definitive wording, "making it almost impossible to quote him."

Olsson's biobibliography quotes two lines from the play Skuggar (Shadows, 2007), which show how the language works:

"No, of course / That's just what you say when you meet someone"— Skuggar · as quoted in the Nobel biobibliography

The same small courtesy comes back again and again, and each time it sounds less sure. Is it politeness, or is it what the speaker really means?

What happens in the books: two crossing lines, one sentence with no end

Stengd gitar (Closed Guitar, 1985) is the easiest way in. A young mother steps out to throw rubbish down the chute and locks herself out, with her baby still inside. She has to go for help, and she cannot leave her child. Olsson's point is that Fosse's situations are ones we recognise instantly from our own lives: the critical moment of irresolution.

His magnum opus in prose is Septology (2019–2021), 1,250 pages by the committee's count. The narrator, Asle, is an ageing painter, a widower living alone on the west coast. The novel opens with him at his easel, looking at the painting he cannot finish and loves most:

In the excerpt published by the Nobel Prize, he switches off the light and keeps looking at it in the dark:

"I see that there's a soft light in the painting, yes, a soft invisible light, well then yes so it probably is a good painting, maybe, I think"— The Other Name: Septology I–II · tr. Damion Searls · Nobel Prize excerpt

Notice that the sentence does not end. The whole novel has no full stops at all. It is held together by repetition, recurring motifs and a fixed span of seven days; each part opens with the same phrase and closes with the same prayer. In the nearby town lives another Asle, also a painter, also named Asle, and an alcoholic. In the first part, the narrator searches the town for him and finds him lying in a snowdrift, saving his life. Fosse called writing that scene one of his happiest moments as a writer. In his lecture he also gave the game away: the silent language of the book says that the first Asle and the other Asle "may well be the same person", and that the whole novel may be "a written expression of one extracted now."

The missing full stops were not a device, he says: "I just wrote the novel like that, in one flow, one movement that didn't demand a full stop." He never plans. He proceeds by listening, and at some point he always feels the text already exists somewhere out there, and he only has to get it down before it disappears. He wrote Septology in Hainburg, a small town near Vienna, from five to nine every morning, over some four years.

Why him, and why that year

The wait was long. His Chinese translator, Li Shubo, told the Beijing News that, according to his Norwegian publisher's editor-in-chief, Fosse had been on the shortlist since 2001. Fosse himself said on the prize call that he was used to being high on the betting lists, and had not expected to win "the tenth year I was into the speculations." The Irish Times noted that Ladbrokes once suspended betting on him altogether.

In January 2020, as the first English volume of Septology appeared, the Irish Times reviewer John Self wrote that Fosse had the European modernist sensibility the Swedish Academy had "rediscovered its appetite for recently" and told readers to "watch this space". When the call came, Fosse was driving alone along a fjord. The timing was right, he told the Irish Times: a decade earlier, the prize would have made it hard to write a book like Septology, which he expected people to hate.

◐ OFFSTAGE

He was frank with the Irish Times. He loved to drink, Jameson in particular, "the favourite of Beckett too". Shy and private, he drank to manage everything: "I covered myself in whiskey." He began drinking in the morning and stopped eating, until one day he collapsed and was hospitalised. Carrying on, he said, "would have meant my death, for sure." About ten years before the prize he gave up alcohol and converted to Catholicism. Before that he had taken part in Quaker meetings in Bergen, and he still thinks like a Quaker in one respect: that you hear God closest in the silences.

◎ WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE REST OF US

What reading him can do

Permission not to say it. We tend to assume that if you can't say something clearly, you haven't thought it through. Fosse's plays show how much a pause can carry: that something cannot be said, that it doesn't want to be said, or that it is best said by saying nothing. After reading him, you may hear more in an awkward silence or a quick "I'm fine".

Reading close to meditation. Olsson put it plainly: Fosse "is not a difficult writer". The difficulty is "our readiness to open ourselves to the existential uncertainty upon which he constantly touches." The long sentences are not hard. Slowing down is.

A note on suicide. There are many suicides in his work, he admitted in his lecture, and he has feared that he helped legitimise it. What touched him most after the prize were readers who wrote that his work had saved their lives; a Greek woman told him that the play Death Variations was the reason she was still alive. If you are in a dark place yourself, talk to someone. A book is not a substitute for help.

Where to start

On the prize call, Fosse himself suggested Morning and Evening (2000), one of his favourites and "rather short": one morning the old man Johannes finds that his sense of reality is dissolving, and the reader understands he is dying, yet the tone is one of reconciliation. Damion Searls's translation is the English edition. For the plays, the Oberon Plays volumes collect the major works, with nameless characters called simply Man and Woman; Someone Is Going to Come reads quickly. Trilogy (tr. May-Brit Akerholt), about a poor young couple in western Norway, has the most plot. Septology (tr. Searls) is the summit, best attempted once the rhythm feels familiar.

What it does not mean

Minimal does not mean empty. Knausgård has even said Fosse is not a minimalist at all but an essentialist. He is not preaching either. His faith runs through the work, but in his lecture he set good writing against "all kinds of preaching", religious, political or otherwise. And this is not autobiography. Septology's painter gives up drink and finds God, like his author, yet Fosse insists he writes to get away from himself; he once told a student that you cannot write directly about your own life.

🌀 CROSSING OVER · CONNECTIONS ACROSS FIELDS

  • Distributed systems · slow, or dead? In The Name (1995) a pregnant girl waits for the father of her child, who is late. Delayed, or gone for good? This is the basic problem of asynchronous systems: when the heartbeats stop, you can never be sure whether the other node is slow or has crashed. Someone Is Going to Come hangs on exactly this timeout. Engineers resolve it with a threshold; Fosse's characters have none, so they stay suspended.
  • AI · cliché as the most probable next word. Teaching a writing class, Fosse took a poem by his student Karl Ove Knausgård, later famous for autofiction, and struck it out line by line as cliché, keeping only the word "widescreen-sky". A cliché is the continuation a language model predicts most readily. "Love", Fosse says, says almost nothing in English. The unsayable he chases is the part of meaning that won't compress into a familiar phrase. Where the analogy breaks: low probability is not meaning, and random words are rare too.
  • Mysticism · saying by not saying. Olsson compared Septology's Asle to the medieval mystic Meister Eckhart, praying to a God beyond all concepts. Apophatic theology, which speaks of God only by saying what God is not, has the same structure as the Daoist line that the Way that can be named is not the eternal Way, and Zen's refusal to rest on words: the most important thing can only be circled. Fosse's language is full of pauses, interruptions and negations; Aliss at the Fire (2004) packs 200 questions into 70 pages.
  • Neuroscience · entrainment. Reviewers describe his repetition as having "the hypnotic feeling of a mantra"; the novelist Rónán Hession hears "a steady reading beat coded into his words, by which the thought processes of reader and character synchronise." Fosse traces the repetition to playing guitar. It is the same mechanism as rhythmic entrainment in music, where a repeated beat pulls the listener's internal timing into line with it.
  • Literature · sentences without end. Krasznahorkai, the laureate two years later, is also known for sentences that run for pages. They are built for different things: his feel chased by the apocalypse, while Fosse's feel like prayer and slow down as they go.

◆ TEN TAKEAWAYS

  1. Terrified of reading aloud, the teenage Fosse ran out of class, then used writing to take back the language that fear had taken.
  2. Fosse writes in Nynorsk, the written Norwegian closest to western dialects, used by only about one Norwegian in ten.
  3. Fosse was the first Nobel laureate in Literature to write in Nynorsk, and received the whole 2023 prize alone.
  4. The famous repetitions came from guitar playing; after quitting music, he sought the same mood at a typewriter.
  5. Paid to write the opening scene of a play, he wrote all of Someone Is Going to Come, still his most performed work.
  6. "Pause" is the most-used word in his plays: a pause can hold what cannot be said, what won't be said, and what needn't be.
  7. Septology contains no full stops, and Asle the painter and the drinking other Asle may be the same person.
  8. The committee calls Fosse an easy writer; the hard part is opening yourself to existential uncertainty.
  9. Start with Morning and Evening, the short novel Fosse himself recommended.
  10. Waiting for someone who may be delayed or may never come is the same problem as telling a slow node from a crashed one.

// THINKING FURTHER

How does a writer who is "almost impossible to quote" win the Nobel?
We usually remember writers by their lines. Olsson says Fosse avoids definitive wording, and Fosse says meaning comes from the totality, not the parts. Perhaps his value was never in single sentences but in the rhythm between them. How do you judge work like that? When you remember a song, is it the lyrics or the melody?
He writes to get away from himself, yet Septology's painter is so much like him. A contradiction?
Fosse says writing directly what he has lived "becomes nothing"; it needs transformation. The other Asle reads like a counterfactual: what he might have become without sobriety and faith. Is writing a possible self a way toward yourself or away from it? Have you ever understood yourself better through a "what if"?
Is a minority language a moat or a wall?
Only about one in ten Norwegians writes Nynorsk, and Fosse says that binds you to it: no arguing, fight or leave. Yet he reached the world through translators who could carry his "music" into other languages. Where do staying with your dialect and being read everywhere conflict, and where don't they?
His work contains many suicides. Is the author responsible?
Fosse feared he might be legitimising suicide; readers told him his plays saved their lives. The same text can point in opposite directions for different people. Where is the line between depicting and encouraging? How would you judge whether a book is a danger or a shelter for a particular person?
