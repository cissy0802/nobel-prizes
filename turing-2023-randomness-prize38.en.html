TURING AWARD · 2023

Randomness lives in the eye of the beholder

A theorist showed that if truly hard problems exist, flipping coins cannot make computers fundamentally stronger, and that you can prove you know a secret without giving away a single bit of it

Avi WigdersonInstitute for Advanced Study · sole
For foundational contributions to the theory of computation, including reshaping our understanding of the role of randomness in computation and mathematics, and for his decades of intellectual leadership in theoretical computer science
2026-11-19 · BigCat

The same string of digits can be random to you and transparent to someone with unlimited computing power. Randomness belongs to the observer.

Before kickoff, the referee tosses a coin to decide who starts. Nobody calls it unfair, because "nobody knows how it will land." Yet a coin is not mysterious at all: the force of the flick, the spin, the height above the ground all obey Newton. As Wigderson told Quanta after the award, if you have enough information about the physical system, the outcome is entirely predictable.

So what makes it random? The answer hides in a phrase that is easy to skim past: unpredictable to you. You can't measure fast enough, or compute fast enough. Over several decades, this theorist from Haifa turned that near-truism into rigorous mathematics, and drew two counterintuitive conclusions from it. Coin flips most likely do not make computers more powerful. And you can convince someone you hold a proof while revealing nothing about it.

Coin-flipping algorithms that were suspiciously fast

In 1977, Robert Solovay and Volker Strassen published a randomized test for whether a large number is prime, faster than the best deterministic algorithms of the day. The price was a chance of error, but that chance can be crushed: as Wigderson explains in his book Mathematics and Computation, running an algorithm that errs at most a third of the time several times independently and taking a majority vote drives the error down exponentially.

A more tangible example: someone conjectures a long polynomial identity. Expanding and comparing coefficients takes exponential time. The randomized shortcut is to plug in a few random numbers. If the polynomial is not identically zero, random inputs almost never happen to land on a zero. To this day, no subexponential deterministic algorithm is known for this problem.

The book lists more such cases, like finding a prime between x and 2x or approximating the volume of a high-dimensional convex body: fast with coins, exponential without. Which raises the basic question of whether randomness adds real power to computation, known as BPP versus P. At first the answer looked like yes. Wigderson recalled the confusion to Quanta:

Initially, it was not clear if this was only our own stupidity, that we cannot remove randomness.— Avi Wigderson · Quanta Magazine · 2024

Boiling a hard problem down into random bits

The lead came from cryptography. In the early 1980s Shamir, Blum and Micali, and Andrew Yao showed in relay that if one-way functions exist, functions easy to compute but very hard to invert, then a few truly random bits can be stretched into a long string no efficient algorithm can tell apart from true randomness: a pseudorandom generator. But a one-way function is a strong assumption, stronger than P≠NP.

Wigderson and Noam Nisan's paper "Hardness vs. Randomness" (journal version 1994, the work done while Nisan was a student at Berkeley) changed the angle. Cryptography needs the generator to run faster than its adversary, because it must hold off government-scale computers from your laptop. But if the goal is only to make a randomized algorithm deterministic, that requirement is pointless. The generator may be slower, as long as its output fools that one algorithm.

The construction is startlingly plain. Take a hard function and apply it to many subsets of the seed, one output bit per subset, with the subsets overlapping only a little. If the function is hard enough that small circuits can't even approximate it, the output fools every small circuit. With a seed only logarithmically long, there are only polynomially many seeds; try them all, take a vote, and the randomized algorithm has become deterministic. The abstract says it plainly: the construction reveals an equivalence between proving lower bounds and generating good pseudorandom sequences.

In 1997, with Russell Impagliazzo, he pushed the assumption to its most natural form: if some problem solvable in exponential time requires exponential-size circuits, then BPP = P. The book flags the oddest feature of such results: one task being hard implies another is easy. Two appealing beliefs, "some problems really are hard" and "randomness is powerful," now contradict each other; most experts, the book says, reluctantly dropped the second. Wigderson's version to Quanta: "You can always eliminate randomness."

Randomness belongs to the observer

What this line of work really rewrote was the word "random." In probability, statistical physics, information theory, chaos, Kolmogorov complexity, Wigderson writes, randomness was always an objective property of the phenomenon. In computational pseudorandomness it is subjective, a property of the observer. The same distribution can look random to a computationally bounded observer and not random to an unbounded one. Harvard's Madhu Sudan, a former collaborator, told Quanta:

It's not just randomness but perceptions of randomness. That's the key.— Madhu Sudan · Quanta Magazine · 2024

The flip side of the same idea is his best-known work, though the citation does not name it. In 1985 Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff introduced zero-knowledge proofs: convincing a verifier that something is true while revealing nothing else. Until then these existed only for a few number-theoretic statements. In 1986 Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson proved that if one-way functions exist, every NP statement has a zero-knowledge proof.

Their paper explains it with a supply of locked boxes. The prover holds a legal three-colouring of a map, neighbouring countries always in different colours. Each round he randomly permutes the three colours, locks each country's colour in a numbered box, and hands them all over. The verifier picks any border; the prover hands over just those two keys; the two colours inside differ. Repeat as many rounds as the square of the number of borders, and a cheater is almost certain to be caught. Because the colours are reshuffled every round, all the verifier ever sees is "two different colours," a conversation he could have invented alone. Through NP-completeness, the book notes, even "I have proved the Riemann Hypothesis" can be translated into such a map and proved this way.

What grew from it

"Find a randomized algorithm, then remove the randomness" became a design pattern. The most famous case came on 6 August 2002, when Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal and Nitin Saxena at IIT Kanpur posted "PRIMES is in P," the first unconditional deterministic polynomial-time primality test. The paper states that the prime identity it rests on was the basis of a randomized algorithm by Agrawal and Biswas in 1999; it appeared in the Annals of Mathematics in 2004.

Zero knowledge, meanwhile, left the journals. On 3 July 2025 Google open-sourced its zero-knowledge libraries for age assurance: proving to a website that you are over 18 while sharing nothing else. Google's announcement notes that the EU's eIDAS regulation, taking effect in 2026, encourages member states to build such technology into the European Digital Identity Wallet.

The most unexpected branch leads into quantum physics. Physicists often ask Wigderson why bother, when quantum mechanics produces perfect random bits. His answer in the book: "Even if I do trust the theory, why should I trust the devices?" In his 2006 PhD thesis Roger Colbeck observed that if two non-communicating boxes repeatedly play the CHSH game, classical strategies win at most 75% of the time; if they consistently win more, their output must contain genuine randomness, even if your enemy built the boxes.

◐ Off the podium

Wigderson's parents were both Holocaust survivors; his father, an electrical engineer, loved puzzles and shared them with his sons. "He's the guy from whom I was infected by this virus." He is the only person to have won both the Abel Prize (2021, shared with László Lovász) and the Turing Award, and he has mentored more than 100 postdocs. The concept of zero knowledge belongs to Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff; the first two already received the 2012 Turing Award for that line of work. Collaborators such as Nisan and Impagliazzo are not on this year's citation.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Every https page you open makes both ends generate fresh random numbers for keys. A computer is deterministic, so it slowly gathers a little unpredictability from its surroundings and stretches it with a cryptographically secure pseudorandom generator. Cloudflare's explainer is concrete: the lowest digits of a high-precision CPU temperature reading, the exact timing of keystrokes, and a wall of lava lamps in its San Francisco lobby, filmed by a camera whose feed is fed into a generator as a backup source. "A short seed plus computational hardness yields a long string nobody can tell apart" is the core of this hardness-for-randomness line of theory.

Zero knowledge is the second. Google's 2025 open-source age-assurance library and the EU's digital-identity plans are turning "prove a fact without handing over the data" into an everyday step.

What may change next

Zero-knowledge proofs are already in use; Princeton's announcement lists privacy, compliance, identity verification and blockchain. Whether they become the default for digital identity depends on rollouts after 2026. Still in the lab is Colbeck's certified quantum randomness: confirming that randomness exists through statistics alone, without trusting the device.

What it does not mean

"BPP = P has been proved" is wrong. Every result is conditional on some problems being truly hard, exactly the lower bounds nobody can prove. Kabanets and Impagliazzo even showed that derandomizing just the "plug in random numbers" identity test would yield circuit lower bounds that remain unproven. "Randomized algorithms are now useless" is also wrong. The book's example: the best randomized primality test runs in under n cubed time, the best deterministic one needs at least n to the fifth; cryptography and distributed computing cannot work without randomness. "Using zero knowledge makes a system private" is wrong too. It only guarantees the proof step leaks no secret; how data is stored and who sees the transaction records are separate questions.

A way of thinking to borrow

Whenever you call something "random," "secure" or "patternless," add the missing clause: to whom, with how much computing power.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • AI · the GAN discriminator: a GAN defines "realistic" as "the discriminator can't tell," and pseudorandomness defines "random" as "this class of circuits can't tell." Both define a quality through a bounded observer, and a stronger observer moves the bar. The difference: pseudorandomness must fool a whole class of circuits, not one discriminator.
  • Distributed systems · Byzantine fault tolerance: the GMW "compiler" turns a protocol that is private only if everyone follows it into one that stays private even when some parties cheat maliciously. Each party attaches a zero-knowledge proof to every message showing it was computed correctly from its own secrets. It is the BFT instinct of making messages verifiable instead of trusting senders: replace trust in people with checks on every step.
  • Quantum · Bell inequalities: the experiments behind the 2022 physics prize showed entangled particles beat any classical strategy at the CHSH game; Colbeck flipped the same gap into a certificate of randomness. Quantum key distribution in the 2025 Turing Award works the same way: security comes from physical law rather than computational difficulty. This page relies on "too hard to compute," those two on "physics forbids it."
  • Philosophy · Bayesian probability and Laplace's demon: Bayesians hold that probability is an agent's degree of belief, not a property of the world. Computational pseudorandomness goes a step further: even with complete information, a world looks random to anyone whose computing power is bounded.
  • Mathematics · structure and pseudorandomness in the primes: citing Terence Tao's survey, the book traces Szemerédi's theorem through the Green–Tao theorem on progressions in the primes to one decomposition: any object splits into a simple part plus a remainder that looks random to the tests. Sudan points out that the primes and the digits of pi are completely determined; they only look random to us.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. A tossed coin obeys mechanics and is predictable given enough information; its randomness comes from the observer's inability to compute.
  2. Primality testing and polynomial identity testing had fast randomized algorithms while deterministic ones long needed exponential time.
  3. The error of a randomized algorithm shrinks exponentially by running it independently several times and taking a majority vote.
  4. A pseudorandom generator stretches a few truly random bits into a long string no efficient algorithm can distinguish from randomness.
  5. Nisan and Wigderson saw that a generator for derandomization may be slower than the algorithm it fools, so any hard function suffices.
  6. Impagliazzo and Wigderson proved in 1997 that if some problem needs exponential-size circuits, then BPP equals P.
  7. In computational pseudorandomness, randomness is not an objective property of a phenomenon but depends on the observer's computing power.
  8. Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson proved that if one-way functions exist, every NP statement has a zero-knowledge proof.
  9. Derandomization led to the 2002 deterministic primality test; zero-knowledge proofs reached Google's open-source age-assurance library in 2025.
  10. BPP equals P remains a conditional result, resting on circuit lower bounds that nobody has yet proved.

// Think further

If P = NP were proved one day, what would happen to "hardness for randomness"?
One-way functions would vanish, and cryptographic pseudorandom generators would lose their foundation. But Nisan–Wigderson only need "some exponential-time problem requires exponential-size circuits," a far broader assumption than P ≠ NP, and it need not fall with it. Worth asking about your own systems: which guarantees rest on "the adversary can't compute it," and which on "physics doesn't allow it"?
If randomness can be removed, why is engineering still full of it?
Derandomization promises replacement at polynomial cost, not equal speed or simplicity; primality testing is the example. And in cryptography and distributed protocols, randomness defends against an adversary who reacts to your choices, a different need from computing a fixed answer. Where do random choice in load balancing and Raft's randomized election timeouts fit?
If randomness is a property of the observer, is "looks human-made" for AI text and images also just a property of the observer?
Structurally it is close: authenticity is judged by whether some class of testers can tell. The difference is that pseudorandomness has a precisely defined class of testers, while "looks human" is judged by people whose standards shift. Once the testers get stronger, yesterday's convincing fake may give itself away today.
