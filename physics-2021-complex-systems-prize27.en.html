PHYSICS · 2021

You can’t forecast next Wednesday, but you can forecast 2070

Half the prize went to two people who put Earth’s climate inside a computer and then found humanity’s fingerprint in the noise of weather; the other half to a man who found hidden order in a hopelessly messy magnetic alloy

Syukuro ManabePrinceton University · 1/4Klaus HasselmannMax Planck Institute for Meteorology · 1/4Giorgio ParisiSapienza University of Rome · 1/2
for the physical modelling of Earth’s climate, quantifying variability and reliably predicting global warmingfor the discovery of the interplay of disorder and fluctuations in physical systems from atomic to planetary scales
2026-11-08 · BigCat

Noise is not dirt to be scrubbed away. Understand the noise, and the signal shows itself.

Your weather app gives you ten days, at most. Beyond that it won’t commit. Yet the news tells you how many degrees warmer the planet will be by the end of the century. How can a field that can’t say whether it will rain next Wednesday talk about a hundred years?

Now pour rice into a jar. Do it twice: the grains jam into a different arrangement every time, yet the jar holds almost exactly the same amount.

The 2021 physics prize put these two things side by side. What they share: the details are too messy to predict, but the mess itself has rules. Three people found ways to make that mess calculable.

A column of air 40 kilometres high, and a computer with half a megabyte

That carbon dioxide traps heat was not news. In 1861 John Tyndall systematically measured how strongly different gases absorb infrared. In 1896 Svante Arrhenius calculated that doubling CO₂ would warm the surface by about 6°C. But his atmosphere was a single uniform slab.

Real air moves. The ground heats up, warm air rises, water vapour condenses higher up and releases heat. Compute radiation alone and temperature falls far too fast with height: about 15°C per kilometre, against roughly 6°C in reality.

In 1959 Manabe, who had left war-ravaged Tokyo for the United States, was recruited to the lab that became the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory (GFDL) in Princeton. He made a bold simplification: squash the whole atmosphere into one column, from the ground to 40 km. Whenever temperature in the column dropped off too steeply, the air was made to “convect” and the profile snapped back. He also held relative humidity fixed: warm the air and it automatically holds more water vapour, the strongest greenhouse gas of all. That single choice built the warmer-wetter-warmer feedback into the model.

In 1967 he and Richard Wetherald published the answer: double CO₂ and the surface warms by about 2°C (the committee gives 2.3°C). The shape of the warming mattered even more. The lower atmosphere warmed while the stratosphere above it cooled. A brighter Sun would have heated the whole column at once. That shape later became a fingerprint.

By 1975 they had stretched the column into a global three-dimensional model, run on a computer with about 0.5 MB of memory: no ocean currents, fixed clouds. It gave 2.93°C. Today’s models give 2.5–4°C. The committee notes that a model so simple proving so robust suggests that more complexity does not necessarily mean better prediction.

The prediction of climate change without accompanying understanding of it is no better than prediction of fortune teller.— Syukuro Manabe · nobelprize.org telephone interview · 2021

Weather as noise: a pollen grain jostled by water molecules

Manabe’s model answered “what happens if CO₂ doubles”, but left a puzzle. In the 1960s the American meteorologist Edward Lorenz showed that weather is chaotic: nudge the starting point slightly and within weeks you get a different world. If weather can’t be computed, why should climate, which is made of weather, be any easier?

Hasselmann was a Hamburg physicist who had worked on ocean waves. In 1976 he published “Stochastic climate models” in Tellus, splitting the Earth in two: fast “weather” (mostly the atmosphere) and slow “climate” (ocean, ice, land). Warming the atmosphere by a degree takes weeks; the ocean, a thousand years.

His inspiration was Brownian motion, as explained by Einstein: a pollen grain drifting in water, kicked at random by countless invisible molecules. The ocean is the grain, the weather the molecules. Each gust is random, but the ocean adds them up, producing slow, long-period swings – “red noise” – that match observations. For the first time, “how much would climate wander on its own?” had a calculable baseline.

With a baseline you can ask the sharper question: is the observed warming natural wandering, or a push? From 1979 Hasselmann developed fingerprinting. The Sun, volcanoes and greenhouse gases each leave a distinctive pattern in space and time; compare the observations against all of them at once, and see which one stands far above the natural noise. He also pointed out that a signal is not necessarily easiest to see where it is strongest, but where the noise is weakest.

Later researchers summed up his approach: instead of searching one tiny corner of the haystack, then the next, search the entire haystack simultaneously. Hundreds of studies followed his framework, and in 2013 the IPCC concluded that human influence was “extremely likely” the dominant cause of the warming observed since the mid-20th century.

Three magnets at war, and a tree that branches without end

The other half of the prize starts with a strange alloy. Sprinkle a few iron atoms at random into copper, and each iron atom acts as a tiny magnet, a spin. In this spin glass, some pairs want to point the same way, others want to point opposite.

The simplest picture is a triangle with a magnet at each corner and one rule: neighbours must point opposite ways. Set two of them up and down, and the third is stuck – whichever way it turns, one edge is unhappy. Physicists call this frustration. Parisi’s own analogy: trying to be friends with two people who hate each other.

Frustration fills a spin glass’s “energy landscape” with valleys of every depth. Quench it and it gets stuck in one valley; quench it again and it gets stuck in another – like the rice. In the 1970s several future and past Nobel laureates were trying to calculate it. In 1975 Edwards and Philip Anderson used the replica trick: imagine many copies of the system and compute them together. But Sherrington and Kirkpatrick’s version gave a negative entropy at low temperature, a physical impossibility. The trouble was the assumption that all copies were symmetric with one another.

In 1979 Parisi found the way out. An ordinary magnet has two stable states, all up or all down. A spin glass has infinitely many, and they are not a heap: they are arranged in a strictly hierarchical tree. Pick any three states and two of the three pairwise similarities are equal; two leaves that split later are more alike. Proving the solution mathematically rigorous took many years.

there are very, very simple problems that are very hard to understand— Giorgio Parisi · nobelprize.org telephone interview · 2021

Why together, and what grew from it

A climate and an alloy sit dozens of orders of magnitude apart, yet the committee saw one thing: many interacting parts plus randomness produce many time scales and many possible states. Hasselmann treated weather as noise and pulled climate out of it; Parisi found the shape of order inside disorder. The scientific background closes by saying that without a proper accounting of disorder, noise and variability, determinism is just an illusion.

On the climate side, the models Manabe and others pioneered are still the main tool. In 2020 Zeke Hausfather and colleagues went back and tested climate models published between 1970 and 2007: most predicted the subsequent global warming consistently with observations, especially once mismatched emissions assumptions were accounted for.

Parisi’s line grew well beyond physics. He and his collaborators showed that the memories stored in a Hopfield network correspond to the many valleys of a spin glass (see Physics 2024). The difficulty of the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model also gave rise to simulated annealing. In 2008 a team he was part of reconstructed flocks of a few thousand starlings in 3D and found that each bird interacts with a fixed six or seven neighbours, however far away they are. In 2015 researchers directly observed his predicted “replica symmetry breaking” in random lasers.

◐ Off the podium

The committee’s own background names several people who did not make the stage. By 1960 Cecil Leith had independently run what could be considered the first comprehensive atmospheric general circulation model. Describing climate with stochastic equations goes back to a 1966 proposal by Mitchell; Hasselmann’s contribution was deriving it from the underlying equations and, with Frankignoul, testing it against observations. Both of Manabe’s key papers were co-written with Wetherald.

On the phone, Manabe said that looking at past laureates, nobody had received the physics prize for his kind of work. Hasselmann said: “I’ll wake up tomorrow morning and find out.”

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

“Was this heatwave climate change?” now has an answer. In late June 2021 the Pacific Northwest saw temperatures never recorded before; the Canadian village of Lytton hit 49.6°C and was largely destroyed by wildfire shortly after. Within weeks World Weather Attribution concluded that the heatwave would have been virtually impossible without human-caused climate change: about 2°C hotter than at the start of the industrial era, and at least 150 times more likely. It asks the same question Hasselmann asked: how much of an observed change did we push?

Simulated annealing, one optimisation method that grew out of spin-glass work, comes from a 1983 paper cited more than 45,000 times.

What may change next

There is no “approval” moment here, but two layers can be separated. Settled: the direction and rough size of global warming, which models have got right for fifty years. Unsettled: how much warming a doubling of CO₂ actually brings – still 2.5–4°C, held up mostly by clouds and water vapour – and the high latitudes remain hard to simulate. WWA estimates that in a world 2°C warmer, a 2021-type heatwave goes from roughly once in a thousand years to once every five to ten.

What it does not mean

Climate models cannot tell you the weather on a given day. They can tell you how cold Stockholm is on average in December, not on 10 December next year. A cold winter does not refute warming – and, conversely, a few warm years are not proof of it: Hasselmann himself said ten years of warming may be natural variability. Parisi did not study climate: spin glasses have no direct connection to it; the two halves share a question about how to treat disorder and noise.

A way of thinking to borrow

Model the noise first, then look for the signal. Hasselmann did not start by proving warming; he first worked out how much climate wanders when nothing is done. Only against that baseline does a departure mean something. And look for the signal where the noise is smallest, not necessarily where the signal is largest.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • AI · energy landscapes and associative memory: Parisi’s many-valleyed landscape is a Hopfield network’s memory store: each memory a valley; give the system a partial cue and it rolls into the nearest one, completing the memory. Physics 2024 on this site is the other face of the same landscape. The difference: a spin glass’s landscape is set by random couplings, a network’s is dug out by data.
  • Observability · alert attribution: latency jumps in production. Was it the deploy, the traffic peak, a flaky dependency? The mature approach is isomorphic to fingerprinting: know the metric pattern each cause leaves, regress the observations on all patterns at once, and keep a noise baseline of “it always jitters this much”. Watching one metric is searching one corner of the haystack.
  • Computational complexity · phase transitions in constraint satisfaction: the frustrated triangle is a set of constraints that cannot all be met. In 2002 Marc Mézard, Parisi and Riccardo Zecchina showed that random Boolean constraint problems near the satisfiability threshold sprout huge numbers of metastable states – exactly why search algorithms suddenly slow down. “Why are some optimisation problems easy and others hard?” became a question about the shape of a landscape.
  • Distributed systems · fixed-fanout gossip: each starling aligns with its six or seven nearest neighbours regardless of distance, which the paper argues is why the flock holds together when a predator causes sharp density changes. Gossip protocols likewise contact a fixed k peers per round, not “every node within so many milliseconds”, so traffic stays bounded however the scale and density shift.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Half the 2021 physics prize went to climate modellers Syukuro Manabe and Klaus Hasselmann, half to Giorgio Parisi for disordered systems.
  2. Manabe reduced the atmosphere to a 40 km column and held relative humidity fixed, building in the water vapour feedback.
  3. The 1967 column model found about 2°C of warming for doubled CO₂, not far from today’s 2.5–4°C range.
  4. Greenhouse gases warm the lower atmosphere and cool the stratosphere, while a brighter Sun warms both: a fingerprint of human influence.
  5. Weather is chaotic, but Hasselmann treated it as random noise that the slow ocean adds up into long climate swings.
  6. Fingerprinting compares observations with the patterns of Sun, volcanoes and greenhouse gases at once, seeking signal where noise is weakest.
  7. In a spin glass the tiny magnets frustrate one another, so no arrangement satisfies every pair and the energy landscape fills with valleys.
  8. In 1979 Parisi solved the replica puzzle: a spin glass has infinitely many stable states arranged in a hierarchical tree.
  9. Event attribution found the 2021 Pacific Northwest heatwave virtually impossible without human-caused climate change.
  10. Baselining noise before hunting for signal links climate fingerprinting to production alert attribution and Hopfield memory landscapes.

// Think further

Manabe got close to today’s answer with a single column of air. Is a more complex model more trustworthy?
The committee reads the robustness of simple models as evidence: if the conclusion does not depend on the details, it has caught the dominant mechanism. But a simple model can also be right by luck – Arrhenius’s 6°C came from two errors cancelling. Think of capacity estimates you have seen: a rough estimate where every term’s origin is clear, or a detailed simulation no one can explain – which would you trust?
Weather can’t be predicted, climate can. What other systems are “unpredictable individually, predictable in aggregate”?
An insurer does not know who will claim next year but can price premiums; a load balancer does not know the next request’s size but can plan capacity. Hasselmann’s condition is time-scale separation: the fast variable must be fast enough and the slow one slow enough for the slow one to treat the fast one as noise. Where the scales do not separate – a financial market in a panic, say – the method breaks.
What does “model the noise first” mean in your own work?
Many A/B-test misreadings come from not knowing how much a metric moves on its own. A concrete exercise: pick a metric you watch, plot its range over a few weeks when nothing changed, then revisit past “significant improvements”.
