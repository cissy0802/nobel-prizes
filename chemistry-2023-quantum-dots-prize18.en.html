Same crystal, different size, different colour

The 2023 chemistry prize: discovering, and learning to reliably make, quantum dots: semiconductor crystals of a few thousand atoms whose colour is set by their size

for the discovery and synthesis of quantum dots
2026-10-30 · BigCat

To change the colour, don’t change the recipe. Change the size.

Walk past a wall of televisions and some of the labels say QLED. The Q stands for quantum dot. Behind the screen the first light to switch on is blue. It passes through a thin film sprinkled with countless tiny crystals, which swallow part of the blue and give it back as red and green. Three primaries, done.

Here is the odd part: the dots that glow red and the dots that glow green can be the same substance, atom for atom. The only difference is their size. A dot typically holds a few thousand atoms; the Nobel committee put it this way: a quantum dot is to a football what a football is to the Earth. At that scale, colour stops depending only on what a thing is made of. It also depends on how big it is.

A textbook prediction that waited forty years

In 1937 the physicist Herbert Fröhlich worked out that sufficiently small metal particles should not behave like a lump of the same metal. One way to picture it: an electron is also a wave, and if you shut it in a smaller box it has fewer, higher notes available, like a guitar string pressed shorter. In a semiconductor, that means the light it absorbs and emits drifts towards blue. This is the quantum size effect.

Predicting it was easy; making it was not. By the 1970s physicists did see it in ultrathin films, grown atom-layer by atom-layer with molecular-beam epitaxy in ultra-high vacuum. But those were skins sitting on bulk material, studied near absolute zero, and nobody expected them to leave the lab. As the committee’s scientific background puts it, Fröhlich’s idea of small particles as materials in their own right was still beyond experimental test.

The clue had been sitting in glassworks for millennia. Glassmakers added gold, silver or cadmium and played with the heat to get their colours. By the twentieth century it was known that a single dopant, a mix of cadmium sulphide and cadmium selenide, could turn glass yellow or red depending on how it was heated and cooled, and that the colour came from particles forming inside. Why size should matter, nobody had pinned down.

One piece of glass, one flask of liquid, both sides of the Iron Curtain

In 1979 Aleksey Yekimov joined the S.I. Vavilov State Optical Institute in the Soviet Union to study coloured glass. He was a semiconductor physicist, and his tool was absorption spectroscopy. He made glass doped with copper chloride, heated it between 500 and 700 °C for anything from 1 to 96 hours, and X-rayed the result: copper chloride crystals had grown inside, from about 2 nm to 30 nm, larger the longer they were baked. Measured at 4.2 kelvin, their absorption lines shifted towards blue as the crystals shrank. The 1981 paper in JETP Letters, co-written with A. A. Onushchenko, has a three-sentence abstract that ends by attributing the shift to “a quantum size effect.”

He did not find it shocking. When nobelprize.org rang him in the small hours on the day of the announcement, he said:

It was not very surprising, because that’s what we hoped, just to make experimental confirmation.— Aleksey Yekimov · telephone interview, nobelprize.org · October 2023

Two years later, across the ocean, Louis Brus at Bell Labs was trying to drive chemical reactions with sunlight using cadmium sulphide particles suspended in liquid, kept small to expose more surface. Something strange happened: leave a sample on the bench for a while and its optical properties changed. He guessed the particles were growing. Fresh particles were about 4.5 nm across; after a day they were about 12.5 nm, the small ones dissolving and feeding the big ones. The big ones absorbed like ordinary cadmium sulphide; the small ones were shifted to the blue. Published in 1983, it was the first sighting of the quantum size effect in particles floating freely in solution.

Neither knew of the other. Yekimov’s crystals were frozen in glass: you could look, not touch. Brus’s were in a liquid, so they could be fished out, coated and processed. Everything that later went into screens and cells came down the second road.

The real hurdle: make a whole batch the same size

Then came the catch. If colour depends on size, a batch of mixed sizes is a batch of mixed colours, and the spectrum smears. You cannot tell which properties come from size and which from defects. In the late 1980s, quantum dots typically re-emitted only a few percent of the light they absorbed.

Moungi Bawendi spent the summer of 1987 in Brus’s lab as a graduate student and came back as a postdoc in 1988. When he set up his own group at MIT and tried to reproduce the Bell Labs samples, they were not as good as he hoped. In a Nobel Week interview he said that this failure is what pushed his group to reinvent how the material was made.

The 1993 paper, with Christopher Murray and David Norris, lays out the method. Heat a high-boiling solvent to about 300 °C, then empty a 50 ml syringe of precursors into it in a single shot. The concentration spikes, and every seed crystal is born at the same moment; the cold reagents drop the temperature to about 180 °C, so no new seeds form. Then warm slowly to 230–260 °C and let the existing seeds grow together. Every few minutes, draw a sample and take its spectrum: if the sizes start to spread, lower the heat; if growth stalls, raise it. Want a particular colour? Scoop out a portion when you get there. A single run yields a whole series from 1.2 to 11.5 nm, and after sorting, the diameters vary by less than 5%.

This was not a new piece of physics. It was a recipe that worked. In the committee’s words, it turned nanometre-sized materials with sub-nanometre precision into something you could make safely in a benchtop flask, and it drew chemists into nanoscience in growing numbers.

From a flask to a wall of screens

Mark Reed coined the name “quantum dot” in 1986. In 1996 a shell of zinc sulphide around a cadmium selenide core shielded the core from surface defects and pushed the light yield up to 50%. In 1998 a single issue of Science carried two papers that made dots water-soluble and attachable to proteins and DNA, turning them into fluorescent labels for cells with narrow, pure colours and better resistance to fading than conventional dyes.

Then came screens: start with the efficient blue LEDs honoured by the 2014 physics prize and let quantum dots convert some of the blue into red and green. The scientific background cites an estimate of about USD 4 billion for the global quantum dot market in 2021. The same document is frank about the cost: common dots contain heavy metals such as cadmium or lead, and getting rid of them is still a research goal.

◐ Off the podium

Brus told a Nobel Week interviewer that when he asked around about small-particle research, everyone said there was none. A couple of years later he came across Yekimov’s work in an American translation of a Soviet journal, and only the abstract had been translated. He sent a handwritten letter by surface mail, with a preprint, to Yekimov’s collaborator Sasha (Alexander) Efros, a theorist whose calculations the committee’s background cites repeatedly. Efros was not among the three.

The names also escaped early. A press release went out before the announcement and Swedish media ran the winners. “There was a press release sent out for still unknown reasons,” Hans Ellegren, the Academy’s secretary-general, told the press conference. “This is very unfortunate; we do regret what happened.”

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Screens and lamps. QLED TVs and monitors work as in the opening scene: a blue light, partly recoloured by dots. Some LED lamps use dots to warm up cold white light. Samsung says it developed cadmium-free quantum dot material in 2014, put it in TVs in 2015 and holds about 150 related patents (the company’s own claim). In laboratories, quantum dots have been one of biologists’ colouring tools since 1998.

What may change next

Medicine is the most talked-about and the furthest off. A 2022 review counted just 6 clinical trials involving quantum dots in the US NIH registry as of December 2021; toxicity, fate in the body and cost all stand in the way. “Lighting up tumours for a surgeon,” as the committee put it, is a research direction, not routine surgery. Solar cells, infrared detectors and flexible electronics are likewise in labs or small runs. Even screens in which the dots themselves light up electrically are listed by the committee among applications still being explored; in the QLED TVs on sale, the dots mostly just recolour blue light.

What it does not mean

A QLED label is not a guarantee of meaningful quantum dots. In 2025 TCL and Hisense faced US class actions after the Korean materials maker Hansol Chemical had three TCL sets tested and reported no indium or cadmium, the elements the dots rely on. TCL answered with its own tests showing 4 mg of cadmium per kilogram of film, an amount Korean industry officials called too small to matter. The claims are contested, but the point stands: a buyer cannot see from outside how much quantum dot a TV contains. Nor has any of this to do with quantum computing. “Quantum” here just means electrons shut in a small box, with energy that comes in steps.

A way of thinking to borrow

When a property everyone treats as fixed starts to drift, look for the parameter nobody was tracking. Glassmakers and Brus both saw colour change; the answer was size, a quantity no one had treated as a variable. And: shrinking the variance can itself be the breakthrough. The size effect had been seen; Bawendi’s “all the same size” is what made it usable.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · barrier synchronisation: the heart of hot injection is that every seed is born at once, after which crystals only grow. That is a barrier in parallel computing: everyone starts the step together and finishes together; stagger the start and you get a long tail. Murray and Bawendi added a feedback loop on top, watching the spectrum and lowering the heat when the size distribution widened, raising it when growth stalled: a controller whose metric is distribution width.
  • Complexity · rich get richer: in Brus’s bench sample the small particles dissolved within a day and the big ones grew. Chemists call it Ostwald ripening. Small particles dissolve more easily and feed the large ones, structurally the Matthew effect or preferential attachment. Bawendi’s recipe is a way of fighting this spontaneous inequality.
  • Quantum mechanics · man-made energy levels: in a quantum dot the electron’s energy levels are set not by the kind of atom but by the size of the box. The 2025 physics prize on this site is about another man-made ladder: a superconducting circuit you can hold in your hand, with energy that comes in steps. One gets there by shrinking, the other by cooling, but both build quantum steps outside the atom. The same year’s 2023 physics prize is about watching electrons move between such steps.
  • Materials chemistry · geometry instead of composition: the 2025 chemistry prize (metal–organic frameworks) follows the same idea: what a material can do depends not only on which atoms it uses but on the shape they are built into and the space left over. Quantum dots tune particle size; MOFs tune pore size.
  • Buddhism · emptiness of inherent nature: “what colour is cadmium selenide?” is not a complete question; it depends on size and surroundings. Colour is not a nature the substance owns but something the substance and its conditions produce together, which shares its structure with the teaching that things lack inherent existence and arise from conditions. Where the analogy stops: physical conditions can be measured and dialled precisely.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Quantum dots are semiconductor crystals of a few thousand atoms; for the same composition, smaller dots absorb and emit bluer light.
  2. The quantum size effect was predicted in 1937, and for decades was seen only in thin films grown in ultra-high vacuum and studied at low temperature.
  3. Glassmakers knew one dopant could turn glass yellow or red, but not that the colour depended on the size of particles inside it.
  4. In 1981 Yekimov reported that copper chloride crystals in glass shift their absorption bluewards as they shrink, a quantum size effect.
  5. In 1983 Brus at Bell Labs independently saw the same effect in cadmium sulphide particles floating freely in solution.
  6. Mixed particle sizes smear the spectrum; in the late 1980s quantum dots typically re-emitted only a few percent of absorbed light.
  7. Bawendi’s 1993 hot-injection method made all seeds form at one moment and grow together, giving samples with under 5% size spread.
  8. QLED screens use blue light partly recoloured to red and green by quantum dots; medical uses are still far from routine care.
  9. A QLED label does not guarantee meaningful quantum dot content, the issue behind 2025 class actions; nor is any of this quantum computing.
  10. Hot injection mirrors a barrier in parallel computing, and Ostwald ripening, where small particles feed big ones, is a Matthew effect.

// Think further

Yekimov says the result was “not very surprising”: it confirmed the textbook. Is that still a discovery?
The theory existed; the hard part was finding a system that showed it cleanly. Yekimov recognised that coloured glass was that system and then measured size and energy together. Is there something in your own field that everyone knows “should” happen but no one has actually measured? What would one clean measurement be worth?
Bawendi’s contribution is largely a recipe. Why does a method for making things tidier share the prize with the discovery itself?
Before it, the size effect was tangled up with defects and size spread, and no one could say which properties were real. A reproducible method that others can follow turns a phenomenon into a field. Think of benchmark datasets and training frameworks in AI: not new ideas in themselves, but they decide how many people can join in.
The Iron Curtain kept Brus from Yekimov’s work for years. Now that information flows freely, will duplicate discoveries disappear?
Two independent findings also made the result more credible. Today the bottleneck may be not access but volume: a key paper can still drown. Is duplicate discovery waste, or a kind of redundant verification, like replicas in a distributed system?
