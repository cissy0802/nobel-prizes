The gap doesn't open on your first day at work. It opens the year your first child is born

Two hundred years of archives, reread: why women's work and pay moved the way they did, and where today's gap is really stuck

Claudia GoldinHarvard University · 1/1
for having advanced our understanding of women’s labour market outcomes
2026-11-01 · BigCat

Goldin's conclusion: even if no one in the labour market were biased, a sizeable gender pay gap would still be there.

Most forms give you a single box for "occupation". You might have a day job, freelance at night and mind the family shop on weekends, but the box takes one answer.

Before 1940, the US census asked that same single question. The wife of a small farmer worked the fields beside her husband and made textiles or dairy goods to sell; in the census taker's box she was "wife". The woman who ran a boarding house, cooking for lodgers, collecting rent and keeping the books, was also "wife". By today's definitions, that means she did not work.

Economists drew curves from those boxes and reached a tidy conclusion: the richer an economy gets, the more women go out to work. Claudia Goldin, an economic historian at Harvard, went back into the archives, recovered the women filed as "wife", and found that the first half of that curve had been drawn the wrong way round.

Workers recorded as wives

Goldin started out studying slavery in the American South, then nineteenth-century family decisions about who went to school and who went to work. By the late 1970s she realised she had been leaving out the person who would change most: the wife and mother. Her explanation, in her Nobel autobiography, is short:

I neglected her because the sources had.— Claudia Goldin · Nobel Prize autobiography

So her first job was repairing the data. Cross-checking income reports, time-use surveys and census records, she estimated how many women working on farms, keeping boarding houses or doing piecework at home the 1890 census had missed. After the correction, the participation rate of white married women rose from the official 2.5% to 12.5%, five times higher; for all women it went from 19% to 26%.

For earlier decades the census did not even ask women's occupations, so she took another route. Philadelphia's city directories listed the occupation of each household head, which for a married couple meant the husband. Working with about 12,000 female-headed households from 1791 to 1860, she found a clever clue: after a husband died, his widow often appeared in the directory under his trade, as shoemaker, ironmonger, tinplate worker. Around 1790, 43% of widows carried the same title as their late husbands. Nobody learns to make shoes the week of a funeral. She had almost certainly been working in the shop all along; no one had written it down.

Stitched together, the corrected data trace a U-shaped curve. During nineteenth-century industrialisation, young single women poured into factories: by 1837, over 40% of Massachusetts girls and women aged 10 to 29 worked in manufacturing. Married women's work went the other way. Work left the home for the factory, small children could not come along, and the community looked down on a husband who sent his wife to the mill. Only in the twentieth century, with offices and schools, did the curve turn up.

That one finding breaks a popular belief: economic growth does not automatically bring women into work. The US economy grew for two hundred years; women's employment fell and then rose. India's female participation rate today is close to that of the US in 1900.

Who shut the door, and who opened it

Between 1870 and 1930, typewriters and cash registers standardised clerical work, and office jobs grew from 1% to 10% of employment. From 1910 the "high school movement" pushed the share of teenagers in high school from 19% to 73% by 1940. The good jobs were there, and so were educated women. Yet married women's participation barely moved.

Part of the reason was written down: marriage bars. "Hire bars" kept firms from taking on married women; "retain bars" fired women when they married. In 1942, 87% of school districts had hire bars and 70% had retain bars. A 1930s survey found that 35–40% of working women were in firms that would not hire a married woman.

The bars were gone by the end of the 1940s. Goldin's explanation is not a moral awakening but arithmetic: there were too few young high-school graduates to hire, and a large pool of married women who had done office work and were ready to come back once the children were older. Turning them away had become too expensive.

A second finding runs against intuition. In nineteenth-century factories much work was paid by the piece, so pay tracked output and the gap came mostly from which jobs women could get. In salaried offices, firms built promotion ladders that rewarded long, unbroken careers, and employers who expected women to leave early used sex as a signal. So the overall gap narrowed while the part that productivity could not explain grew: by 1940, more than half of the male–female earnings difference in office work was unexplained.

By the time mothers went back to work, their daughters had chosen their majors

From 1930 to 1980, US GDP per capita tripled and women's participation doubled, yet the gender earnings gap hardly moved. Protest banners said women earned 59 cents on the man's dollar, and many read that as proof of discrimination.

Part of Goldin's answer is to look by cohort. If 20% of one generation of women work and 40% of the next, and the two are the same size, the average is 30%: participation has doubled, but the statistics show a slow crawl. Many of the new entrants were women returning after years away, which pulled down the average experience of working women and held the gap in place.

Underneath that sat expectations. In 1967, only 35% of American women aged 20–21 expected to be working at 35, close to what their mothers had done. When they reached 35, around 65% were working. They had planned their education around their mothers' lives and discovered too late that their own would include long careers. By 1979, the expectation had risen to 80%.

What sped the shift up was a pill. Oral contraception was approved in the US in 1960, but in many states single women under the age of majority, then 21, needed parental consent. Around 1970 many states lowered the age of majority to 18, for reasons tied to the Vietnam-era draft, nothing to do with family planning. Goldin and her co-author Lawrence Katz used that accident: because states changed their laws in different years, they could compare similar young women with and without access.

The result was clear. The age at first marriage for college-graduate women rose from under 23 to 25 in six years, and women went from 10% of first-year law students in 1970 to 36% in 1980. Being able to delay marriage and children made years of medical or law school worth it. Goldin called this the "quiet revolution": not the marches of the women's movement happening at the same time, but high-school girls quietly signing up for more maths. Since 1982, more women than men have graduated from college in the US every year.

Greedy work

So why is there still a gap? In 2020 the average gender gap in median full-time earnings across OECD countries was 13%: 18% in the US, 7% in Sweden. Women now have more education than men, and equal pay is the law.

Goldin, Marianne Bertrand and Katz followed MBAs who graduated from the University of Chicago's business school between 1990 and 2006. Men and women started on nearly identical pay; ten years later the gap was huge. Most of it came from time out and weekly hours: 41% of the women but only 10% of the men had taken a break of six months or more. And what made women cut back was, above all, children. Studies in Denmark, Sweden, Germany and elsewhere found the same picture: earnings paths overlap until the first child, then the mother's drops and the father's holds steady or rises.

The deeper cause Goldin calls greedy work: jobs where the hourly rate rises with long, unpredictable, always-on hours. Much of finance, corporate management and law works this way, because clients want that particular person, and handing them to a colleague is costly. Picture a couple with a child. Someone has to be able to collect a feverish toddler at 3 p.m. If both take greedy jobs, no one can; if both take flexible ones, they leave a lot of money on the table. The best deal is one person on call for the office and one on call for home. In most different-sex couples, the second one is the wife.

That is Goldin's point: the gap shows up at work, but its roots are in that household calculation. Her counter-example is pharmacy. Chains, pre-packaged drugs and computerised prescription records mean any pharmacist can serve any customer, so working fewer hours costs only the pay for those hours. Women pharmacists' earnings relative to men's rose from 0.60 in 1960 to 0.85 today.

The point is that the gender gap in pay is not simply an issue of workplace discrimination or biased supervisors.— Claudia Goldin · Nobel Lecture · 2023

◐ Off the podium

Goldin is the third woman to win the economics prize and the first to win it unshared. She was denied tenure at Princeton in the late 1970s; in her autobiography she notes she was never once asked to give a seminar there.

Her 2000 study with Cecilia Rouse on orchestra auditions behind a screen is often cited as proof of hiring bias. The committee's own background is restrained: the effects are significant only at the 10% level, and the later audition rounds are hard to interpret. It also says the evidence that workplace inflexibility drives the parenthood gap is mixed, and the exact mechanism is still debated.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

The headline figure "women earn X% of what men earn" is now read differently because of Goldin: economists increasingly ask how far apart men and women are before and after their first child. In 2019, Henrik Kleven and colleagues applied one method across six countries: mothers' long-run earnings loss after a first birth was about 21–26% in Denmark and Sweden, 31–44% in the US and UK, and 51–61% in Germany and Austria. Goldin sees one unexpected upside of the pandemic: working from home made flexible jobs more productive, and remote meetings made greedy jobs a little less greedy.

What may change next

Goldin's remedy is to lower the price of flexibility: make hand-offs between colleagues smooth, with teams that can cover for each other, as in paediatrics, anaesthesiology and veterinary medicine. One study found that when some US medical specialties capped weekly hours, more women chose them. Policy has limits too: a study of Austria found that parental leave and childcare had little effect on the gap; one of Norway found paternity leave had none, while childcare shrank the parenthood effect by almost a quarter. Goldin also notes that where women's education has surged but housework still falls on them, as in southern Europe and parts of Asia, birth rates are low, and she stresses this is a correlation, not a cause.

What it does not mean

Not that the gap is all discrimination, nor that there is none: discrimination is real, it just cannot explain most of what remains. Not that "women chose it, so it's fine": the choice is made in a market that pays a premium for being on call. Not that development will fix it on its own: the U-shaped curve shows there is no automatic conveyor belt from growth to equality.

A way of thinking to borrow

When an aggregate refuses to move, split it by generation. An average mixes newcomers and veterans; real change often hides in the youngest cohort and shows up in the totals only once they reach middle age.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · stateful nodes and failover: a greedy job is a node that keeps all session state locally. Clients can only talk to it, and when it goes offline the service breaks, so its uptime is priced at a premium. Pharmacy externalised the state into shared storage so any replica can take any request; one node working fewer hours is no longer an incident. Goldin says a single good substitute is enough to make a job less greedy, which is the logic of adding one hot standby.
  • AI · biased labels: the 1890 census is a training set with systematic label noise. "Wife" relabelled workers as non-workers, and the errors were not random: they got worse further back in time. The fitted rule "more development, more women working" was an artefact of the data-generating process. What Goldin did amounts to going back to the raw logs and relabelling.
  • Software engineering · rolling upgrades: in a rolling deployment, the fleet's average version changes only as old nodes are retired. Cohort effects work the same way. Beliefs and schooling choices are set when people are young, so society's "average expectation" flips only once a new generation fills the workforce, and a good policy may take fifteen or twenty years to show in the totals.
  • Economics · the price of a norm: in her Nobel lecture Goldin argues that as more women were educated and office jobs spread, the norm against wives working became more expensive to enforce and less useful, so it faded. This site's 2024 economics prize asks why rules are hard to change; here is one answer: a rule need not be argued away, it can be priced away. What raised the price were technologies like the typewriter and the pill, linking to the 2025 economics prize.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Before 1940, US censuses often recorded married women's occupation as "wife", missing their work on farms, in workshops and in boarding houses.
  2. After Goldin corrected the 1890 data, white married women's participation rose from the official 2.5% to 12.5%.
  3. Philadelphia widows often appeared under their late husbands' trades, such as shoemaker or ironmonger, showing wives had worked in family businesses all along.
  4. US women's long-run work follows a U-shape: down with industrialisation, up with offices and schooling, so growth alone does not raise female employment.
  5. Marriage bars still covered 87% of school districts in 1942 and disappeared during the 1940s as labour grew scarce.
  6. The move from piece rates to salaries and promotion ladders narrowed the overall gender gap but widened the unexplained part.
  7. Women who grew up in the 1950s planned their education on their mothers' lives and underestimated how long they would work.
  8. States lowered the age of majority for draft-era reasons, unintentionally giving young women the pill, delaying marriage and opening law and medical school.
  9. Greedy work pays a premium for on-call hours, so couples split into one partner on call for the office and one for home, creating today's parenthood gap.
  10. A greedy job resembles a node with only local state; pharmacy made workers substitutable through shared records, and its gender gap shrank sharply.

// Think further

If, from tomorrow, any colleague had to be able to pick up your work, what would need to change, and what would be lost?
On-call rotations, runbooks and shared code ownership are all ways of making work less greedy. The cost is that some hero-style individual value gets diluted, and some people lose bargaining power. Goldin says one substitute is enough; you do not have to turn the job into an assembly line. What does the minimum viable substitute look like?
Did remote work make greedy jobs less greedy, or did it let "on call" leak into every hour at home?
Goldin adds a caveat in her lecture: working from home is good as long as it does not become "Work From Hell". The same tools that make flexible workers more productive can make a 10 p.m. message feel normal. Much depends on whether an organisation rewards output or the appearance of being online.
If cohort effects mean policies show up in the totals only after fifteen years, what does that do to policy evaluation?
A policy whose totals have not moved after a few years may be judged a failure and scrapped while it is quietly working on the youngest generation. Conversely, an effect you see today may come from a change made twenty years ago. Grouping by birth year is safer than watching annual totals.
