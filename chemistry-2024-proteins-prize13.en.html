CHEMISTRY · 2024

A string of letters folds itself into a machine. Now we can read it forwards and write it backwards.

Half the prize went to AlphaFold, which computes a protein's shape from its amino acid sequence; the other half to the reverse trick: draw a shape first, then write the sequence that builds it, including proteins nature never made

Designing new proteins worked seventeen years before predicting natural ones did. Setting your own exam is easier than guessing someone else's.

Crack an egg into a hot pan and the clear white turns opaque and firm in seconds, and no amount of cooling will turn it back. Egg white is mostly protein. What you are watching is a set of carefully folded shapes being shaken apart by heat and tangling into a mess.

The American biochemist Christian Anfinsen once ran that process the other way. He coaxed a protein to unfold gently, with chemistry rather than a frying pan, and let it fold back up on its own. Every time, it came back to exactly the same shape. In 1961 he drew the conclusion: everything about a protein's shape is written in its sequence of amino acids.

If the answer is in the string, computing the shape from the string can't be that hard. People worked on it for more than fifty years.

A hundred letters, and more ways to fold than you can write without exponents

Proteins are the cell's working machinery: antibodies, hormones, the enzymes that run its chemistry. Their alphabet is about 20 amino acids, strung into a long chain according to the DNA's instructions. The chain then twists and folds by itself into a three-dimensional shape, and the shape is the function. A key with the wrong teeth opens nothing.

The classic way to see the shape is X-ray crystallography: grow the protein into a crystal, shine X-rays through it, and work back to where the atoms sit. At the end of the 1950s John Kendrew and Max Perutz in Cambridge used it to build the first 3D models of proteins, earning the 1962 chemistry prize. In the decades since, often after months or years of work per structure, labs have built up around 200,000 of them. The number of known protein sequences has passed 200 million.

Why not just compute it? In 1969 Cyrus Levinthal did the arithmetic. A chain of only 100 amino acids can in principle take at least a 1 followed by 47 zeros of different shapes. Trying them one by one would take longer than the age of the universe. Inside a cell, the protein folds in milliseconds.

In 1994 John Moult and Krzysztof Fidelis launched CASP, a blind contest held every two years. Labs that had just solved a structure kept it secret; competitors got only the sequence and submitted their guesses before the reveal. The score, GDT, roughly measures what percentage of the predicted backbone lands close to the real one, out of 100. On the hardest class of targets, where no similar known structure could be borrowed, the best score stayed below 40 all the way through 2016.

Baker's shortcut: stop guessing the exam, set it yourself

David Baker went to Harvard to study philosophy and social science; a copy of the textbook Molecular Biology of the Cell changed his course. In 1993 he opened a lab at the University of Washington in Seattle. By the late 1990s his group had built Rosetta, a program that works by borrowing parts. It hunts the database of known structures for short fragments whose local sequence resembles the target, stitches them together, then nudges the whole thing again and again toward lower energy. In plain terms, low energy means atoms aren't crammed together, hydrogen bonds are paired up, and the water-hating parts are tucked inside.

Rosetta made its CASP debut in 1998 and did well. Then the group asked: what if you run it backwards? Instead of feeding in a sequence and getting a shape, draw the shape you want and ask for a sequence that will fold into it.

Protein design already existed. In 1988 DeGrado and colleagues followed the simple rule "water-haters in, water-lovers out" to build a bundle of four helices; in 1997 Mayo and colleagues used a fully computational search to give a 28-residue structure a new sequence. But everyone was still imitating shapes nature had already made.

If you want to build an airplane, you don't start by modifying a bird; instead, you understand the first principles of aerodynamics and build flying machines from those principles.— David Baker, as quoted in the Nobel Committee's popular information

In 2003 Baker's team sketched a fold that appeared nowhere in the structure database: two helices packed against a sheet of five strands. Rosetta cycled between designing a sequence and predicting its structure, and settled on 93 amino acids that resembled no natural protein. The team put the gene into bacteria, had them make the protein, and solved its structure by X-ray. It matched the design to within 1.2 ångströms (a ten-billionth of a metre), down to which way the side chains pointed, and it was extremely stable. They called it Top7: the first protein whose shape and sequence both came from somewhere other than nature.

Jumper's rebuild: let the network read evolution's notebook

The opening on the prediction side came from evolution. If two amino acids touch in the folded protein, a mutation in one usually forces a matching change in the other, or the contact breaks. Line up the same protein from hundreds of species, look for positions that change together, and you can guess which ones touch. The catch is that co-variation isn't always contact: if A touches B and B touches C, A and C will look correlated too. In 2016 researchers learned to separate direct from indirect correlations, and contact prediction jumped.

In 2018 DeepMind entered CASP with AlphaFold. Demis Hassabis had been a chess master at 13, then a games developer and a neuroscientist; he co-founded DeepMind in 2010, and in 2016 its program beat a top Go player. AlphaFold lifted the best score from around 40 to almost 60 and won. But near-experimental accuracy meant about 90, and the team stalled.

The way forward came from a newcomer. John Jumper trained in physics and in 2008 joined a company that simulated protein motion on supercomputers; his name is on the 2010 folding-simulation paper the Nobel Committee cites. In 2017, freshly out of his PhD, he heard rumours that DeepMind was secretly working on proteins and applied. He and Hassabis tore the model down and rebuilt it as AlphaFold2.

The new model used a transformer, the attention machinery behind today's large language models. It reads the multi-species alignment while keeping a table of how every pair of amino acids relates, and the two keep correcting each other. Then each amino acid becomes a small free-floating triangle, and the network rotates and slides the triangles until they form a backbone, feeding the result back in for a few more passes.

When CASP14 was scored in 2020, AlphaFold2 reached about 90 on the hardest targets. Its median backbone error was 0.96 ångströms; the next best team's was 2.8. A carbon atom is about 1.4 ångströms wide. Closing the meeting on 4 December, Moult asked: what now?

Two directions on one map, and why they share a prize

The committee puts it plainly: prediction goes from sequence to structure, design from structure to sequence, two faces of one problem. Baker said the same on the morning of the call: there have always been two sides to the protein folding problem, and he liked seeing them recognised together.

Why did design succeed seventeen years earlier? The committee's answer: designers pick their own targets, usually regular and idealised, so a well-optimised sequence is likely to fold as drawn. Prediction has to face whatever irregular shape nature happened to write.

Then the two lines merged. After AlphaFold2, Baker built a similar deep-learning architecture into his own tools and published RoseTTAFold in 2021; he called AlphaFold2 a wake-up call. In 2023 his group retrained that prediction network to remove noise, turning it into a generative model, RFdiffusion, on the same idea as the image generators that conjure pictures out of static: ask for a function and it draws a brand-new protein backbone.

On the prediction side, DeepMind released AlphaFold2's code, computed every human protein, then predicted structures for more than 200 million known sequences. By October 2024 more than two million people in 190 countries had used it. The committee called it the first real scientific breakthrough of artificial intelligence. Jumper, on the phone the morning of the announcement:

we need computation to solve the problems of biology. And I just love that it's starting to work— John Jumper · nobelprize.org telephone interview · 2024

◐ Off the podium

On the morning of the call Baker said that if protein design ever won a Nobel, he had always assumed he would share it with Steve Mayo and Bill DeGrado, "So I'm a little sorry about that." A prize holds three people at most, and both are pioneers the committee names in its own background. The committee also states that neither breakthrough would have been possible without decades of experimental structures that structural biologists solved one at a time and deposited in the database.

When AlphaFold 3 appeared in Nature in May 2024 without its code, an open letter gathered more than 1,000 signatures by the end of the month, faulting the journal for not enforcing its own policy. DeepMind reversed course; within half a year the code was out for non-commercial use, with the trained weights available to academic researchers on request.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

The most direct product is a vaccine. In June 2022 South Korea approved SK bioscience's COVID-19 vaccine SKYCovione for adults 18 and over. Its core is a self-assembling nanoparticle designed at the University of Washington's Institute for Protein Design and studded with pieces of the coronavirus's protein. The university called it the first therapeutic approved for people to come out of the institute; Baker called it the first truly de novo designed medicine approved for human use. In a phase 3 trial of 4,037 adults it raised roughly three times the neutralising antibodies of the Oxford/AstraZeneca vaccine, and the Korean government ordered 10 million doses. AlphaFold's reach is less direct: it helps researchers see why diseases develop and how bacteria resist antibiotics. It is not itself a medicine, and this page found no approved drug designed directly with it.

What may change next

Baker named three directions on the phone: smarter drugs that act only at the right time and place in the body, new enzymes to break down pollutants and plastics, and greener chemistry. Keep the stages apart: the vaccine is approved; designed virus inhibitors, sensors and protein switches are mostly still in the lab; the first de novo enzymes in 2008 were far slower than natural ones and needed rounds of directed evolution to improve. "AI will end disease within years" is hype; seeing the shape of a target is only the first step in making a drug.

What it does not mean

It does not mean "protein folding is solved." The committee draws the line carefully: the folding process is a separate field, and AlphaFold2 answers the final shape. EMBL-EBI's user guide lists what it handles poorly: regions with no fixed shape, proteins that switch between forms, the effect of a single-letter mutation, and how a protein changes when other molecules bind to it. Proteins with too few relatives defeat it too; the paper itself reports accuracy falling off sharply once the alignment drops to around 30 sequences. Nor does it replace experiments: it was trained on those 200,000 experimental structures.

A way of thinking to borrow

When a problem won't yield head-on, solve its inverse first. Baker didn't wait for prediction to be cracked. He took on the reverse problem, where you choose the target, and the tools and intuition he built there later flowed back into prediction.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • AI · from discriminative to generative: RFdiffusion takes a network trained to recognise structure, fine-tunes it to denoise, and it starts to draw. That is the same path image models took: a model that understands a distribution well enough can be run backwards to sample from it. The committee also argues AlphaFold2 is not mere pattern matching but has probably learned an energy landscape, the same landscape a Hopfield network rolls downhill on in this site's 2024 physics prize. Rosetta's Monte Carlo search for low energy is the same move.
  • Distributed systems · recovering topology from correlations: inferring which residues touch from which ones co-vary across species is like inferring a service call graph from correlated metrics. The trap that held the field for twenty years is familiar too: if A calls B and B calls C, A and C move together. The 2016 jump came precisely from separating direct dependencies from transitive correlation.
  • Neuroscience · it works, and we don't know why: asked whether it matters that the rules are not understood, Baker answered that people solve problems every day with a very complicated neural network they don't understand at all: the brain. Prediction and explanation have come apart here; a model that gives the right answer does not mean we understand the reason.
  • Evolution · experiments already run: a sequence alignment reads billions of years of mutation experiments that natural selection has already filtered. This site's 2024 medicine prize uses cross-species conservation to judge whether an RNA matters, another reading of the same data. Baker's "don't modify the bird" deliberately steps outside that data, toward shapes evolution never tried.
  • Materials · fix the interfaces, let the whole assemble: Baker's group designs protein cages that snap themselves into icosahedra, and SKYCovione is built on this kind of self-assembling designed particle. The MOFs of the 2025 chemistry prize follow the same logic: specify only how metal nodes and linkers join, and a vast ordered framework grows on its own.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Anfinsen found that an unfolded protein refolds to the same shape, so a protein's 3D structure is encoded in its amino acid sequence.
  2. Levinthal estimated that a 100-residue chain has an astronomical number of possible shapes, yet cells fold it in milliseconds, so brute-force search cannot be how it works.
  3. Experiments have solved about 200,000 protein structures against more than 200 million known sequences, and structure prediction exists to close that gap.
  4. The CASP contest has blind-tested structure prediction since 1994; on the hardest targets the best score stayed below 40 through 2016.
  5. David Baker ran the prediction program Rosetta in reverse and in 2003 designed Top7, a 93-residue protein with a fold not found in nature.
  6. Positions that co-vary across species tend to touch in 3D, and separating direct from indirect correlation made contact prediction leap in 2016.
  7. Jumper and Hassabis rebuilt AlphaFold around a transformer, and AlphaFold2 scored about 90 at CASP14 in 2020, close to experimental accuracy.
  8. Protein design succeeded seventeen years before structure prediction because designers choose regular target shapes, while prediction must handle any natural protein.
  9. SKYCovione, a COVID-19 nanoparticle vaccine approved in South Korea in 2022, was the first approved therapeutic from the Institute for Protein Design.
  10. AlphaFold2 predicts a static final shape and remains unreliable for disordered regions, multiple conformations and the effects of point mutations.

// Think further

AlphaFold2 gives the answer without saying why a protein folds that way. Does that count as solving the problem?
Depends what you need. For a structure to design an experiment around or a drug target to look at, the answer is enough, and Baker takes that view. For understanding the folding process, or predicting how a mutation will break a protein, it isn't; the model is insensitive to single-point mutations by design. It's like a black-box service that has never failed: would you trust it on an input unlike anything it has seen? How much does its built-in confidence score change that answer?
A chemistry prize went to a tech company's CEO and one of its researchers. What does that mean for science?
AlphaFold2's public code let the whole world test it, and the committee counts that as part of its impact. AlphaFold 3, published without code, drew a protest letter from more than a thousand scientists. The same company, two choices, side by side. When the strongest tools sit inside companies, who holds the line on reproducibility?
Without those 200,000 hard-won experimental structures, would there be an AlphaFold at all?
Almost certainly not, and the committee says so. Which raises a question: how many AI breakthroughs rest on decades of unglamorous data collection? And when a model trained on a public database creates enormous value, how should some of it flow back to the people who built the database? The arguments over open-source software and LLM training data are a useful comparison.
