CHEMISTRY · 2021

An amino acid you could eat did a heavy metal's job

No metals, no enzymes: small organic molecules of a few dozen atoms that act as catalysts and make only the mirror-image molecule you want

Benjamin ListMax-Planck-Institut für Kohlenforschung · Germany · 1/2David W.C. MacMillanPrinceton University · born in the UK · 1/2
for the development of asymmetric organocatalysis
2026-11-09 · BigCat

In 1971, two groups of chemists saw that proline could act as a catalyst. They wrote it up and moved on.

Chew a mouthful of rice long enough and it turns sweet. The worker is an enzyme in your saliva: a big molecule strung together from hundreds of amino acids, snipping starch into sugar. It isn't used up; it finishes one cut and starts the next. That is a catalyst.

Now a thought experiment. Take that machine of several hundred parts apart, keep just one amino acid, and drop it into a flask. Will it still do anything?

Almost any chemist would have said no: a part without its machine is just a part. Early in 1999, a German who had just become an assistant professor in California decided to try it anyway.

Left hands, right hands, and a toolbox with two drawers

Many molecules come in two "hands": the same atoms arranged as mirror images, like your two hands, which never quite overlap. This is chirality. The two hands can do very different things in the body. In the thalidomide disaster of the 1960s, one mirror image of the drug caused severe malformations in thousands of developing embryos. So drug makers often want one hand only, and the art of making just one is asymmetric catalysis.

Before 2000, chemists had essentially two kinds of catalyst. Metals are good at lending and taking back electrons, loosening strong bonds; the 2001 chemistry prize went to people who used metals for asymmetric catalysis. But many metal catalysts are spoiled by oxygen and water. You can pump the air out of a lab flask; a factory reactor is another matter. And many are heavy metals. Enzymes, life's own catalysts, almost always produce a single hand, but they are huge assemblies of hundreds of amino acids, hard to make and fragile.

Small organic molecules as catalysts had in fact been glimpsed. In 1971, two groups independently found that the amino acid proline catalysed a ring-closing reaction, producing an intermediate for making steroids, with one hand strongly favoured. The Nobel committee puts it bluntly: these studies were not followed up by the authors, nor did they result in a general concept. In its words, the time was not yet ripe.

One took an enzyme apart; the other walked away from metals

List's thread starts with an antibody. In 1997 he went to the Scripps Research Institute in California as a postdoc with Richard Lerner and Carlos F. Barbas III, working on an antibody redesigned to catalyse the aldol reaction: joining the carbon atoms of two molecules, one of the most basic moves in building complex molecules.

The antibody's crystal structure was on hand: on one side of the active pocket sat an amino group, on the other an acid. In his Nobel lecture he recalls January 1999, newly an assistant professor, asking himself why a small molecule couldn't carry all that machinery. All it needed was an amine and an acid. Then he remembered such a molecule already existed: proline, the very catalyst of the 1971 reaction. He called it a "micro-aldolase", an enzyme shrunk to one amino acid. The experiment worked almost at once: acetone joined to several aldehydes, in high yield and clearly favouring one hand.

This "direct asymmetric aldol reaction" was then considered one of the hardest problems in the field. The only previous solution was a metal catalyst from the group of Masakatsu Shibasaki.

All of a sudden, it turned out that there existed an edible, non-toxic, readily available catalyst that provides the same kind of reactivity with state-of-the-art selectivity.— Benjamin List · Nobel Lecture · 2021

MacMillan's thread starts with a question. At Harvard he had worked on metal-based asymmetric catalysis, and noticed that these elegant catalysts rarely made it into industry. His guess: the metals were simply too touchy and too expensive. When he set up his own lab at UC Berkeley in 1998, he left the metals behind.

A metal's trick is to grab a molecule briefly, make it "hungrier", then let go. He chose small molecules that form an iminium ion with the starting material: once attached, the material grabs electrons more eagerly and reacts much faster. From the natural amino acid phenylalanine he made an imidazolidinone in three steps and tried it on the Diels–Alder reaction, which chemists use to build carbon rings. More than ninety per cent of the product came out as one hand.

In January 2000, just before List's paper came out, he submitted his manuscript and gave the idea a name, organocatalysis, so others would see there were more such catalysts waiting to be found.

How two short papers drew a line between before and after

As reactions, neither paper was earth-shaking. Iminium ions speeding up Diels–Alder reactions had been reported in 1976 and 1989, but there the iminium ion, once formed, would not let go, so nothing could turn over a second time. A key part of MacMillan's design was a bond just loose enough to be washed off by water once the job was done.

The committee speaks of a clear before and after because what the two offered was not a reaction but a generic mode of activation. List's proline joins the starting material as an enamine, making it more willing to give up electrons and go on the attack; MacMillan's iminium ion does the opposite, making it hungrier for electrons. One pushes, one pulls: the two halves of a chemical reaction. Working independently, in the same year, they opened the same door from both sides. Organocatalysis now stands beside metals and enzymes as the third pillar of asymmetric catalysis.

Once the door was open, people poured in; one review called it the organocatalysis "gold rush". Another advantage was the assembly line. Making a complex molecule used to mean isolating and purifying each intermediate, losing material every time. Organocatalysts are forgiving enough that several steps can often run in one go, a cascade reaction. Take strychnine, the poison of Agatha Christie novels. Its first synthesis, in 1952, took 29 steps, and only 0.0009 per cent of the starting material ended up as strychnine. In 2011, with organocatalysis and a cascade, it took 12 steps and was 7,000 times more efficient.

Twenty years on: where it got to, and where it is stuck

By the time of the prize, the committee described organocatalysis as a routine tool in synthesis planning in industry and academia, with pharmaceuticals as its main destination. Novartis researchers described an organocatalytic route to the blood-pressure drug aliskiren (approved by the US FDA in 2007). In his lecture, List describes how a team at DSM in the Netherlands found that a reaction pioneered in his lab and advanced by MacMillan's group gives a product easily converted into the HIV drug darunavir. In 2008 MacMillan coupled organocatalysis to visible-light photoredox catalysis. List, meanwhile, has turned proline, a "little acid", into catalysts approaching superacid strength, wrapped in a confining pocket, working at loadings below one part per million.

Assessments from outside the laureates' labs are cooler. A 2020 review from the Budapest University of Technology and Economics notes that high catalyst loadings and long reaction times are generally seen as drawbacks, that recovering the catalyst and separating it from the product are obstacles to industrial use, and that the application of organocatalysis in industrial processes is still not significant. A 2021 paper by List and a colleague says it is on the rise in industrial settings. Read together: the direction is right; the volume is still small.

◐ Off the podium

The 1971 reaction still carries the names of its discoverers, Z. G. Hajos and D. R. Parrish, and U. Eder, G. Sauer and R. Wiechert; the committee calls their contributions pioneering, simply never pursued. List notes in his lecture that the reaction was meant to pave an industrial route to steroids, which never became a reality: steroids are still largely made from natural materials. The other two authors of List's 2000 paper, Lerner and the late Barbas, are not among the laureates.

On the morning of the announcement, MacMillan got a text from Stockholm with his name wrong. Assuming a prank by a mischievous former co-worker, he went back to sleep. When List called, he still didn't believe it, bet List $1,000 it wasn't happening, and went back to sleep again. He woke to a phone going crazy: I was $1,000 down but a very happy person.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

You can't see it; no pill box says "made by organocatalysis". The committee's examples: drug makers use it to streamline production of paroxetine, for anxiety and depression, and the antiviral oseltamivir. List says it is used on a technical scale to make pharmaceuticals and scent molecules. The broader effect sits upstream in drug discovery: candidate molecules that could once be extracted only in tiny amounts from rare plants or deep-sea organisms can now be made in quantity, in one hand, for testing.

What may change next

Already in use: drug discovery, some production routes, and the photoredox catalysis it was coupled with (which the committee says has reached most areas of organic chemistry, in academia and industry). Still mostly in the lab: the ultra-low-loading acid catalysts and membrane methods that let catalysts be recovered and reused. The realistic expectation for the next decade is shorter, cleaner syntheses for some drugs, not the replacement of metals and enzymes. The three pillars will stand side by side, and which one you pick depends on the reaction and the cost.

What it does not mean

"Organic" here has nothing to do with organic food. An organic molecule in chemistry is simply one built on a carbon framework; petrol is organic. Organocatalysts contain no metal and are often cheap and tolerant of air and water, but that does not automatically make them non-toxic, natural or green: high loadings and the need for recovery eat into the gains.

Nor did it make metals and enzymes obsolete. The committee's phrase is "third pillar", standing alongside biocatalysis and metal catalysis. The prize rewards an extra option, not a replacement.

A way of thinking to borrow

Strip it to the smallest piece that still works. List didn't try to build a better enzyme. He asked which two parts of the big machine actually do the work, and found both in an off-the-shelf amino acid. When a function seems to demand a complex system, look for its minimal working unit first.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · leases must expire: the bond between organocatalyst and substrate is a lease. Acquire, change the holder's behaviour, release, so the catalyst returns to the pool for the next client. The iminium ions of 1976 and 1989 were locks never released: usable once. MacMillan made the binding just loose enough. The difference is that chemistry's timeout is hydrolysis, not a clock.
  • Software architecture · extracting the core from a monolith: an enzyme has hundreds of amino acids, yet List saw that the catalysis rests on an amine and an acid; much of the rest is scaffolding that makes it faster and more precise. Proline is the extracted microservice. Tellingly, he later added a "pocket" back around his catalysts, conceding that the scaffolding does work too. For the opposite direction, see the 2024 chemistry prize: Baker designs whole protein machines from scratch.
  • Information theory · chirality as a copyable bit: a single-handed catalyst writes one bit, left or right, onto thousands of product molecules, cycle after cycle; each catalyst molecule is a write head. The 2026 chemistry prize is the next step: a product that catalyses its own formation, so a slight bias amplifies itself. And Sharpless of the 2022 chemistry prize was one of the 2001 laureates for metal-based asymmetric catalysis.
  • Sociology of science · multiple and premature discoveries: List and MacMillan, hundreds of kilometres apart, reached the same concept in the same months: what Robert Merton called multiple discovery, an idea surfacing in several places once its time has come. The 1971 proline reaction is the mirror case: the result was there, but no frame yet to read it as a class of things. The contrast is a reminder that naming is itself a contribution.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Many molecules come in two mirror-image hands, and drug makers often want only one; producing just one is asymmetric catalysis.
  2. Before 2000, chemists believed asymmetric catalysis had only two tools: metal catalysts and life's own enzymes.
  3. Metal catalysts are often spoiled by oxygen and water, and many are heavy metals, so elegant lab methods rarely reached factories.
  4. Proline was shown to catalyse a ring-closing reaction in 1971, but nobody followed up or turned it into a general concept.
  5. Benjamin List saw from a catalytic antibody's structure that an amine plus an acid did the work, so he tried the amino acid proline.
  6. David MacMillan deliberately abandoned metals, designed an imidazolidinone that forms iminium ions, and coined the word organocatalysis.
  7. Enamines make a substrate readier to give electrons, iminium ions make it hungrier for them; together they became the third pillar of asymmetric catalysis.
  8. Organocatalysis suits cascade reactions: strychnine went from 29 steps in 1952 to 12 steps in 2011, about 7,000 times more efficient.
  9. Organocatalysis has streamlined production of drugs such as paroxetine and oseltamivir, but high loadings and recovery still limit industrial use.
  10. The catalyst–substrate bond works like a lease that must expire: loose enough to release, so one catalyst turns over many times.

// Think further

Proline catalysis was seen in 1971. Why did it take almost thirty years?
One answer is framing: it was treated as a handy single reaction, not as evidence that small molecules can mimic enzymes. Another is demand: the industrial steroid route it served never happened. A third is vantage point: List happened to be standing in front of a catalytic antibody's crystal structure, seeing exactly what an active site needs. Is there a result in your field, published long ago, still filed as a one-off?
If a catalyst needs high loadings and recovery, in what sense is it "greener"?
No heavy metals, no tolerance problems with air and water, so no need for oxygen-free equipment; cascades skip purification rounds. But at dozens of times the loading of a metal catalyst, plus extra solvent to separate it, the sums may not work. The right question is: at which step, compared to what, counting which cost.
MacMillan named the field. Is naming a scientific contribution?
Before him, examples of small-molecule catalysis lay scattered, never treated as one kind of thing. A name tells colleagues there is unclaimed land here. Think of how words like "microservices" or "deep learning" gathered scattered practices into a movement.
Shrinking an enzyme to one amino acid: what gets lost?
The whole pocket that wraps around the reaction. That is why proline is usually slower and less selective than an enzyme and needed in far larger amounts. List's later confined catalysts put the pocket back. The minimal unit proves the principle; the performance often hides in what you took for scaffolding.
