A bank that did nothing wrong can still be killed by a rumour

Two theorists explained why banks exist and why they are born fragile; an economic historian showed that the Great Depression ran so deep and so long because the banks went down

Ben S. BernankeBrookings Institution · 1/3Douglas W. DiamondUniversity of Chicago · 1/3Philip H. DybvigWashington University in St. Louis · 1/3
for research on banks and financial crises
2026-11-06 · BigCat

The thing that makes a bank useful and the thing that makes it fragile are the same thing.

Your salary lands and a number goes up in your banking app. You assume you can have it whenever you like: rent tomorrow, a doctor next month, all of it moved elsewhere on a whim.

At the same moment, the bank has lent that money to someone buying a house, repayable over thirty years. Both things are true at once. They can both be true only because of an unspoken assumption: we will not all come for our money on the same morning.

On 9 March 2023, depositors pulled more than $40 billion out of Silicon Valley Bank in a single day, and management expected another $100 billion to follow. The next day the bank was gone. It had real management problems, but what brought it down in under 24 hours was that the assumption broke. Three papers from four decades ago explain why it can.

In economics, banks were scenery

Between January 1930 and March 1933, US industrial production fell 46% and unemployment reached 25%. In three years the number of American banks roughly halved, many of them finished off by runs.

Yet for a long time economists did not treat bank failures as a root cause. The usual view was that they were a consequence of the slump. The most influential exception was Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz's 1963 monetary history, which blamed the failures for wiping out deposits and shrinking the money supply while the Fed failed to print enough to compensate.

Finance theory went further. In a frictionless market, the reigning framework said, it does not matter whose hands money passes through; banks are optional. The bank models of the 1970s simply assumed banks into existence. Nobody explained why they had to look the way they do: funding thirty-year loans with deposits you can withdraw any time.

Two classmates outside a professor's door

Diamond and Dybvig were at Yale together, both students of Stephen Ross. Ross did not make appointments; you sat outside his door and waited. In a phone interview with the Nobel website, Diamond recalled that the two of them talked a lot while waiting, and that Ross's assistant kept them supplied with cookies. Around the end of their PhDs they started building a model together and kept simplifying it. Dybvig insisted the paper be written more carefully and simply than usual, so that bank regulators who were not theorists could follow it.

A group of people each has some savings. There is a project that pays well if left for two years but returns only the original stake if torn up halfway. Nobody knows whether they will need cash next year, only that some of them will. Acting alone, the unlucky ones have to pull out early and take the hit.

A bank pools everyone's money and promises: come whenever you like and you will get more than you would by bailing out on your own; wait until the end and you get more still. Since only a fraction really need cash in any year, the bank keeps a little on hand and invests the rest long-term. This is maturity transformation: turning long, locked-up assets into deposits you can take out at any time. In effect the bank sells insurance against not knowing when you will need your money, and everyone is better off than without it.

And there is the catch. The bank cannot tell the needy from the nervous, so it pays whoever shows up first. If you believe many people will come tomorrow, the bank will have to dump its long-term projects and those at the back of the queue will get nothing, so your best move is to come tomorrow too. One contract, two outcomes: everyone waits and everyone gains, or everyone runs and the bank fails. Which one you get depends on what you think everyone else will do.

Their 1983 paper is explicit that the signal setting everyone off need have nothing to do with the bank itself:

This could be a bad earnings report, a commonly observed run at some other bank, a negative government forecast, or even sunspots.— Diamond & Dybvig · Journal of Political Economy · 1983

The cure is in the same paper: have the government guarantee deposits. If you know you will get your money back regardless, there is no reason to race anyone, and the run never starts. A well-designed deposit insurance scheme in theory never has to pay out.

The following year Diamond answered the other half. When a bank lends, someone has to keep an eye on borrowers for thousands of depositors, and that job is delegated to the bank. But who watches the bank? He showed that if a bank makes enough loans that are not tied to each other, the chance it fails from bad luck alone is close to zero; failure would mean it had slacked. That threat keeps it watching its borrowers, and depositors do not have to watch it.

When a bank dies, it takes more than deposits with it

Bernanke's paper of the same year went back to the Depression. He agreed the monetary collapse did damage, but thought it could not explain why the slump was so deep and lasted so long. His answer: what a bank really owns is knowledge of its borrowers. Which farm is sound, which small manufacturer pays on time: loan officers build this up over years, it is not on the balance sheet, and it cannot be moved to another bank. When the bank goes, the knowledge goes with it.

Using monthly data from 1921 to 1941, he found that money alone explained part of the fall in output; adding the deposits of failed banks and the liabilities of failing businesses explained most of it. He also found witnesses from the time. A 1932 industry survey reported:

During 1931 and the first half of 1932 … it unquestionably represented pressure by banks on customers for repayment of loans and refusal by banks to grant new loans.— National Industrial Conference Board survey · 1932 · quoted in Bernanke 1983

The hardest hit were households, farmers and small firms, who could only borrow from banks; big companies that could issue stocks and bonds suffered far less. In March 1933 Roosevelt closed every bank for a week and the panic finally stopped; later that year Congress created federal deposit insurance. But credit kept shrinking for years, because broken lending relationships had to be rebuilt one by one.

The committee put the three together because each line completes the other: Diamond and Dybvig supplied the why, Bernanke the proof that it happened and what it cost. Bernanke's paper even cited their 1981 working paper.

Runs did not go away; they moved

After the FDIC was set up in 1933, the US went decades without a large-scale bank run. But in the 2000s a crop of institutions that were not called banks began borrowing short and lending long: they funded themselves with short-term debt that had to be constantly rolled over and bought long-dated mortgage securities, with no deposit insurance. The committee calls them shadow banks.

In September 2007 depositors queued outside Britain's Northern Rock; on 15 September 2008 Lehman Brothers failed and short-term funding markets nearly froze. This time most of the runs were not at counters but in repo markets and money-market funds, and the logic was exactly the 1983 model's. The Fed chair was Bernanke himself (2006–2014). The Fed stretched the lender-of-last-resort role to cover shadow banks, and the US Treasury put $250 billion of equity into the nine largest banks. Afterwards capital rules were tightened, liquidity requirements were added and big banks were made to run stress tests.

When the pandemic hit in 2020, central banks used the same toolkit to keep credit flowing. Then came Silicon Valley Bank. The Fed's own review says it lost over $40 billion in a day, and with the expected second day the outflow came to about 85% of its deposit base; Wachovia in 2008 lost about $10 billion over eight days. Social media and technology, the review says, may have fundamentally changed the speed of bank runs.

◐ Off the podium

The committee's background names a predecessor who was not honoured: John Bryant's 1980 model already had runs and deposit insurance, but no long-term investment, and its runs came from bad news rather than people second-guessing each other, so insurance could not stop them.

The award drew criticism straight away. A Reason Foundation roundup from October 2022 gathered the voices: Paul Krugman said 2008 had vindicated Bernanke; David Henderson of the Hoover Institution argued this very research made Bernanke, as chair, focus on rescuing particular institutions; George Selgin said his quarrel was not with the model but with treating it as sufficient grounds for government intervention in banking. On the day of the announcement, Diamond said the world had been lucky to have Bernanke at the Fed during the crisis.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Deposit insurance is the cure that in theory never pays out. China's Deposit Insurance Regulations took effect on 1 May 2015, covering up to 500,000 yuan per depositor per insured institution. The FDIC covers at least $250,000 per depositor per bank and says that since its founding in 1933 no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds. Out of sight are capital buffers, liquidity rules, stress tests, and the promise that the central bank will lend when panic hits.

What may change next

The speed of runs. Silicon Valley Bank's depositors were largely start-ups and investors who knew each other, with balances far above the insured limit; a burst of chatter on social media could move tens of billions at once. The Fed's review says the stability of uninsured deposits needs rethinking. Whether regulation can keep pace is an open question. The general rule: any institution that promises money on demand while holding long-term assets can face the same problem, whatever it calls itself.

What it does not mean

It does not mean people who run are irrational. In the model, running when others run is exactly the rational thing to do, which is what makes it dangerous. It does not mean bank "money creation" is a con: deposits you can withdraw any time are backed by real long-term loans, which is the whole point of a bank. Nor are insurance and bailouts free: the committee itself notes that too much protection tempts banks to gamble, keeping the winnings and leaving taxpayers the losses. This research does not settle how to regulate.

A way of thinking to borrow

When something holds as long as people believe it and collapses when they stop, do not only ask whether the rumour is true; ask what would remove the reason to get out first. Fact-checking will not stop a self-fulfilling expectation. A credible backstop will.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · oversubscription and retry storms: a cloud provider oversells CPU and bandwidth on the bet that tenants will not all max out together, the same arithmetic as a bank's thin cash reserve. A client times out and retries, the retries add load, more clients time out: that is a run. Rate limiting and shedding map to suspending withdrawals; convincing clients their request will be served so they need not retry eagerly maps to deposit insurance. The difference is that retry loops do not second-guess each other.
  • Systems · cold caches: Bernanke's "knowledge of borrowers" behaves like a warm cache. Restart the node and every byte is still on disk, but the cache is empty and the system crawls for a long time. The Depression lasted because the economy's credit cache had been wiped.
  • Complexity · a bistable landscape: one contract with two stable outcomes is a landscape with two valleys, and where the system settles depends on where it starts and how it is nudged. Deposit insurance fills in the bad valley. The Hopfield network in this site's 2024 physics prize rolling downhill on an energy landscape is the same picture.
  • Sociology and Buddhism · self-fulfilling prophecy and dependent origination: Diamond borrowed the sociologist Robert K. Merton's term "self-fulfilling prophecy" for bank runs. The number in your account has no independent existence; it holds only because everyone, right now, is not withdrawing.
  • Economic thought · Schumpeter: the committee notes that Schumpeter argued in 1911 that financial intermediaries are essential to innovation and development. The creative destruction of this site's 2025 economics prize needs someone willing to lend to new things that have not yet worked.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Before the 1980s, mainstream economics treated banks as scenery and bank failures as a result of the Great Depression, not a cause.
  2. Diamond and Dybvig showed in 1983 that funding long-term projects with on-demand deposits is the best way to serve both savers and borrowers.
  3. Maturity transformation insures people against not knowing when they will need cash, and the same feature makes banks inherently vulnerable to runs.
  4. One deposit contract has two outcomes: everyone waits and all gain, or everyone runs and the bank fails, depending on expectations about others.
  5. The signal that sets off a run can be unrelated to the bank's health, so a sound bank can be destroyed by self-fulfilling panic.
  6. Government-guaranteed deposits remove the reason to rush, so a well-designed deposit insurance scheme in theory never has to pay out.
  7. Diamond showed in 1984 that a bank lending to many unrelated borrowers solves the problem of who watches the watcher.
  8. Bernanke showed in 1983 that the Depression was deep and long mainly because failed banks destroyed lending relationships that were slow to rebuild.
  9. The 2008 runs hit uninsured shadow banks, and in 2023 Silicon Valley Bank lost more than $40 billion of deposits in one day.
  10. Deposit insurance fills in the bad valley of a bistable landscape, much like promising cloud clients they need not race to retry.

// Think further

Dybvig says financial crises are unpredictable by nature, because if people could predict them they would not have signed the contracts that get run on. Do you agree?
This turns unpredictability from a lack of skill into a structural property: crises that can be seen get avoided, so the ones left are the unseen ones. It may also be an excuse. People did warn about shadow banking before 2008; perhaps the problem is less whether a crisis can be predicted than whether the forecaster can get everyone to believe it at once.
The more generous deposit insurance is, the fewer the runs, but the stronger banks' incentive to take risks. Where should the limit sit?
China's 500,000 yuan and America's $250,000 cover the great majority of ordinary savers while leaving big depositors a reason to watch their bank. After Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank, all deposits ended up guaranteed. Is the line still credible? A rule that gets broken in every crisis may be worse than none.
Is there an "available on demand" promise in a system you run that is actually backed by something long-term and locked up?
A team's promised response time, prepayments sitting on a company's books, an open-source project's promise of long-term maintenance can all be maturity transformation. What happens if everyone comes to collect at once? What would make them not need to come first?
