CHEMISTRY · 2025

A football pitch in a few grams of powder

Chemists could build almost any single molecule, yet they could not build a three-dimensional crystal to a blueprint. Three people learned to use metal ions as joints and organic molecules as beams, and put up molecular buildings full of empty rooms: metal–organic frameworks

The most valuable part of this material is the part with nothing in it.

The bag of activated charcoal in a fridge soaks up a whole shelf of smells. It works not because it is big but because it is hollow: countless invisible channels whose walls add up to an absurd area. But nobody designed those channels. Wide or narrow, open or blocked, they are whatever they happen to be.

Now picture the opposite: every pore drawn in advance. How many nanometres wide, which way it runs, what chemical groups line its walls, all chosen by you. Not hunting for a porous material, but designing an apartment for one particular kind of molecule. The 2025 chemistry prize went to the first people who learned to build like that.

Molecules, yes. Buildings, no.

By the late twentieth century organic chemists could assemble almost any complex single molecule, and they had good methods for stringing molecules into long chains (plastics are such chains, called polymers). What they could not do was grow a structure in two or three directions at once, to a plan.

But in two or three dimensions, it's a synthetic wasteland.— Roald Hoffmann · Scientific American · 1993 (quoted in the committee's scientific background)

Even prediction was out of reach. In 1988 John Maddox wrote in Nature that one of the continuing scandals in the physical sciences was that you generally could not predict the structure of even the simplest crystalline solid from its chemical composition. Crystals were made by trial and error: mix, heat, see what grows.

Porous solids did exist. Industry ran on zeolites, hard, heat-proof cages of silicon and oxygen that sieve molecules and soak up gases. But zeolites were found, not designed. Nobody could draw a pore first and then make the solid that contained it.

A box of drilled wooden balls

In 1974 Richard Robson, a lecturer at the University of Melbourne, was given a teaching chore: make model kits so students could build molecules. Wooden balls for atoms, rods for bonds. The department workshop drilled the holes, and the holes could not go just anywhere: a carbon atom reaches out in four fixed directions. Robson worked out the angles with log tables.

When the balls came back he noticed something. Drill the holes right, and anyone who plugs the kit together gets the right structure. The shape of the whole molecule was already written into the holes. Asked about it by nobelprize.org in 2025, he put the next thought like this:

What if we used molecules in place of the balls and chemical bonds in place of the rods?— Richard Robson · nobelprize.org interview · 2025

The idea came back every year with that lecture, and sat for about a decade. In the mid-1980s he finally went to the bench himself. His template was diamond, where each carbon links to four neighbours in a small pyramid. For the joints he picked copper(I) ions, which also like four neighbours; for the beams, an organic molecule with four arms, each ending in a group that grabs copper.

Most chemists would have bet on a shapeless bird's nest, or on several nets threading through each other until no space was left. Instead a regular crystal grew: wired like diamond, except each "bond" was now a whole molecule, leaving large chambers filled with freely moving solvent. Robson published it in 1989 with the crystallographer Bernard Hoskins in the Journal of the American Chemical Society. The next year they swapped one kind of ion inside the chambers for another without the framework collapsing: things could move in and out.

The trouble was that these buildings were flimsy and often fell apart once emptied. Some people, Robson recalls, thought the whole idea was a whole load of rubbish.

Making empty rooms stand up, then making them follow the plan

Two people picked it up, independently. In the early 1990s Susumu Kitagawa, then at Kindai University in Japan, made a first framework that was flat and held a few acetone molecules: not much use. His grant applications kept being turned down, and the reviewers' question was fair: zeolites already absorb gases, and are more stable, so why build a worse one? He kept going. In 1997 he made three-dimensional frameworks threaded with open channels that stayed intact when dried and, at room temperature, took up and released methane, nitrogen and oxygen. In 1998 he named something zeolites could not offer: these frameworks could be soft. He later called materials that flex as guests enter and leave "soft porous crystals".

The other was Omar M. Yaghi. He grew up in Amman, Jordan, a large family in a single room with no electricity or running water. At ten he slipped into the normally locked school library, opened a book at random and saw molecular structures for the first time. At fifteen, on his father's firm instruction, he left to study in the United States. In 1995 he reported in Nature a layered cobalt-based net that, emptied of its guests, survived heating to 350°C. The paper is where the name "metal–organic framework", MOF, first appears.

MOF-5, in 1999, became the field's emblem. Clusters of four zinc atoms around one oxygen form the joints; straight organic molecules form the beams; together they make a lattice of cubic rooms. Fully emptied, it survived 300°C. Gas measurements put its internal surface at about 2,900 square metres per gram, against a few hundred for typical zeolites. Unfold the inner walls of two or three grams and you have a football pitch.

The step the committee singles out came in 2002–2003. Yaghi showed these buildings could be edited to plan: keep the joints, swap in longer, shorter or decorated beams, and you get a whole family sharing the same net. He made 16 versions of MOF-5 with different pore sizes, one of which held large amounts of methane. He called the method reticular chemistry: choose rigid building blocks, then assemble them into a net decided in advance. Robson showed you could build; Kitagawa and Yaghi made the buildings stand, breathe and follow the drawings.

From records to factories

After that the field grew like a construction kit. The committee's popular summary says laboratories have now designed tens of thousands of different MOFs.

The applications are worth reading by date. In 2018 Yaghi's group took a prototype holding 1.2 kg of MOF into the Arizona desert. With no power, it soaked up moisture at night and released it when the sun warmed it, yielding about 100 g of water per kilogram of material per day-night cycle. In 2019 an aluminium-based MOF-303 managed about 0.7 litres per kilogram per day in the Mojave Desert at humidity as low as 10%. In 2021 a University of Calgary team reported CALF-20 in Science: a zinc MOF that prefers carbon dioxide even in humid flue gas, survived more than 450,000 steam cycles, forms in one step and scales to multi-kilogram batches. It is being tested in a factory in Canada.

The committee's own summary is candid: so far, most MOFs have only been used on a small scale. Between the 1989 paper and the first real products lay two to three decades.

◐ Off the podium

A Nobel Prize goes to at most three people, and this building had a larger crew. The first author of the 1989 paper was the crystallographer Bernard Hoskins. Robson told nobelprize.org that crystallographers, Hoskins above all and later Brendan Abrahams, gave the field a respectability it would not otherwise have had, while he himself would just mix things up and get compounds. The committee's background names many 1990s peers too, and M. O'Keeffe co-authored the key MOF-5 and reticular-synthesis papers.

Robson was 88 when the prize came. Of the people he knew well 25 to 30 years earlier, he said, they're all dead or close to it like myself.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

The most concrete foothold is the chip fab. Doping silicon means handling arsine, phosphine and boron trifluoride, which are lethal. NuMat's ION-X cylinders hold these gases inside a MOF below atmospheric pressure, so a leak does not push gas outward. A 2018 NuMat conference paper describes it as the first commercial product using MOFs; Merck KGaA now distributes it worldwide. The chip in your hand may have been made with gas from such a cylinder. The other foothold is carbon capture: in October 2023 BASF and the Canadian company Svante signed a commercial supply agreement for CALF-20, which goes into filters for industrial flue gas.

What may change next

Out of the lab and into industrial-scale trials: capturing CO₂ at smokestacks. Svante says it aims for hundreds of large facilities by 2030; that is a company target, not a result. Desert water harvesting has passed field tests, but at under a litre per kilogram per day it is far from supplying a household, and Yaghi co-founded a water-harvesting company, which is worth remembering when reading his group's numbers. Pulling PFAS "forever chemicals" out of water, storing hydrogen at normal pressure and recovering rare earths from wastewater are still laboratory work.

What it does not mean

"A football pitch per few grams" is surface area, not volume. It says the walls are thin and the pores many; how much the material actually holds depends on how strongly molecules cling to the walls. MOF is not one material but a way of building, and two MOFs can differ more than plastic and steel do. And smokestack capture is not pulling CO₂ back out of the sky: CALF-20 works on concentrated flue gas, and the captured gas still needs somewhere to go.

A way of thinking to borrow

Robson's wooden balls say: don't design the finished object; design the connection rules. Fix how many links each part takes and at what angles, and the whole assembles itself. To change the whole, change the interfaces.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Laozi · the use of the empty room: chapter 11 of the Tao Te Ching, in Legge's translation: "The door and windows are cut out (from the walls) to form an apartment; but it is on the empty space (within), that its use depends." A MOF is that sentence at molecular scale: the metals and organics are only walls, and what you pay for is the space they enclose. Kitagawa himself cites Zhuangzi's "usefulness of the useless", which he met in Hideki Yukawa's essays. Same point: what does the work is often not the solid part you can see.
  • Software architecture · interfaces fix the topology: a MOF node is like a service whose number of ports and their angles are a hard-coded interface spec; linkers are the cables. Fix the spec and the network topology is locked in, no matter who assembles it. Yaghi's "longer linker, same net" is keeping the topology while swapping the links: architecture is a graph, implementation is filling it with parts. Where the analogy breaks: a crystal has no orchestrator, thermodynamics does the wiring.
  • Complexity · local rules, global order: Robson poured two ingredients into solution, nobody placed anything, and countless molecules still joined the same way into a crystal, the way a few local rules in a cellular automaton grow a whole pattern. This site's 2026 chemistry prize is a different local-to-global story: a slight handedness bias amplified by autocatalysis into a whole flask. The difference is that MOF crystallisation runs toward equilibrium, while the Soai reaction is self-copying far from it.
  • Business · worse on the old metric: Kitagawa was turned down because zeolites already did the job better. That is the shape of Clayton Christensen's disruptive innovation: the newcomer loses on the incumbent's metric (stability, robustness) and wins on a dimension the old scorecard never measured (designability, softness).

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) use metal ions as nodes and organic molecules as linkers to build crystals full of designed cavities.
  2. Until the late twentieth century chemists could make complex single molecules but could not synthesise three-dimensional ordered crystals to a plan.
  3. Making wooden model kits in 1974, Richard Robson realised a structure is already written into where each ball's holes are drilled.
  4. In 1989 Robson combined copper ions with a four-armed molecule and got a porous diamond-like crystal, not the expected tangle.
  5. Early frameworks collapsed when emptied; Kitagawa's 1997 frameworks stayed intact when dried and took up and released gases at room temperature.
  6. Kitagawa proposed that MOFs can be soft: flexible porous crystals change shape as guests enter and leave, which zeolites cannot do.
  7. Yaghi's 1999 MOF-5 has about 2,900 square metres of internal surface per gram, so a few grams hold a football pitch.
  8. Reticular chemistry keeps the nodes and swaps the linkers, producing the same net with pores of chosen size.
  9. MOFs already store toxic doping gases in chip fabs and are in industrial carbon-capture trials; desert water and PFAS removal remain in the lab.
  10. The useful part is the space the walls enclose, the same structure as Laozi's "use of the empty room".

// Think further

Robson had the idea in 1974 and waited ten years. Was that waste, or necessary incubation?
He says the lecture reminded him every year, and he finally went to the bench in somewhat of a state of desperation. Ask whether the crystallography and the ligand chemistry he was working on only came together in the mid-1980s. Many ideas are not ignored; they are waiting for their tools. And the reverse question: is there a thought that resurfaces for you every year?
Maddox called our inability to predict crystal structures a scandal. Did reticular chemistry fix it?
Partly. Reticular chemistry works because it chooses rigid blocks and strong bonds, shrinking the possible outcomes to a few. That makes prediction easy inside a constrained subset rather than in general. Kitagawa's soft frameworks pull the other way: once the skeleton can flex, the tension between design and emergence returns. Compare the software trade of strong constraints for predictability.
Zeolites already absorbed gases, so the reviewers who rejected Kitagawa were not wrong. From the 1990s, could you tell "useless for now" from "useless forever"?
What Kitagawa did next was to spell out what MOFs could do that zeolites could not: combine many kinds of molecules, and be soft. In effect he translated "useless" into a new scorecard. What would a worse-looking newcomer need before it is worth a bet? Does anyone care about the one dimension where it is unique?
