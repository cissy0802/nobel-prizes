PHYSICS · 2022

No one looked, so there was no answer yet

Three experimentalists took a philosophical quarrel between Einstein and Bohr, turned it into an instrument that outputs a number, and then turned the strangeness itself into a tool for quantum communication

for experiments with entangled photons, establishing the violation of Bell inequalities and pioneering quantum information science
2026-11-03 · BigCat

Entanglement was already in the theory in 1935. What won the prize was turning "is the world settled before anyone measures it?" into a question a lab can score.

Split a pair of gloves. Ship one to Beijing and the other to Vienna. The moment Beijing opens its box and finds a left glove, it knows Vienna has the right one. Nothing spooky happened: the answer was fixed when the boxes were packed, and opening one just reveals it.

Quantum mechanics says some particle pairs behave a lot like those gloves, but are not gloves. Before anyone measures, neither "glove" is left or right yet. Measure one and the other's result falls into place, however far apart they are. Einstein found this absurd; his phrase for it, often quoted since, was spooky action at a distance.

For decades this was coffee-break philosophy. Then someone noticed that the glove story and the quantum story predict different numbers for certain measurements.

1935: a question shelved for thirty years

In 1935 Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen set out what became the EPR paradox. Measure one particle and you can predict, with certainty, the result for a distant partner that nobody touched. So that property must have been there all along. Quantum mechanics says it was not definite before measurement. Therefore, they argued, quantum mechanics must be incomplete.

The same year Erwin Schrödinger named the condition of two systems that can no longer be described separately: entanglement.

I would not call that one but rather the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics, the one that enforces its entire departure from classical lines of thought.— Schrödinger, 1935, as quoted in the Nobel committee's scientific background

Bohr disagreed, and most physicists sided with Bohr. The fuller theory Einstein had in mind came to be called local hidden variables: each particle carries an invisible instruction sheet saying what to show for each possible measurement, and nothing far away can change it. That is the glove story. But both views predicted the same results for every known experiment, so there was nothing to decide.

In 1964 John Stewart Bell, a Northern Irish physicist at CERN, found a way to decide. If particles carry instruction sheets and cannot signal faster than light, then the agreement between the two sides' results has a ceiling: a Bell inequality. Quantum mechanics predicts that at certain angles the agreement breaks through it. The committee's verdict is that Bell turned philosophy into empirical science. His version, though, assumed idealised detectors and could not be run as written.

Two years in a basement, and a switch that flips every ten nanoseconds

As a graduate student at Columbia, Clauser failed quantum mechanics twice. Once he read Bell's paper he couldn't let it go, and everyone around him said he was nuts. When he went to see Richard Feynman, he recalls, he was thrown straight out: the predictions of quantum mechanics are perfect, no need to look. In 1969 he, Michael Horne, Abner Shimony and Richard Holt proposed a version that existing technology could test, now known by their initials as the CHSH inequality.

In 1970 he went to Berkeley as a postdoc. Charles Townes had hired him for radio astronomy. An earlier student's apparatus for making photon pairs from calcium atoms was still sitting in the lab, and Townes agreed to let Clauser spend half his time rebuilding it, with graduate student Stuart Freedman. It took the two of them two years in the basement: light kicks a calcium atom, which spits out two entangled photons heading for polarisers on either side. Then about 200 hours of data. The 1972 result: the inequality was violated, and the points fell on the curve quantum mechanics had predicted in advance, not a curve fitted to the data.

I was having fun even though I had destroyed my hero Einstein's work. I was not very happy about that, but that's what the data kept telling me.— John Clauser, nobelprize.org interview, 2022

But the experiment had loopholes. The one Bell worried about most: the polariser angles were fixed in advance. If the source could somehow "know" what each side would measure, an instruction sheet could cheat.

Aspect learned quantum mechanics from a textbook while teaching in Cameroon in place of military service, then came home to France and found Bell's paper. He proposed his scheme in 1976 and carried it out in 1981–82 with Philippe Grangier, Gérard Roger and Jean Dalibard. The problem was speed. A photon reaches a polariser six metres away in about 20 nanoseconds, and nothing mechanical can rotate a polariser that fast. So he put a switch in each beam that sent the photon, mid-flight, to one of two polarisers set at different angles, flipping roughly every 10 nanoseconds. The switch deflected light with an ultrasonic standing wave. When the supplier couldn't make a crystal big enough, he used water instead. The homemade device is now in the Nobel Prize Museum.

The inequality was still violated. The committee says Aspect closed an important loophole, and also notes plainly that his switching was not truly random.

From "it's real" to "it's useful"

Zeilinger went the other way: if entanglement is real, use it. His group made entangled photon pairs by shining a laser into a special crystal, far more efficient than calcium atoms.

In 1997 they achieved quantum teleportation (a group in Rome did so at almost the same time). An unknown quantum state cannot be copied; measuring it destroys it. Teleportation gets around that rule: with a pre-shared entangled pair plus an ordinary two-bit message, a particle's quantum state reappears intact on a distant particle, and the original is destroyed. What travels is a state, not matter.

In 1998 Jian-Wei Pan, Dik Bouwmeester, Harald Weinfurter and Zeilinger showed entanglement swapping: take two separately entangled pairs, bring one photon from each together for a joint measurement, and the two remaining photons, which have never met, become entangled. The same year his group separated two stations by 400 metres and set the measurement angles with fast random numbers, violating Bell's inequality under strict locality conditions.

The most stubborn gap left was the detection loophole: detectors always miss photons, so what if a sly Nature hid the uncooperative ones? Only in 2015–17 did four groups, one led by Zeilinger, close the locality and detection loopholes at once. The Delft team entangled two electrons 1.3 km apart and ran just 245 trials, not overwhelming statistically; the Vienna photon experiment reached 11.5 standard deviations.

A line from a basement to a satellite

Later the loophole question changed costume. It started as "might Nature cheat?" In cryptography it became "might an eavesdropper cheat?"

In 1991 Artur Ekert proposed distributing keys with entanglement: both sides measure at random angles, publish part of the results and run a Bell test. A large enough violation means nobody tampered; the rest becomes the key. In 2006 Zeilinger's group used it to set up a key across 144 km of open air between La Palma and Tenerife in the Canary Islands.

Optical fibre loses about half its photons every 10 km, and since quantum states can't be copied, the signal can't be amplified along the way. One way out is up. China launched the quantum satellite Micius in 2016; Pan's team used it to deliver entangled pairs to two ground sites 1,203 km apart, and later, with Zeilinger's group, used it as a trusted relay to share a key between Beijing and Vienna. The other way out is quantum repeaters, built on entanglement swapping.

In 2018 Peter Bierhorst and colleagues used a loophole-free Bell test to produce 1,024 random bits whose unpredictability rests on the impossibility of faster-than-light signalling. In 2022 three groups demonstrated device-independent quantum key distribution: the key stays secure even if the eavesdropper built your hardware.

◐ Off the podium

The 1972 experiment was also Stuart Freedman's PhD thesis. He died in 2012, and Nobel Prizes are not awarded posthumously; the committee's background names him repeatedly. "I am 100% sure Stuart would have received the prize if only he was still with us," said his longtime Berkeley colleague Dmitry Budker. Bell died in 1990. Horne, Shimony and Holt, the other CHSH authors, are not on the list either.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Honestly, not in your daily messages yet. What exists is a set of demonstrations and early networks: satellite-to-ground keys via Micius, the 144 km link in the Canaries, national testbeds. The more indirect route is quantum computing: qubits made from trapped ions or superconducting circuits (the kind covered in this site's 2025 physics page) do their work through entanglement. The committee frames it as a second quantum revolution, after the first gave us transistors and lasers.

What may change next

Whether quantum key distribution reaches everyday life depends on an unsettled argument. The US National Security Agency's public position is that it does not recommend QKD for national security systems, citing special equipment, cost and insider risk at trusted relays; it prefers "post-quantum cryptography" that runs on ordinary hardware. In 2023 Renato Renner and Ramona Wolf published a rebuttal arguing some objections are unjustified and others likely solvable in the foreseeable future. Quantum repeaters and quantum memories are still in the lab. A global quantum internet is a direction, not a schedule.

What it does not mean

It does not allow faster-than-light messages. Each side alone sees pure randomness; the striking agreement only shows up when the two records are compared afterwards. Teleportation likewise needs that ordinary, light-speed-or-slower message. Nor did it "prove Einstein wrong." Aspect objects to that himself:

So, when people say, Oh, you showed Einstein wrong, I say, Come on, I showed Einstein was great.— Alain Aspect, nobelprize.org interview, 2022

What the experiments exclude is the particular kind of completion Einstein had in mind. And it is not evidence that consciousness creates reality. At the announcement, committee member Thors Hans Hansson called the world of entanglement "certainly not a virtual world of mysticism or science fiction, but the real world we live in."

A way of thinking to borrow

When two worldviews can't be argued apart, find the situation where they predict different numbers and turn the disagreement into a test with a ceiling. The decades of loophole-closing that followed teach a second habit: assume your adversary (Nature, your instrument, an eavesdropper) is as sly as possible, and ask where it could cheat.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Causal inference · common causes and random assignment: a local hidden-variable model is a causal graph in which the two results can be correlated only through a common cause, the instruction sheet. A Bell inequality is the constraint that graph imposes on observed data; breaking it means no common cause can explain the correlation. Bell's key assumption, that measurement choices are independent, is exactly the randomised trial's requirement that assignment not be driven by confounders. Aspect's switches and Zeilinger's random numbers are successively cleaner sources of randomisation.
  • Distributed systems · shared seeds and coordination without messages: two nodes that cannot talk can still make correlated decisions if they agreed on a random seed beforehand. That seed is a hidden variable. Bell says every shared-seed strategy has a ceiling on the CHSH game (2), while entanglement reaches about 2.8. Yet entanglement still carries no bits, so it cannot help a consensus protocol. It buys coordination better than any prior agreement, with zero communication.
  • Security engineering · zero trust: device-independent QKD trusts no hardware, only a statistic: if the measured Bell violation is large enough, it doesn't matter who built the boxes. Zero-trust architecture makes the same move: ignore network location, trust only evidence you can verify every time.
  • Buddhism · emptiness of inherent existence: Madhyamaka holds that things lack a self-nature independent of conditions. What Bell experiments exclude is a world where particles carry measurement-independent properties and are influenced only by their surroundings. The shared point: properties are not the private possessions of objects. Where it breaks: Bell rules out only local realism, and non-local deterministic theories remain possible. Zeilinger, who twice spent a week at the Dalai Lama's residence, warns that some parallels are "wisdom in hindsight" and issued a challenge: "Tell me one thing which we have not discovered yet, and I go to the lab and check it." He says that has not happened.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Entanglement entered quantum theory in 1935; the 2022 physics prize rewarded testing it experimentally and turning it into a tool.
  2. Local hidden variables means particles carry an instruction sheet fixing their results, like a pair of gloves boxed in advance.
  3. In 1964 Bell proved that under any local hidden-variable theory the agreement between distant results has a ceiling that quantum mechanics breaks.
  4. Clauser and Freedman, using photon pairs from calcium atoms in a Berkeley basement, first measured a Bell inequality violation in 1972.
  5. Aspect in 1982 changed measurement settings mid-flight with switches flipping about every 10 ns, closing the locality loophole.
  6. Zeilinger's group achieved quantum teleportation in 1997 and entanglement swapping in 1998, turning entanglement from a test subject into a resource.
  7. The locality and detection loopholes were closed simultaneously only in 2015–17, by four independent groups.
  8. Bell-test-based quantum key distribution draws its security from a physical test; device-independent versions were demonstrated in 2022.
  9. Entanglement cannot send faster-than-light messages: each side alone sees randomness, and teleportation needs an ordinary message too.
  10. A local hidden-variable model is a causal graph with only a common cause, and a Bell test checks that graph against data.

// Think further

What if Clauser's data had obeyed Bell's inequality?
Quantum mechanics would have failed in a regime where it had never been tested. Clauser says people back then didn't grasp the stakes: either quantum predictions were wrong or Einstein's whole picture was. Worth asking: why is an experiment whose answer "everyone knows" still worth running?
Can the "independent choices" assumption ever be fully verified?
You can always imagine a universe that arranged every choice and result from the start. Experiments can only push the source of the choices further away, from switches to random numbers to light from distant galaxies. It is like a clinical trial: randomisation can't logically exclude every confounder, but it makes confounding explanations ever more expensive.
Quantum key distribution or post-quantum cryptography: must we choose?
One bets security on physical law but needs new hardware and trusted relays; the other bets on hard maths problems and runs on existing devices, but wagers that nobody finds a break. Engineering usually answers with layers and combinations. Start from the threat model: who are you defending against, for how many years, on what budget?
