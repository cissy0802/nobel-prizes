How a few extra molecules can leave a whole flask with only one hand

Two chemists made a tiny left–right imbalance amplify itself to nearly 100 percent — the first time anything other than life has managed to go single-handed

Henri B. KaganUniversité Paris-Sud · France · 1/2Kenso SoaiTokyo University of Science · Japan · 1/2
for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis
2026-10-14 · BigCat

Run the same reaction 37 times and it comes out left-handed 18 times and right-handed 19. What decides each outcome is which side happened to be a few molecules ahead at the start.

A right-hand glove will never fit a left hand, however you twist it. The two are mirror images: identical in every measurement, impossible to superimpose.

Plenty of molecules come in the same two versions. And here is the odd part: every amino acid your body builds proteins from is the "left" version, and every sugar in the backbone of your DNA is the "right" one. Yet when chemists make these molecules in a flask without steering, they always get a 50–50 mix. So where did the first living things get a supply of just one hand?

The 2026 chemistry prize goes to two people who made that happen in glassware.

Pasteur's tweezers and a homework problem that sat for a century

It starts with wine. In the mid-19th century Louis Pasteur looked at crystals of tartaric acid under his microscope and saw two kinds, each the mirror image of the other. He sorted them with tweezers, dissolved each pile, and shone polarised light through: one solution twisted the light right, the other left, and mixed together they did nothing. Molecules with handedness came to be called chiral, and a mirror pair enantiomers.

Chemists' flasks, though, stayed stubbornly fair: equal amounts of each hand, a mixture called a racemate. In the early 1900s the German chemist Willy Marckwald used a catalyst that was itself chiral and got slightly more of one hand than the other. The margin was tiny, but it showed one-handed chemistry was not life's private property.

In 1953 Frederick Charles Frank, a theoretical physicist at the University of Bristol, heard from a colleague that chemists still couldn't explain life's asymmetry. He judged the problem trivial, at least in theory, and wrote a short paper. What you need, he said, is a substance that is a catalyst for its own production and an anti-catalyst for the production of its optical antimer. Unpacked, that is three conditions: a reaction biased toward one hand, mutual suppression between the hands, and a product that catalyses its own formation. He closed, laconically: A laboratory demonstration may not be impossible.

The first condition was later honoured by the chemistry prizes of 2001 and 2021. The other two waited more than thirty years, a puzzle lecturers liked to set their students.

Kagan: an impure catalyst that makes a purer product

In the early 1980s Henri Kagan, at Université Paris-Sud, was doing what everyone in the field was doing: trying to make one hand as cleanly as possible. The motivation was concrete. In the thalidomide disaster of the early 1960s, thousands of children were born with birth defects, and analysis pointed to the mirror image of the drug's active molecule.

The standard catalysts had two parts: a metal atom as the engine and a chiral molecule, the ligand, doing the steering. Purity is measured as enantiomeric excess (ee): how far the majority hand outnumbers the minority, from 0 for a 50–50 mix to 100% for a single hand. Everyone assumed a straight line: halve the ligand's ee and you halve the product's.

Kagan didn't rush to the bench. He thought about how the engine actually runs. Metals usually hold several molecules at once. If the metal grabs two ligands from a mixed supply, you get three combinations: right–right, left–left, and the odd one out, left–right. The first two each make one hand. What does the mismatched pair do?

It turned out to work far more slowly. So most of the minority-hand ligands get tied up in sluggish mixed pairs, effectively benched, and the catalysts doing the real work are mostly majority-hand pairs. The product ends up purer than the catalyst. In 1986 Kagan's group reported three reactions in the Journal of the American Chemical Society, and none of their curves was straight: some bowed up, some sagged down. These are non-linear effects.

Twelve years later Kagan and a co-author opened a review with: Who would have thought before 1986 that an enantiomerically impure catalyst could give a product in an asymmetric synthesis with an enantiomeric excess higher than that of the catalyst?

That ticked Frank's second box: the two hands suppressing each other.

Soai: a molecule that photocopies itself

Kagan's paper drew many followers. One was Kenso Soai at the Tokyo University of Science. Studying a reaction with a strong non-linear effect, he noticed that the catalyst and the product looked structurally alike. Could the product be the catalyst, copying itself? That is autocatalysis.

In 1990 he found the first chiral molecule that copied itself, but every copy came out fainter: a product of 86% ee used as catalyst gave new product of only 35%. The arithmetic is unforgiving. If each round keeps 90% of the purity, the next keeps 81%, and so on down. Copying alone erodes; you also need to suppress the rival.

He tried molecule after molecule. In 1995, in Nature, he reported a pyrimidine-containing alcohol that, used as catalyst at just 5% ee, gave product at 55% ee in the first round. Feed that product back in and the second round reached 87%, levelling off around 90% after a few more. For the first time, a single reaction met all three of Frank's conditions. It is now called the Soai reaction.

He kept going. In 2003 his team started from about 0.00005% ee, which by Soai's own reckoning is a surplus of only a handful of molecules among roughly five million of each hand. After three rounds the product was above 99.5% ee, and the winning hand had multiplied about 630,000-fold.

The last step is the startling one: add nothing chiral at all, just mix the starting materials. In theory you should get 50–50. But with enough molecules an exact tie is almost impossible; toss a coin a hundred times and the chance of exactly fifty heads is under one in ten. The Soai reaction is sensitive enough to hear that accident and blow it up. Of the 37 runs he reported, 18 gave one hand and 19 the other, at 15% to 91% ee. This is absolute asymmetric synthesis: asymmetry growing out of symmetry with no chiral guidance from outside.

Why it was a breakthrough, and what it didn't settle

Until then, the idea that life's handedness could arise on its own lived on paper. Kagan and Soai turned it into something you can repeat in a flask: a small bias, plus self-copying, plus mutual suppression, is enough for one side to take everything.

For working synthetic chemists, Kagan's discovery is more like a stethoscope. Whether a reaction's curve bends, and which way, tells you how many molecules are cooperating inside the catalyst. Donna Blackmond and others extended his models, and non-linear effects became a routine tool for working out mechanisms.

The Soai reaction itself became an ultra-sensitive chirality detector. Soai's group used it to show that circularly polarised light, left- and right-handed quartz crystals, even the faint chirality created by where a carbon-13 isotope sits, could each be amplified into a nearly single-handed product.

It is just as important to say what it did not do. The reaction runs on organozinc reagents, and the committee's scientific background is blunt: it is an important proof of concept but not relevant to how biological homochirality emerged in water, and Frank's model is one possible solution among several to an event 3.5–4 billion years ago that we will probably never answer definitively. Even how the Soai reaction works is still contested after thirty years: Scott Denmark's group points to a ring-shaped cluster of four molecules, Oliver Trapp's to a fleeting intermediate.

◐ Off the podium

Soai was not the only one chasing "handedness from nothing." Daniel Singleton ran similar experiments independently, and his first result was a cold shower: in 2002 he ran the reaction 48 times with no chiral additives, every run came out optically active, and the evidence suggested most or all were steered by trace chiral impurities. An amplifier that sensitive amplifies dust too. In 2003, with improved experiments, he got 27 runs of each hand out of 54 and estimated that about 60,000 molecules were enough to decide the outcome, supporting genuine chance symmetry breaking. The committee's background cites both groups side by side.

On announcement day the committee reached Soai but not Kagan, who is 95. Soai, speaking to reporters afterwards: This is not the final answer.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Single-hand drugs. The two mirror forms of a drug are often two different drugs. The US FDA's May 1992 policy on stereoisomeric drugs gives examples: l-propranolol blocks beta receptors, d-propranolol doesn't; the two hands of sotalol do different jobs. The policy expects manufacturers to know the composition of each hand. Asked which drugs draw on this year's discoveries, committee member Peter Somfai declined to name one: I would say all of them, because we use this as a tool.

A concrete case: Merck, making a leukotriene antagonist, prepared a reducing agent from α-pinene of only 70% optical purity and still got product at 95% ee — a positive non-linear effect at work.

What may change next

The most direct impact is on origin-of-life chemistry, all still in the lab. In 2006 Blackmond's group showed an amplification mechanism that works in water, driven by how amino acids split between solution and crystal. In 2024 they reported in Nature that a plausible prebiotic route to peptides actually prefers joining mismatched hands — and that this paradoxically enriches the single-handed chains. In 2025 Trapp's group confirmed the intermediates of the Soai reaction, aiming to design new autocatalytic reactions from that blueprint. Making drugs directly by autocatalysis is not on the horizon: the Soai reaction works on a very narrow range of substrates.

What it does not mean

It does not mean the case of "why life is left-handed" is closed. The press release says we now know how homochirality can emerge. The same committee's scientific background states plainly that this is not the answer to life's origin.

Nor does it mean thalidomide would have been safe as a single hand. Its two forms interconvert in the body; a 2018 paper in Scientific Reports is devoted to this "thalidomide paradox." Going single-handed is not a cure-all.

A way of thinking to borrow

Treat disproportion as a clue, not as noise. When everyone assumed input and output were proportional, Kagan asked what a bend in the line would reveal about the inside. The stretch where a system departs from linearity is often where components are pairing up or competing.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Physics · spontaneous symmetry breaking: cool iron below its Curie point and its tiny magnets must agree on a direction, though the equations prefer none; which one wins depends on random fluctuations. Soai's 18-to-19 is the same story: perfectly symmetric rules, a necessarily asymmetric and random outcome. Iron relies on neighbouring spins aligning each other; the Soai reaction on like-copies-like and unlike-suppresses-unlike. The site's spin systems and Hopfield networks issue has the same structure: which valley of the energy landscape you roll into.
  • Neuroscience · winner-take-all decision circuits: choosing between two options is often modelled as two neural populations, each exciting itself and inhibiting the other. Tilt the evidence slightly and one population runs away while the other is silenced. That is Frank's model exactly: autocatalysis is self-excitation, anti-catalysis is lateral inhibition. The brain has to reset and choose again; the Soai reaction locks in for good.
  • Distributed systems · symmetry-breaking leader election: a ring of identical nodes running the same deterministic program cannot, in the classic result, elect a unique leader, since none has a reason to be special; the standard fix is randomness. The Soai reaction uses thermal noise as a free random source, then autocatalysis turns "who's slightly ahead" into global consensus. Singleton's trap applies too: if your random source is contaminated, the fair election was rigged from the start.
  • Economics · increasing returns and lock-in: in format wars over video tapes or keyboard layouts, whoever sells a little more early attracts more complements and users and ends up owning the market. Positive feedback alone is not enough, though; Soai's 1990 reaction showed that copying without suppressing the rival just fades. Winner-take-all needs the competitor actively squeezed out.

// Think further

If life's handedness came from chance, would a re-run Earth have right-handed life?
The Soai reaction models both answers. With perfect symmetry, outcomes split 50–50; add even a whisper of outside bias (circularly polarised light, a quartz crystal) and the amplifier reliably tips that way. So the question becomes whether early Earth had an outside bias strong enough to swamp random fluctuation. What evidence could tell a fair coin from a loaded one, and how many planets would you need to sample?
Which relationships in your own systems does everyone assume are linear?
Kagan's breakthrough began not with a new experiment but by doubting an untested assumption, that catalyst purity and product purity are proportional, and then asking about the mechanism. Familiar equivalents: double the machines, double the throughput; double the data, double the gain; double the people, double the speed. Where the line bends, components are usually pairing up or contending.
Thirty years on, the reaction's mechanism is still argued over. Has a discovery been explained if no one fully understands it?
As a phenomenon the Soai reaction is thoroughly reproduced, and Frank's three conditions explain it at one level; who does what at the molecular level is still open. Many engineered systems are like this: you know why they work (the feedback structure) without being able to say which line of code mattered at which moment.
