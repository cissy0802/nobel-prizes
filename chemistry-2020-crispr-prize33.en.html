A bacterial grudge became scissors for rewriting genes

Charpentier and Doudna took apart a streptococcal defence against viruses and boiled it down to two parts. Swap a 20-letter "address" and it cuts almost any organism's DNA exactly where you choose

Emmanuelle CharpentierMax Planck Unit for the Science of Pathogens · Berlin · born in France · 1/2Jennifer A. DoudnaUniversity of California, Berkeley · USA · 1/2
for the development of a method for genome editing
Announced 2020-10-07 · 2026-11-14 · BigCat

The scissors cannot rewrite a single letter. The actual writing is done by the cell's own repair crew, which was there all along and is often clumsy.

If you make yogurt or cheese for a living, bacteria are not your problem. Viruses that eat bacteria are. When bacteriophages get into a vat of Streptococcus thermophilus, whole batches are downgraded and yields drop. Scientists at the Danish food-ingredients company Danisco fought this every day.

In 2007 they reported something odd. Bacteria that survived a viral attack had copied a short stretch of the virus's genes into their own genome, like a wanted poster pinned by the door. When the same virus came back, the bacteria recognised it.

Five years later, two scientists took apart the machine behind that poster and turned it into a tool that cuts the DNA of mice, rice and human cells.

The hard part was never cutting. It was finding the spot

Ever since Watson and Crick described the structure of DNA in 1953, scientists have wanted to edit it. The bottleneck was targeting. In mouse embryonic stem cells you could swap a stretch of DNA by homologous recombination, where the cell uses a similar sequence as a template, but it happens so rarely that you had to add an antibiotic-resistance gene just to fish out the few cells where it worked.

Then came zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) and TALENs: a cutting domain bolted onto a protein that reads a particular DNA sequence. They worked. But as the committee's scientific background puts it, their use was limited by the inherent difficulty of designing, synthesising and validating proteins. Every new target meant building a new protein.

Meanwhile an unrelated clue was surfacing in microbes. In 1987 a report noted a strange structure in the E. coli genome: five nearly identical 29-letter sequences separated by spacers of fixed length and varying content. In 1993 Francisco Mojica found similar repeats in a salt-loving archaeon. In 2002 the pattern got its name, CRISPR: clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats. In 2005 several groups noticed that the spacers came from viruses and plasmids; one paper called them a "memory of past genetic aggressions". The 2007 Danisco experiment settled it: add or remove a spacer, and resistance to the matching virus appeared or vanished.

A long winter in Umeå, and a café in Puerto Rico

Charpentier worked on a different streptococcus, S. pyogenes, which causes tonsillitis and, at its worst, earns the name "flesh eater". In 2009 she moved to Umeå University in the north of Sweden. People warned her it was remote. The long dark winter turned out to be good for quiet work.

With colleagues in Berlin she sequenced every small RNA in the bacterium, and up came one that was abundant and completely unknown. It was encoded about two hundred letters from the CRISPR region, and part of it paired neatly with the CRISPR repeats, like two puzzle pieces clicking together. She called it tracrRNA (trans-activating CRISPR RNA) and published in Nature in March 2011: without it, the long RNA transcribed from CRISPR never matured into working short pieces.

That spring she went to a conference in Puerto Rico hoping to meet the Berkeley biochemist Jennifer Doudna, who had spent two decades on RNA and had first heard of CRISPR in 2006. They were introduced in a café on the second day. On the third, walking the cobbled streets of the old city, Charpentier proposed working together.

Their guess was that CRISPR RNA (crRNA) recognises the viral DNA and a protein called Cas9 does the cutting. In the test tube, the two together did nothing. After many failed runs they added tracrRNA, and the DNA was cut in two. tracrRNA had two jobs: helping crRNA mature, and then switching on Cas9.

Then came the decisive move. They fused the two RNAs into one, a guide RNA. They took a gene already sitting in a freezer in Doudna's lab, picked five sites, and rewrote the front of the guide to match each. All five were cut where intended. The paper appeared online in Science in June 2012, and its abstract ends by saying the study "highlights the potential to exploit the system for RNA-programmable genome editing".

From one pair of scissors per target to editing one line of an address

The breakthrough is obvious next to what came before. In ZFNs and TALENs the part that finds the target is a protein, so a new target means a new protein. In CRISPR-Cas9 that part is a piece of RNA, so a new target means rewriting 20 letters, and making a short RNA is far cheaper and faster than designing a protein. Fixed scissors, swappable address: that is what "programmable" means here. The one constraint is a short tag next to the target, the PAM, which for S. pyogenes is any letter followed by two Gs.

After the cut, the cell repairs itself in one of two ways. It can glue the ends straight back, often gaining or losing a letter or two, which breaks the gene: good for switching it off. Or it can repair from a template DNA you supply, which writes in the change you want. Strictly speaking, the scissors only make a wound in the right place; the rewriting is the cell's own repair.

In early 2013 two papers showed it worked in mouse and human cells, one from Feng Zhang's team at the Broad Institute. Yeast, fruit flies, nematodes, zebrafish and Arabidopsis followed. The committee's verdict: genome manipulation is no longer an experimental bottleneck.

Eight years to the prize, eleven to the pharmacy

The scissors were soon refined. Base editing (2016) disabled one of Cas9's blades so it nicks only one strand, and attached an enzyme that turns a C directly into a T, with no double-strand break. Prime editing (2019) went further: the guide RNA itself carries the new sequence to be written in, a kind of search-and-replace.

On 16 November 2023 the UK regulator authorised Casgevy, the first licensed medicine based on CRISPR; the US FDA followed on 8 December for sickle cell disease in patients aged 12 and up. It does not fix the disease-causing mutation. It takes the patient's blood stem cells out, cuts a switch so they start making fetal haemoglobin again, and puts them back. In 2025 a baby in Philadelphia with a rare metabolic disease received, at about seven and eight months old, a base-editing therapy made for that one child, delivered straight into the body in lipid nanoparticles.

The same scissors were used in 2018 by He Jiankui on human embryos, editing a gene that HIV uses to get into cells; twin girls were born. At the end of 2019 he was sentenced to three years in prison and fined 3 million yuan.

◐ Off the podium

A Nobel can go to at most three people, and CRISPR had many more contributors. Lithuania's Virginijus Šikšnys is said to have submitted similar findings earlier than the two laureates, and he shared Norway's Kavli Prize with them. The committee's background also mentions a parallel paper on S. thermophilus, noting that it missed the crucial role of tracrRNA in targeted cutting. Feng Zhang, who brought the tool into human cells, was not included.

The patent fight lasted longer. In February 2022 the US Patent Trial and Appeal Board gave priority for using CRISPR in eukaryotic cells to the Broad Institute, partly because the Berkeley side's 2012 records showed uncertainty about what their results meant. A 2020 analysis noted that in Europe the key patents were held by the Charpentier and Doudna side.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Medicine: Casgevy was approved in the UK and US in 2023 for sickle cell disease and transfusion-dependent beta thalassaemia. About 100,000 people in the US have sickle cell disease. A 2026 NEJM paper on children aged 5 to 11 reported that every child followed for at least 16 months was free of transfusions or of pain crises.

The lab: to learn what a gene does and which disease it touches, the standard move today is to change it with CRISPR in cells or lab animals and watch what happens. The field: researchers have made rice that takes up less cadmium and arsenic, and crops that better resist drought and pests, without the antibiotic-resistance genes older methods required.

What may change next

What is in the clinic now is editing blood stem cells outside the body, and it is not gentle. Patients first need myeloablative conditioning, high-dose chemotherapy that empties the bone marrow. In the children's study every child had a grade 3 or 4 adverse event, and one died of liver disease linked to the chemotherapy. The next step is delivering editors directly into the body, and therapies made for a single patient; the 2025 baby was the first, and the authors themselves say longer follow-up is needed.

What it does not mean

It does not mean designer babies are legal or safe: making heritable changes to the human genome is prohibited in many places, and He Jiankui went to prison for illegal medical practice. It does not mean a one-shot cure: the first approved CRISPR medicine involves harvesting stem cells, editing them, chemotherapy and reinfusion, a treatment on the scale of a bone-marrow transplant. And it is not perfect aim: if the roughly 10-letter "seed" at the front of the guide resembles another spot in the genome, Cas9 can cut there too. That is called off-target editing.

A way of thinking to borrow

When a tool refuses to spread, check whether its recognition and its action are welded together. CRISPR won by pulling them apart: the action is always the same Cas9, and the recognition became a sequence anyone can write.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Computing · stored programs vs. custom silicon: ZFNs and TALENs are like taping out a custom chip for each task. CRISPR is a stored-program machine: fixed hardware (Cas9), the program as data (guide RNA), and a new task is just new data. Where the analogy stops: this computer has exactly one instruction, "cut here".
  • Security · signature databases and self-recognition: the spacers are an antivirus signature database, with a new entry appended for each new virus. But the database is itself stored in the bacterium's DNA, so why doesn't the system attack it? The PAM: invading DNA carries this tag next to the signature, and the bacterium's own CRISPR region does not. It is the same problem as in our 2025 Medicine page, how immunity avoids attacking self, solved here with a three-letter tag.
  • Retrieval · coarse filter, then verify: Cas9 does not compare the whole genome letter by letter. It looks for a PAM first, only then checks the ~10-letter seed next to it, and only if the seed matches does it zip up the rest and cut. That is the same two-stage shape as an inverted-index prefilter followed by re-ranking, and off-target cuts are prefilter false positives from a similar prefix.
  • Evolution · memories that are inherited: bacteria write the viruses they meet into their genome and pass the record on when they divide, so acquired immunity becomes heritable. It recalls Lamarck, except what gets passed on is not a stretched neck but a precise viral sequence.
  • RNA biology · small RNAs that steer proteins: Doudna came to CRISPR from RNA interference. A short RNA steering a protein by base-pairing is the same design as the microRNAs in our 2024 Medicine page, and the 2025 baby's therapy rode in lipid nanoparticles, the same class of carrier as the mRNA vaccines in 2023 Medicine.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. CRISPR began as bacterial adaptive immunity: copy a virus's sequence into the genome, then use it to recognise and cut that virus next time.
  2. In 2007 Danisco showed with a yogurt bacterium that adding or removing a spacer made resistance to the matching virus appear or disappear.
  3. ZFNs and TALENs could also cut DNA at chosen sites, but every new target needed a newly designed protein, which kept them niche.
  4. Charpentier discovered tracrRNA in S. pyogenes and showed in 2011 that CRISPR RNA cannot mature without it.
  5. crRNA plus Cas9 left DNA intact; only adding tracrRNA made Cas9 cut, so tracrRNA has two jobs.
  6. In 2012 Charpentier and Doudna fused the two RNAs into one guide RNA; rewriting 20 of its letters sends Cas9 to any chosen site.
  7. Cas9 only makes a break in the right place; the rewriting relies on the cell's own repair, which either breaks the gene or copies in a template.
  8. Casgevy, approved in 2023, is the first CRISPR medicine: it edits blood stem cells outside the body so they make fetal haemoglobin again.
  9. CRISPR therapy still requires stem-cell harvesting and myeloablative chemotherapy, and heritable human genome editing is banned in many places.
  10. Cas9's search, PAM first and then the seed, has the same shape as a search engine's prefilter and re-rank; off-target cuts are prefilter false positives.

// Think further

Danisco wanted better yogurt; Charpentier wanted to understand a pathogen. Was this tool designed at all?
Lay out the timeline: odd repeats in 1987, "memory" of invaders in 2005, an industrial experiment in 2007, tracrRNA in 2011. Nobody asking those questions was trying to build a gene editor. The decisive step in 2012 was fusing the two RNAs and then deliberately cutting five self-chosen sites: the first time "understand it" became "use it". Perhaps the line between discovery and invention is the moment someone asks "where can I make it cut?"
The patent board said the Berkeley side was "uncertain" in 2012; the Nobel committee gave them the prize. Is that a contradiction?
The patent question is who first definitely possessed a working method in eukaryotic cells. The Nobel question is who revealed the mechanism and saw that it could be programmed. One reads lab notebooks and dates; the other weighs the conceptual step. Two institutions answering different questions can each find a different "first" in the same history.
Casgevy does not fix the mutation; it turns fetal haemoglobin back on. What does that detour tell us?
Correcting a single letter needs a template and efficient precise repair, which is hard in stem cells. Breaking a switch is exactly what Cas9 plus "glue it back" does best. The first approved medicine used the tool in its most reliable mode. Base editing and prime editing can be read as attempts to close the precise-rewrite gap.
