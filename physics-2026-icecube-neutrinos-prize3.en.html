Two kilometres under the South Pole, an eye that looks at the sky through the Earth

A cubic kilometre of ice wired with light sensors catches neutrinos from the deep universe, particles that pass through almost everything

Francis HalzenUniversity of Wisconsin–Madison · USA (born in Belgium) · 1/1
for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin
2026-10-13 · BigCat

When this telescope watches the northern sky, it points down, and lets the whole planet filter out the noise.

Look at the nail on your little finger. In the second it takes to read this sentence, roughly 65 billion neutrinos from the Sun pass through it. You feel nothing. They carry on through your hand, the floor and the entire Earth, and leave from the other side as if nothing had been in the way.

A neutrino has no electric charge and almost no mass, and nearly always ignores matter. That makes it the universe's most honest messenger: it doesn't bend and it isn't absorbed. It also makes it the hardest thing in physics to catch. To record something that ignores everything, you have one crude option: spread an absurdly large net, and wait.

This year's physics prize went to the man who spread that net under the Antarctic ice sheet.

The universe is firing, and nobody can find the gun

Since 1912 we have known that charged particles rain on Earth from space all the time. These cosmic rays are mostly protons, bare hydrogen nuclei, and the most violent of them carry up to a million times the energy of our best accelerators. What builds natural accelerators like that? For more than a century the answer stayed hidden.

The trouble is that protons are charged. Magnetic fields fill the galaxy and the space between galaxies, so a proton is bent again and again, and by the time it arrives it no longer points home. It is like hearing a gunshot in a hall of mirrors. High-energy gamma rays do travel straight, but dust and background light eat them along the way.

Physicists knew that whatever accelerates those protons must also produce high-energy neutrinos, which are uncharged and unabsorbed and point straight back to their source. The catch is how lazy they are. To collect enough of the cosmic ones, a detector needs to be about a cubic kilometre. In 1960 three physicists independently proposed using large volumes of clear water: now and then a neutrino hits a nucleus and knocks out a charged particle, which gives off a faint blue glow in the water called Cherenkov light. The first serious attempt was DUMAND, off Hawaii. Its first string of sensors went down to 4,800 metres in 1993 and failed soon after, while glowing sea creatures and the water's natural radioactivity added noise. The project was cancelled in 1995.

A man who knew nothing about ice

In autumn 1987, Halzen, a Belgian-born particle theorist at Wisconsin, gave a talk at the University of Kansas. A glaciologist in the audience told him that Soviet researchers planned to listen for neutrinos in Antarctic ice with radio antennas. Halzen and two colleagues worked out that the method's threshold was too high for the energies he cared about. Then he had another thought: why not look for the light in the ice itself? Ice is transparent too, and there is a great deal of it at the bottom of the world.

He sent a flurry of emails to John G. Learned, then spokesperson for DUMAND. In June 1988 the two put out a preprint on detecting high-energy neutrinos in deep polar ice. Years later Halzen wrote:

“I suspect that others must have contemplated the same idea and given up on it. Had I not been completely ignorant of what was then known about the optical properties of natural ice, I would probably have done the same.”— Francis Halzen · “Antarctic Dreams” · 1999, quoted in the Nobel Committee's scientific background

In 1991 the team lowered light sensors into existing boreholes in the Greenland ice sheet and showed that particle light could indeed be seen in ice. Then came the South Pole and a forerunner called AMANDA. The drilling technique came from glaciology: a kind of high-pressure hot-water shower head melts a hole a kilometre or more deep, and before it refreezes a cable hung with sensors is lowered in. Halzen calls the sensors a lightbulb in reverse, since they take light in and turn it into an electrical signal. On Christmas Eve 1993 he sat at the dinner table with his laptop on his knees, waiting to hear that the first cable was in the ice.

The first results were disappointing. Between 800 and 1,000 metres the ice was full of tiny air bubbles that scattered the light within half a metre, smearing out any sense of direction. The turn came deeper down. Below about 1,400 metres the ice was astonishingly pure, and blue light travelled hundreds of metres, much further than anyone had expected. On the phone after the announcement, Halzen said this was snow that fell on Antarctica 50,000 years ago. In hindsight it made perfect sense, but at the time there was no guarantee:

“So beyond all the other risks of, whether hot water drilling would work and whether we could beat the backgrounds, this was what really made the project possible and that was pure luck.”— Francis Halzen · telephone interview, nobelprize.org · 2026

AMANDA proved the approach worked, but it was too small. In 1999 a proposal for IceCube, with Halzen as principal investigator, went to the US National Science Foundation. It was approved in 2002 and construction began in 2004. Work is only possible during the Antarctic summer, November to February; 86 holes were drilled between 2005 and 2010, and the detector was finished in 2011. It has 5,160 light sensors hanging between 1,450 and 2,450 metres down, enclosing about a billion tonnes of ice.

Finding a hundred real signals among a hundred million false alarms

Building it was only the start. Every day IceCube records more than a hundred million muons (heavier cousins of the electron) raining down from the atmosphere overhead, all by-products of cosmic rays hitting the air. It also catches around 100,000 neutrinos a year, and most of those are made in Earth's atmosphere too. By the committee's estimate, only about 100 a year come from deep space.

Trick one: use the planet as a filter. Muons cannot cross the whole Earth, so if you keep only tracks moving upward, the atmospheric muons all but vanish. Trick two: post a guard at the door. Treat the outer layer of ice as a veto shell and keep only events that light up out of nowhere inside it; anything that came in from outside is thrown away.

Trick three is the cleverest. Nobody can say that any single event came from the cosmos. But the two kinds of neutrinos have different energy distributions. Atmospheric ones thin out quickly as energy rises; cosmic ones thin out slowly. Above a few tens of TeV (trillion electronvolts), the cosmic ones rise above the background.

In 2013 the team was searching for neutrinos at an even higher energy and stumbled on two events of about 1 PeV (a thousand trillion electronvolts), the most energetic neutrinos anyone had seen. Their paper was cautious: perhaps a first hint of a cosmic flux, not enough for a conclusion. Following that lead back through the same data turned up 26 more, reported as evidence in Science at the end of 2013. In 2014, with a third year of data and 37 events, a purely atmospheric explanation was ruled out at the level physicists call a discovery. The events were spread more or less evenly across the sky, which suggests the sources lie mostly outside our galaxy.

That is what the citation means by "the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin": not the discovery of one particular star, but proof that a flux of high-energy neutrinos really does fill the universe. A new window had opened.

After the window opened: a blazar, a buried black hole, and the Milky Way

On 22 September 2017, IceCube caught a neutrino of about 290 TeV and, within a minute, sent an alert to telescopes around the world. It arrived from within 0.06 degrees of a blazar called TXS 0506+056 (an active galaxy whose jet points at us), which happened to be flaring, its gamma rays up sevenfold. Archived data later showed a burst of neutrinos from the same direction in 2014–15.

In 2022, after examining 110 candidate positions, IceCube saw 79 neutrinos from NGC 1068, a galaxy 46 million light-years away. Its central black hole is wrapped in thick gas and dust; it is faint in gamma rays but bright in neutrinos. Halzen said on the prize-day call that this means the neutrinos are made very close to the black hole, where light cannot escape.

In 2023 the team also saw high-energy neutrinos from the Milky Way itself, and by 2026 twelve years of data made our galaxy the first high-energy neutrino source to pass the discovery threshold, though still without identifying individual points within it. In March this year IceCube published its highest-energy neutrino yet, at about 11 PeV.

To be plain about it: the evidence for NGC 1068 and for TXS 0506+056 has not yet reached discovery level. Gamma-ray bursts were long proposed as sources, yet more than a thousand joint searches have found no matching neutrino. The window is open; most of the view is still blurred.

◐ Off the podium

The idea was never one person's. In 1960 M. A. Markov, F. Reines and K. Greisen each proposed water as a neutrino detector. The 1988 ice preprint carried Learned's name too, though he stayed with DUMAND and never came back to the Pole. DUMAND failed, but the committee notes that its technical legacy was crucial to the underwater telescopes that followed.

IceCube itself is a collaboration of about 450 people from 58 institutions in 14 countries. After the prize, Halzen said that in retrospect the most amazing thing was that the project was funded at all, adding that "The National Science Foundation should take great credit for this", and credited Wisconsin's in-house Physical Sciences Laboratory, where the hot-water drill was designed and built.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

Honestly: almost no consumer product comes out of this. It is basic research. Three things have happened, though. First, there is now a way to watch the high-energy universe in neutrinos at all, and since 2017 IceCube alerts telescopes worldwide within a minute when it sees a promising event. Second, a by-product: to read its own signals, the team mapped the optical properties of deep Antarctic ice in detail, data that glaciologists now use. Third, the engineering itself, hot-water drilling and keeping more than five thousand sensors running at the South Pole with over 99% uptime since 2011, is a reusable capability.

What may change next

The next generation is on its way. In February 2023 KM3NeT, in the Mediterranean, caught a neutrino of about 220 PeV, the most energetic ever seen, when only about a tenth of the detector had been built; the result appeared in Nature in 2025. IceCube hopes to grow into the 8-cubic-kilometre IceCube-Gen2, which the US particle-physics prioritisation panel (P5) recommended funding in 2023, though no construction funding has been publicly settled. Lake Baikal, the coast of British Columbia and the South China Sea all host projects too. The most realistic hope for the next decade is to name one class of cosmic accelerator with discovery-level confidence.

What is still stuck is concrete. Given IceCube's longer exposure and larger area, it should have seen events like KM3NeT's record-breaker, yet it has seen none. That tension currently sits between 2 and 3.5 standard deviations. Some propose new physics; it could also be chance.

What it does not mean

"Ghost particles" are not dangerous radiation; the tens of billions crossing you each second do nothing to you. They are not faster than light either. The more common misreading is that "IceCube found the source of cosmic rays". It proved that cosmic neutrinos exist and produced a few promising suspects, but the century-old case is still open.

A way of thinking to borrow

When no single sample can tell you anything, look at the tail of the distribution. IceCube never pointed at one event and said "this one is cosmic"; it relied on the shape of the whole dataset at high energy. And: look from a direction the noise cannot reach, for instance with the planet in between.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Neuroscience · interaural time difference: with your eyes shut you know a sound came from the left, because your brain compares when the sound wave reached each ear. IceCube finds direction the same way, with five thousand ears instead of two, comparing when blue light reaches each sensor. Different scale, same principle: infer the source from differences in arrival time.
  • Distributed systems · the three pillars of observability: logs, metrics and traces each have blind spots; together they locate the fault. Astronomers now call this multi-messenger work. Cosmic rays carry energy but their directions are scrambled by magnetic fields, gamma rays point true but get absorbed, and neutrinos point true and are never absorbed but arrive about a hundred a year. Each channel covers the others' gaps. TXS 0506+056 was caught exactly this way: a neutrino raised the alarm and optical and gamma-ray telescopes followed up.
  • AI · anomaly detection under class imbalance: of roughly 100,000 neutrinos a year, about 100 are the target, a ratio near one in a thousand, with no labelled positives. IceCube works like fraud detection: hard rules first strip out most of the background (upgoing tracks only, outer veto), then you compare distributions over the remaining population instead of passing judgement on each transaction.
  • Buddhism · dependent origination: a particle that relates to almost nothing is, for us, almost nothing; we know it only through the light left by one rare collision. The teaching that things appear only through conditions becomes, with neutrinos, an engineering problem: the thinner the relation, the more conditions you have to prepare, up to a cubic kilometre of ice.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. Neutrinos carry no charge and almost no mass, travel straight and are not absorbed, which makes them the most honest messengers pointing to cosmic accelerators.
  2. Cosmic rays are mostly protons; being charged, they are bent by magnetic fields and no longer point home by the time they reach Earth.
  3. Catching enough cosmic neutrinos takes a detector a cubic kilometer in size, and DUMAND off Hawaii was cancelled in 1995.
  4. Francis Halzen thought of watching for Cherenkov light in Antarctic ice in 1987, and later said only his ignorance of ice optics kept him from giving up.
  5. Ice at 800 to 1,000 meters is full of bubbles, but below 1,400 meters it is clear enough for blue light to travel hundreds of meters, which was pure luck.
  6. IceCube was completed in 2011: 86 holes drilled with hot water, 5,160 light sensors and about a billion tons of ice.
  7. IceCube looks only at tracks heading upward from the far side of the planet, using the whole Earth as a filter against atmospheric muons.
  8. No single event can be labeled, so cosmic neutrinos show up through an energy distribution that falls off more slowly at the high end.
  9. In 2013 and 2014 IceCube established a flux of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos, while NGC 1068 and TXS 0506+056 are not yet at discovery level.
  10. IceCube infers a neutrino's direction from when light reaches each sensor, the same principle the brain uses to locate sound from the difference between two ears.

// Think further

The prize went to Halzen alone, but IceCube is a 450-person collaboration. In the age of big science, how should we credit a discovery?
The three-person limit naturally favours whoever conceived the idea and carried it for decades. The citation says "decisive contributions", not "the discovery", which already leaves room for the collaboration. The question worth asking is what each choice, crediting the visionary leader or the whole team, teaches the next generation about how science gets done.
Halzen calls the clear ice "pure luck". Had the deep ice been just as murky, where should the project have cut its losses?
The Greenland boreholes and AMANDA's first cables were cheap probes that bought key information about the ice. The real decision point came after the shallow bubbles disappointed: was it worth drilling deeper? Think about projects in your own field that should buy one crucial fact cheaply before placing the big bet.
If no single event can be labelled "cosmic", what exactly was discovered?
A population: an extra flux that atmospheric sources cannot explain. It resembles epidemiology saying a factor raises disease rates while being unable to name which patient it harmed. When is knowledge about a population enough to guide judgements about individuals?
KM3NeT's 220 PeV event and IceCube's silence: new physics, or a statistical coincidence?
Two to three and a half standard deviations is exactly the range where physicists most often get fooled. Many such tensions fade with more data, and a few become discoveries. Arguing won't settle it; more data from both detectors will, which is why next-generation telescopes are designed to work as a network.
