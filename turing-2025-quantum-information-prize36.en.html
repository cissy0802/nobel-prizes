TURING AWARD · 2025

Look once, and you leave fingerprints

A physicist and a cryptographer took quantum mechanics' most annoying habit, that measuring something disturbs it, and turned it into a lock guarded by the laws of physics. Along the way they invented quantum teleportation.

Charles H. BennettIBM Research · sharedGilles BrassardUniversité de Montréal · shared
For their essential role in igniting and shaping the quantum revolution in computer science and in information and communications technology.
2026-11-17 · BigCat

The measurement disturbance physicists spent decades trying to work around is the entire security of this lock.

You post a letter. Somewhere along the way someone steams it open, reads it and glues it shut again. It arrives looking untouched. Digital data is worse: copy a bit string ten thousand times and the original doesn't gain a single scratch. Eavesdropping leaves no trace. Cryptography has assumed this for thousands of years, so every lock ever built has worked on one thing only: making sure the snoop can't understand what they see.

One afternoon in late October 1979, Gilles Brassard, then 24, was swimming off a hotel beach in San Juan, Puerto Rico, when a stranger swam up and, without introduction, started explaining banknotes that could not be forged. The stranger was Charles Bennett. Forty-odd years later, what grew out of that swim won computing's highest award: if the information rides on single photons, looking at it necessarily leaves fingerprints.

Two kinds of lock: one hard to deliver, one nobody had proven

In the 1970s cryptography offered two roads. The first was the one-time pad: both sides hold a string of random digits as long as the message, used once and thrown away. Information theory says it is perfectly secure. The whole problem is delivering the pad. During the Cold War, the keys for the Washington–Moscow hotline were printed on tape and carried across the Atlantic by a diplomat with the briefcase handcuffed to his wrist.

The second road opened in 1976 with Diffie and Hellman's public-key cryptography: two strangers swap a few messages in public and end up with a shared secret; RSA followed a year later. Brassard, a PhD student at Cornell, was handed the paper by his advisor John Hopcroft and found it "so very, very elegant that I dropped what I was doing essentially, and moved on to cryptography." But public-key security rests on an assumption no one has proven: that certain maths problems are hard. The BB84 paper opens by saying so, bluntly:

Conventional cryptosystems such as ENIGMA, DES, or even RSA, are based on a mixture of guesswork and mathematics.— Bennett & Brassard · Bangalore conference paper · 1984

The seed of a third road already existed, unwanted. Stephen Wiesner, Bennett's classmate at Brandeis, wrote "Conjugate Coding" around 1970: use the fact that quantum measurement disturbs what it measures to make a banknote no one can copy. The IEEE Transactions on Information Theory rejected it. Bennett carried the idea around for nearly a decade, telling anyone who would listen. Almost no one would.

Ten minutes in the surf, and a transmission that can't be read in secret

Both men were in San Juan for FOCS, a theoretical computer science conference. "I was trapped, so I listened politely," Brassard told Quanta. Politeness turned into fascination; he also spotted the flaw in quantum money, which is that only the bank that printed a note could check it. By the time they swam back to shore, the key ideas of their first joint paper were settled. That 1982 paper was the first to use the phrase "quantum cryptography."

The turn came shortly after the 1982 conference. As Brassard put it, they realised photons were meant to travel, not to stay put. Instead of locking quantum states inside a banknote, send them flying between two people. By 1983 they had the simplest version: the quantum channel never carries the message, only a string of random key bits. If someone looked, throw the bits away and try again; if not, use them as a one-time pad. Detecting eavesdropping became preventing leaks, and the ancient problem of delivering the pad had a physical answer.

It works like a blind guessing game. For every photon, Alice picks a "basis" at random: either upright (+), where horizontal and vertical mean 0 and 1, or diagonal (×). Bob picks a basis at random to measure each one. When the bases match, his reading is right; when they don't, he gets a coin flip, and the photon's original state is wiped out. Afterwards they talk on an ordinary phone line, announcing only which basis each used, never the readings. Mismatched positions are discarded. What remains is a string both of them hold and nobody else does.

Eve, the eavesdropper, is stuck. She doesn't know each photon's basis, and she can't copy a photon to study it at leisure, because quantum states can't be cloned. The paper works it out: if she intercepts everything in the upright basis and resends it, she learns the right polarisation for half the photons but causes errors in a quarter of the bits Alice and Bob keep. So they compare a random sample in public (the paper suggests a third). If it matches, they use the rest; if not, the whole batch goes.

Hardly anyone cared. An earlier paper of theirs was rejected by STOC and never published. BB84 got its name because the organiser of a session at a December 1984 conference in Bangalore, India, invited Brassard to speak, so the paper never went through submission at all.

32.5 centimetres, and a machine perfectly secure against the deaf

With the theory ignored, they built a machine. Bennett and his summer student John Smolin did the hardware; Brassard's students wrote the software; there was essentially no budget. Bennett and Smolin bought black velvet at a fabric store to block stray light, telling a baffled clerk it was for quantum cryptography. In late October 1989, ten years to the day after the beach, the first secret quantum transmission in history worked, over 32.5 centimetres.

Brassard admits the prototype was "mostly a joke." Its biggest part was the power supply feeding about a thousand volts to the Pockels cells that rotate the photons' polarisation, and different voltages hummed differently, so you could hear zeros and ones. It was unconditionally secure only against an eavesdropper who happened to be deaf. Yet the joke made physicists pay attention. In 1991 Artur Ekert proposed a different scheme in Physical Review Letters, built on entanglement and Bell's inequality, and physics took up the field.

In 1993 Bennett and Brassard, with Crépeau, Jozsa, Peres and Wootters, proposed quantum teleportation. Alice and Bob share an entangled pair in advance. Alice makes a joint measurement on her unknown quantum state and her half of the pair, then tells Bob two ordinary bits over the phone. Bob applies a simple operation and the state appears in his hands, while the original is destroyed. No cloning, and nothing faster than light, because those two bits travel the ordinary way. In 1997 the groups of Anton Zeilinger and Francesco De Martini made it work; Zeilinger shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics.

What the award recognises is a direction of thought. Bennett was recruited to IBM in 1972 by Rolf Landauer, who held that information is a physical quantity governed by the laws of nature. In 1982 Bennett reinterpreted Maxwell's demon, locating its unavoidable cost in erasing information. While most people treated quantum effects as noise to suppress as chips shrank, these two treated them as a resource. In 1994 Peter Shor showed a quantum computer could factor large numbers quickly, threatening RSA, and the fringe idea turned urgent overnight.

From the desktop to a satellite, and back to the lab bench

Quantum key distribution then left the lab. In 2004 Vienna saw the first bank transfer protected by a quantum key. In 2006 Zeilinger's group used Ekert's scheme to set up a key between two of the Canary Islands, 144 km apart through open air. In 2007 Los Alamos and NIST ran BB84 over 148.7 km of fibre.

Distance is the hard wall. The Nobel committee's background notes put it plainly: in optical fibre, on average every second photon is lost over 10 km. Classical networks fix this with amplifiers, but amplifying means copying, and quantum signals can't be copied. One way out is up. China launched the Micius satellite in 2016; Jian-Wei Pan's team distributed entangled photons between ground stations 1,203 km apart, and later, with Zeilinger's group, used the satellite as a trusted relay to share a key between Beijing and Vienna. "Trusted" means the satellite itself knows the key.

Attackers improved too. In 2010 Lydersen, Makarov and colleagues used tailored bright light to remote-control the single-photon detectors of two commercial systems, taking the full key without a trace. The proof holds for ideal devices; real detectors have gaps. In 2022 three groups used loophole-free Bell tests to run "device-independent" key distribution, secure even if Eve can touch the hardware. For now these are experiments.

◐ Off the podium

Stephen Wiesner, where all of this started, did not live to see the award. After "Conjugate Coding" was rejected he left physics in the early 1970s for the California counterculture, later moved to Israel, and worked in construction and as a surveyor. The paper finally appeared in SIGACT News in 1983, prompted by Bennett and Brassard's 1982 paper. In 2006 he shared the Rank Prize in optoelectronics with them, and in 2019 the Micius Quantum Prize. He died in Jerusalem on 12 August 2021.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

To be honest, your banking app and your messages don't use BB84 today. It needs dedicated fibre or satellite links and special detectors, and it runs in a handful of banking, government and research networks, with commercial kit from vendors such as ID Quantique in Geneva and Toshiba. Closer to you is its twin problem: the same Shor algorithm is forcing the whole internet to change its locks. On 13 August 2024 NIST published its first three post-quantum cryptography standards (FIPS 203, 204 and 205), after a process begun in 2015 that weighed 82 candidate algorithms from 25 countries, and urged administrators to switch "as soon as possible." The locks your devices quietly swap in over the next few years will mostly be mathematical ones, not quantum ones.

What may change next

Done already: fibre links of a few hundred kilometres, satellite trusted relays. Still in the lab: quantum repeaters that pass entanglement along a chain, which need reliable quantum memory; device-independent key distribution; and the higher-orbit satellite the Nobel notes describe as under construction, aimed at distributing entangled photons directly over 10,000 km. IBM plans to deliver Starling, a large fault-tolerant quantum computer, to customers in 2029; that is a company roadmap, not an achievement.

What it does not mean

"Quantum encryption is unbreakable" is wrong. The proofs cover ideal devices, and the 2010 bright-light attack is the counterexample. It also provides no authentication: you still have to know it's really Bob on the line. In August 2025 the UK's National Cyber Security Centre said it will not support QKD for government or military use and named post-quantum cryptography the primary defence. "Teleportation moves people, faster than light" is wrong too: only a quantum state moves, the original is destroyed, and two ordinary bits still have to make the trip. And a detected snoop can still spoil things. As Brassard puts it: "The worst the enemy can do by eavesdropping is denial of service."

A way of thinking to borrow

Instead of only asking how to stop anyone looking, ask whether you can make looking leave marks, then throw away whatever was seen. BB84 doesn't block eavesdropping. Its security comes from detect and retry, not from a wall.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Distributed systems · the double-spend problem: digital money's core difficulty is that bits copy, so the same coin can be spent twice; Bitcoin answers with a ledger the whole network agrees on. Wiesner's quantum money takes the other road: the note is physically uncopyable, so no ledger is needed. Same problem, one solved by consensus and one by no-cloning. The analogy breaks exactly where Brassard poked it on the beach: only the issuer can verify a quantum note, which hides the ledger back inside the bank.
  • Security engineering · canaries and sampling audits: BB84 sacrifices a random third of its bits to estimate whether the channel was touched. That is the structure of a honeytoken in a database or a random spot-check of receipts: spend a small, expendable sample to buy confidence about the whole. The difference is that BB84's guarantee comes from physics, not from the attacker happening not to notice.
  • Thermodynamics · Landauer's principle and Maxwell's demon: erasing a bit costs heat, measuring a photon costs disturbance. Information processing is not free abstract manipulation but a process the laws of physics keep accounts on. BB84 runs those accounts in reverse and makes the eavesdropper pay for her own measurement.
  • This site · the 2022 and 2025 physics prizes: the 2022 prize turned Bell's inequality from philosophy into experiment, and the committee itself says the main point of loophole-free Bell tests is more secure key distribution. The superconducting circuits of the 2025 prize are the foundation of today's quantum computers, the very machines that make RSA unreliable and a physical lock necessary. Put together they form a loop: build quantum machines, they break the old locks, and that calls for new ones.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. In the 1970s cryptography had two roads: the one-time pad, perfectly secure but hard to deliver, and public-key systems, convenient but resting on unproven mathematical hardness.
  2. Around 1970 Stephen Wiesner proposed uncopyable banknotes based on quantum measurement disturbance; the paper was rejected and only published more than a decade later.
  3. In 1979 Bennett swam up to Brassard on a Puerto Rico beach to explain quantum money, and their collaboration began there.
  4. The key move in BB84 is that the quantum channel carries only a random key: discard it if anyone looked, use it as a one-time pad if not.
  5. In BB84 both sides pick bases at random, then reveal only the bases, never the readings, and keep the bits where the bases matched.
  6. An eavesdropper can neither know the bases nor clone the photons, so intercepting and resending creates about a quarter errors in the kept bits and gives her away.
  7. The first quantum key distribution prototype in 1989 spanned 32.5 centimetres and hummed audibly with its zeros and ones, yet it got physicists to take the idea seriously.
  8. Quantum teleportation (1993) moves a quantum state using shared entanglement plus two classical bits, destroying the original, with no cloning and nothing faster than light.
  9. Quantum key distribution is not unbreakable: real detectors can be remote-controlled with bright light, it offers no authentication, and post-quantum cryptography is what protects ordinary users.
  10. Quantum money prevents double-spending through physical uncopyability, while Bitcoin does it with a consensus ledger: two answers to the same problem.

// Think further

BB84's security is "guaranteed by physics"; RSA's is "guaranteed by nobody knowing how to solve it." Which guarantee deserves more trust?
The physics guarantees an ideal model: perfect photon sources, detectors that behave like the textbook. The 2010 bright-light attack broke no law of physics; it slipped through the gap between device and model. Mathematical locks are weak in their assumptions, quantum locks in their implementation. A better question may be: at which layer is an attacker more likely to strike?
The UK's NCSC won't support quantum key distribution for government or military systems, while China is building satellites and backbone networks. Is that a technical disagreement, or something else?
The NCSC's reasons are concrete: no authentication, special hardware, more complexity means more attack surface, while post-quantum cryptography fits into existing software. The other side may weigh long-term distrust of mathematical assumptions and a home-grown technology path more heavily. Why does the same physics produce opposite answers on different cost-benefit sheets?
Teleportation needs shared entanglement first, then two classical bits. What exactly does the entanglement contribute?
Think of the entanglement as a prepaid deposit, with the two classical bits telling Bob how to cash it. Without the entanglement, two bits are nowhere near enough to describe a quantum state; without the bits, Bob holds only noise. It resembles pre-sharing randomness in a distributed system so that very little communication is needed later, except that this deposit can't be copied and is spent on use.
