PHYSIOLOGY OR MEDICINE · 2021

Chili doesn't heat your mouth. It fools a thermometer.

One used hot peppers, the other a glass needle, to find the molecular switches that turn heat, cold and touch into nerve signals

David JuliusUC San Francisco · 1/2Ardem PatapoutianScripps Research · 1/2
for their discoveries of receptors for temperature and touch
2026-11-07 · BigCat

As far as your nerves are concerned, a chili pepper and a flame are the same thing.

Take a bite of something very spicy and your mouth is on fire; your forehead may start to sweat. Suck a mint, breathe in, and your mouth turns icy. Put a thermometer in your mouth at either moment and the reading won't budge.

The sweating was studied as early as the 1950s: when hot peppers touch the lips and mouth, the head sweats. Your body isn't feverish, yet it handles the moment as if it were too hot.

What's being fooled? Two molecules. One opens only at temperatures you feel as painfully hot; the other opens when things get cool. Chili pries open the first, mint the second. The people who found them followed the same question to the molecule that lets you feel a hug, and know where your hand is with your eyes shut.

Descartes drew a thread. For three centuries nobody found its end.

In the 17th century René Descartes sketched a foot near a fire: particles of flame tug a thread running from the skin to the brain, and the brain knows. Crude mechanics, but the right question: how does heat out there become a signal in here?

Progress came along the "thread". In the 1880s researchers found separate spots on the skin, some answering only to touch, others only to cold or heat. The 1944 Nobel Prize went to Joseph Erlanger and Herbert Gasser for showing that nerve fibres differ in speed and firing threshold, which let scientists match fibre types to touch, temperature and the sense of body position.

The wires were found; the transducer at their tips stayed blank. Nerves speak only electricity. Heat and pressure are physical quantities. Something has to translate, and the bet was on an ion channel.

Ion channel: a protein forming a tiny gate in a cell's membrane. When it opens, charged ions rush in and the nerve ending fires a burst of electrical pulses. Different gates are pushed open by different things, some by chemicals, some by voltage. The open question: which proteins are the gates for temperature and for pressure?

Touch was the harder half. In the late 1980s Ching Kung and Boris Martinac found channels in E. coli that open when stretched, which keep bacteria from bursting in rainwater. But candidates found in worms and flies didn't seem to matter much for touch in vertebrates. Into the 2000s, how mammals feel a touch remained a mystery.

Addition: keep adding genes to a cell until it flinches at chili

San Francisco, late 1990s. Capsaicin, the pungent compound in chili, was known to excite pain nerves, but nobody knew which molecule it acted on. Julius decided to come at temperature sideways, through the pepper. As he put it on the morning of the prize:

we sort of, kind of, did an end run around the problem, by turning to some natural product pharmacology— David Julius · telephone interview, nobelprize.org · 2021

He and his postdoc Michael Caterina took a blunt approach. From rodent dorsal root ganglia (clusters of sensory nerve cells beside the spinal cord) they copied out the active genes into a library of millions of DNA fragments. Then they took cultured cells that ignore capsaicin, slipped the fragments in batch by batch, added capsaicin and watched for any cell that lit up with incoming calcium. When a batch lit up, they split it and tested again, and again, until a single gene was left.

It encoded an ion channel nobody had seen, a member of the TRP family, first called VR1 and later TRPV1. Then Julius tried something besides chili: heat. Above roughly 40°C the channel opened, a threshold close to where people start to feel heat as pain. The 1997 Nature paper said, with restraint, that it suggests a role as the body's transducer of painful heat.

Later work confirmed it. TRPV1 sits on pain nerves, not on the nerves for touch and posture, and it is the only receptor capsaicin activates. So nothing in your mouth gets hotter when you eat chili. The gate that reports "scalding" has simply been picked with a chemical key.

Subtraction: switch off 72 genes one by one, down to the last

With the heat gate found, the touch gate was still empty. The difficulty: pressure has no capsaicin, no ready chemical key to fish with.

La Jolla, Scripps Research. Patapoutian and his postdoc Bertrand Coste first hunted for the right cell and found Neuro2A, a mouse neuroblastoma line. Press its membrane gently with a glass micropipette and a current appears. A cell that reliably "rings when poked" allowed the reverse experiment: silence suspect genes one at a time, and see which one makes the poke go quiet.

They listed 72 candidates, all proteins likely to span the membrane, some known channels, some of unknown function, and silenced each with RNA interference (a way of temporarily muting a specific gene). Nothing, nothing, nothing. The last gene on the list, known only as FAM38A, abolished the current. They named it PIEZO1, from the Greek for pressure. Put it into cells that ignored poking, and they started to ring. Sequence similarity led to a second gene, PIEZO2, abundant in sensory neurons. The paper appeared in Science in 2010.

PIEZO resembles no known channel. Its subunits, about 2,500 amino acids threading the membrane 38 times, are the largest transmembrane ion channel subunits found to date. The structures solved later look like a three-bladed propeller: curved blades dimple the membrane into a tiny bowl, and when the membrane is stretched flat the blades flatten too and the central pore opens.

One door opened onto a whole corridor

In 2002 both labs, independently, used menthol to fish out the cold receptor TRPM8. People start to feel harmless cool at about 28°C and can detect skin temperature changes as small as 0.5°C.

Temperature soon proved to be reported by a team, not a single switch. Mice lacking TRPV1 were only slightly dulled to acute heat. In 2011 the Voets group found a second heat sensor, TRPM3; later work showed painful heat relies on a triad, TRPV1, TRPM3 and a third channel, TRPA1. Warmth is subtler still: the brain reliably reads "warm" only when warm-sensing fibres fire and cold-sensing fibres are suppressed at the same time.

Pressure went further. In 2014 Patapoutian's team removed PIEZO2 from adult mice's sensory neurons and from the Merkel cells in their skin; the mice lost light touch almost entirely, while their sense of heat and cold stayed intact. In 2015 they showed PIEZO2 is also the main transducer for proprioception, the sense of where your limbs are: without it, mice moved in a badly uncoordinated way. In 2016 Chesler and colleagues at the US National Institutes of Health described two patients without working PIEZO2 in the New England Journal of Medicine. They had lost discriminative touch and much of their proprioception, their movements were unsteady and markedly worse with eyes closed, yet they could walk, talk and write.

Then PIEZO turned up where nobody expected it. In the lungs, PIEZO2 senses the stretch of a deep breath and triggers a reflex that keeps the lungs from over-inflating; mice lacking it during development die at birth in respiratory distress. The reflex that monitors arterial blood pressure needs PIEZO1 and PIEZO2, and bladder control needs PIEZO2. PIEZO1 lets red blood cells sense squeezing and adjust their volume, and Patapoutian's team found that a PIEZO1 variant common in African populations dehydrates red cells and lowers the risk of severe malaria. On the night of the prize he said:

Nobody ever could have thought that pressure sensing is related to these processes.— Ardem Patapoutian · telephone interview, nobelprize.org · 2021

The committee paired the two because they answered two halves of one question: how the physical world becomes nerve signals, one for temperature, one for force. A gate pushed open by a physical stimulus is also the star of this site's 2026 Medicine prize on optogenetics, except that gate answers to light, and scientists turned it around to write signals into neurons rather than read them.

◐ Off the podium

Neither key paper's first author was on stage: Michael Caterina for TRPV1, Bertrand Coste for PIEZO. The committee's scientific background names both. The cold receptor TRPM8 was a dead heat, found independently by two labs in 2002.

Patapoutian was born in Beirut in 1967 and as a young man left war-torn Lebanon for Los Angeles. On the night of the announcement, at 2 a.m., his phone was on Do Not Disturb and Stockholm couldn't get through. They reached his 92-year-old father, who called him.

◎ What it means for the rest of us

Already around you

A chili patch. Qutenza, a prescription patch with 8% capsaicin, has been approved in the US since 2009 for pain after shingles (postherpetic neuralgia), and in July 2020 for diabetic nerve pain in the feet. The maker says one localized procedure may give up to three months of relief. It treats pain with the thing that causes it, and its target is TRPV1.

An eye drop that feels cool. In May 2025 the FDA approved Alcon's Tryptyr (acoltremon), the first drug designed to activate the cold receptor TRPM8. It stimulates nerves in the cornea so the eye makes more of its own tears, for dry eye disease. In two phase 3 trials with more than 930 patients, 42.6% and 53.2% of users had a clear rise in tear production at day 14, against 8.2% and 14.4% on the placebo.

What may change next

The committee points to treatments for chronic pain, and Julius said on prize day he hoped for non-opioid ways of dealing with pain. That road has proved harder than it looked. Amgen's TRPV1 blocker AMG 517 reached phase 1 and raised volunteers' body temperature markedly; after a molar extraction some passed 40°C. The 2008 paper concluded such drugs cannot be developed for systemic use as stand-alone agents. TRPV1, it turned out, also helps regulate body temperature. What has actually reached the market works locally, in a patch or an eye drop; the committee's background mentions no marketed PIEZO drug.

What it does not mean

Not one molecule per sensation. Mice lacking TRPV1 are only slightly dulled to acute heat, and "warm" takes two kinds of fibre to judge. PIEZO2 handles light touch but not the pain of a pinch; the 2014 paper already noted that painful mechanical sensing must rely on other, still unknown channels. Not that mint cools you or chili warms you: they change the report, not the temperature. And not that pain is solved: finding a door is not the same as closing it safely.

A way of thinking to borrow

No key? Build a lock first. Julius had a key, capsaicin, so he could add. Patapoutian had none, so he first found a cell that rang when poked, turning a vague phenomenon into a signal he could read again and again, and only then could he subtract. When you're stuck, ask whether you have a reliable readout yet.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • Debugging · forward screens and bisection: Julius's method is "drop a batch of config into a clean environment; if the feature works, halve the batch and retry", a search for a sufficient condition. Patapoutian's is disabling plugins one at a time until the bug vanishes, a search for a necessary one. Both need a cheap, repeatable pass/fail signal (a calcium flash, a poke current), just as git bisect needs a test that runs on its own. The difference: 72 genes can't be bisected, so they went one by one.
  • Security · authenticating by channel, not content: the brain decides "hot" by which nerve is firing, not by what the stimulus was. Capsaicin opens the same gate on the same pain nerve where TRPV1 sits, so the message goes out on the "scalding" line and the receiver can't tell. That's the structure of a system that checks only the source port, never the payload, and gets fooled by a forged packet. Adversarial examples are the same: a classifier keys on features, and features can be triggered without the real world behind them.
  • Distributed systems · redundancy and graceful degradation: patients without PIEZO2 are much less steady with eyes closed, yet with eyes open they walk and write. One sensor fails; the system shifts weight to vision and keeps serving in degraded mode.
  • Buddhism · contact and feeling: in dependent origination, sense organ, object and consciousness meet to give "contact" (sparsha), and contact conditions "feeling" (vedana): pleasant, painful or neutral. TRPV1 sits right at that interface. Below about 40°C temperature is a physical quantity; once the gate opens, the same molecular motion becomes the painful feeling of "scalding". Where the analogy stops: contact in Buddhist terms includes consciousness, and an ion channel is aware of nothing.

◆ Ten takeaways

  1. David Julius and Ardem Patapoutian shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering receptors for temperature and touch.
  2. Nerves speak only electricity; heat, cold and pressure are physical quantities, and ion channels in the cell membrane translate between them.
  3. Julius ran an addition screen with capsaicin, adding genes to chili-blind cells batch by batch until TRPV1 emerged.
  4. TRPV1 opens above about 40°C, close to the threshold of heat pain, and capsaicin simply picks that same gate.
  5. Patapoutian ran a subtraction screen on poke-sensitive cells, silencing 72 candidate genes; the last one was PIEZO1.
  6. PIEZO2 is the main transducer for light touch and proprioception; people lacking it move unsteadily, worse with eyes closed.
  7. The cold receptor TRPM8 was found with menthol, and temperature is encoded by several TRP channels together, not one switch.
  8. PIEZO channels also govern breathing, blood pressure, the bladder and red blood cells; one PIEZO1 variant is linked to lower severe-malaria risk.
  9. A capsaicin patch and a TRPM8-activating dry-eye drop are on the market, but systemic TRPV1 blockers stalled because they raise body temperature.
  10. The brain judges a sensation by which line fires, so chili can impersonate heat, like a forged packet fooling a port-only check.

// Think further

If you wore a glove that measured temperature precisely, would your "scalding" and the glove's "45°C" be the same thing?
TRPV1 says "scalding" is the moment a gate opens, not the temperature itself; chili can produce it at 37°C. So is a sensation reporting the world, or reporting whether a certain door is open? Compare an alert in a monitoring system: it says a threshold was crossed, which isn't the same as something actually being wrong.
What if the answer hadn't been on Patapoutian's list of 72?
A subtraction screen can only find answers inside the range you guessed in advance. The list was chosen as "likely to span the membrane", and PIEZO, whose function nobody knew, happened to be on it. How is your own suspect list generated when you debug? The hardest bugs often sit outside it.
TRPV1 blockers raise body temperature. If one molecule handles both heat pain and body temperature, what does that mean for drug design?
Biology reuses parts, so blocking one function tugs on another. That may be why the first successes were local: a patch, an eye drop, confining the effect to one place and keeping side effects out. The software analogy is shared global state: change it once and everything feels it.
