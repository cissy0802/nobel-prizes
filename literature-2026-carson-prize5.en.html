LITERATURE · 2026

She translated the Greek fragments, gaps and all

A classics professor who splices broken ancient poems, grief, heartbreak and scholarly footnotes into forms that had no names: a novel in verse, an elegy in a box, a stack of chapbooks you can read in any order

Anne CarsonCanada · 1/1
for her bold and inventive oeuvre that, in playful dialogue with the classical tradition, has created new forms for contemporary literature
2026-10-15 · BigCat

Asked why the classics are relevant to people today, she said they aren't. "I'm interested in us being relevant to them."

In 2000 a woman picks up the phone. The caller is a sister-in-law she has never met: her brother has just died in Copenhagen, in the bathroom of his home. He had fled Canada in 1978 to avoid prison and had barely been heard from since. Not long before, he had called her for the first time in over twenty years, and they had agreed to meet. Her trip to Denmark was a week away.

She later made a book for him. Calling it a book undersells it: it is a box. Lift the lid and you find a single long sheet folded like an accordion, about forty feet when stretched out. Each left-hand page holds one Latin word and its dictionary entry; each right-hand page holds scraps of photographs, fragments of his letters, her own memories. The back of the box reads:

"When my brother died I made an epitaph for him in the form of a book. This is a replica of it, as close as we could get."

The book is called Nox. Its maker, Anne Carson, is this year's Nobel laureate in literature.

A Sappho in a mall bookstore, and Greek over lunch

Carson was born in Toronto in 1950. Her father worked for a bank and was moved often, so she grew up in a string of small Ontario towns. Around 1965, in a bookstore in a Hamilton shopping mall, she found a bilingual edition of Sappho, Greek on the left, English on the right, and "it just looked so fascinating I thought I should learn this." The next year the family moved to Port Hope, where the high-school Latin teacher, Alice Cowan, happened to know Greek and offered to teach her on her lunch hour. "So I owe my career and happiness to Alice Cowan," Carson said decades later.

The path after that was academic: classics at the University of Toronto, a doctorate in 1981, then teaching at McGill, Princeton, Michigan and elsewhere. She writes in English, but her real home language is closer to etymology. The Nobel committee notes that she herself names the history of words as the starting point for everything she writes.

For years nobody published her poems. She was in her early forties when a small Canadian press brought out her first book of poetry, Short Talks (1992). One piece, "On Major and Minor," lists the major things — wind, evil, a good fighting horse, prepositions, inexhaustible love — and the minor ones: dirt, the names of schools of philosophy, the correct time. That deadpan mischief became her signature.

She now spends part of each year in Iceland, where she has become a citizen. On the morning of the prize she was in her kitchen in Reykjavík, eating crackers, drinking tea, reading the London Review of Books; she noticed the floor needed sweeping and got up to sweep it. The phone kept ringing and she kept switching it off — "I just always turn off the phone when it rings" — until her husband, Robert Currie, made her call back.

Four words in the citation, and what each points to

"Classical tradition" does not mean decorative allusion. Her versions of Sappho, Sophocles and Euripides, the committee says, "may be regarded as independent works in their own right." Of Sappho's nine books, roughly three per cent survives, mostly on scraps of papyrus. In If Not, Winter (2002) Carson did something that looks clumsy: wherever the Greek is missing, the English shows an empty bracket. Brackets, she wrote, "imply a free space of imaginal adventure." The classicist Emily Wilson, reviewing it in the LRB, put it this way: "The great thing about this translation is its poverty."

"Dialogue" runs both ways. In her phone interview with the Nobel Prize, asked why the old stories matter today, she answered: "It isn't. I don't think I'm interested in relevance. I'm interested in us being relevant to them." She is not dragging Homer into the present to make him relatable; she is setting today's breakups, abuse and aging mothers in front of Homer and Sappho to see whether they hold up.

"Playful" covers the irony and the collisions. In her first scholarly book, Eros the Bittersweet (1986), she describes a "stereoscopic vision" that can "hold in equipoise two perspectives at once." Hence Simonides read alongside Paul Celan, and Thucydides in conversation with Virginia Woolf.

"New forms" is where the weight falls. The Beauty of the Husband (2001) is subtitled "A Fictional Essay in 29 Tangos," each tango opening with lines from Keats. Nox (2010) is a box. Float (2016) is twenty-two chapbooks packaged together, to be read in whatever order you like.

A red monster, a kitchen table, sixty-three Latin words

Autobiography of Red (1998). The Greek poet Stesichoros wrote a long poem about Geryon that now survives only in fragments. In the myth Geryon is a winged red monster on a red island; Herakles, on his tenth labor, comes for the cattle and kills him almost in passing with an arrow. Carson turns the walk-on into the lead. Her Geryon is a present-day boy who happens to be red and winged, bullied by his older brother, writing his autobiography from the age of five, later obsessed with photography, in love with a careless drifter named Herakles who leaves him. Years later they meet again in Buenos Aires, and the three of them — Herakles has a new boyfriend — head for a volcano in Peru.

The form is the point. The story runs through 47 short chapters, many titled with a single word, told in the third person in alternating long and short lines. Carson first tried it as prose: "whole paragraphs of prose," she told Brick, "a bit hellish." She had wanted to write "an Arthur Hailey novel that people would read in airports," and couldn't; then, "messing around with the lines," she found the long-short couplets, and "the verse form eventually extracted itself." Around the novel she wraps a mock-solemn scholarly introduction, her own translations of the fragments, and a closing fictional interview with Stesichoros. Myth, translation, essay and novel share one spine.

"The Glass Essay" (1995). Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, says it was his first, unforgettable encounter with Carson. A woman whose lover has just left her goes to visit her lonely mother on the moors, carrying Emily Brontë in her luggage. Three solitary women — mother, daughter, and Brontë on the page — sit around a kitchen table. It is lineated like a poem, plotted like a story, and argued like a critical essay on Brontë. She names its source plainly:

"I know my source…it is a moment like no other, / when one's lover comes in and says I do not love you anymore"— "The Glass Essay" · 1995 (as quoted by Charles Simic, NYRB 2005)

Nox (2010). Back to the box. Its skeleton is poem 101 by the Roman poet Catullus, an elegy for a brother who died far from home: sixty-three Latin words. Carson gives each word its own left-hand page, made up to look like an entry in an old, yellowed dictionary, and fills the facing page with her own fragments. The layout comes from a lifetime of bilingual editions: "you get used to thinking in the little channel in between the two languages where the perfect language exists." The definitions are, in her words, "a bit fiddled with": nox, night, never appears in poem 101, yet phrases with night keep slipping into the entries. She set out to fill the elegy with light and admits she couldn't:

"Human words have no main switch."— Nox · 7.1 · 2010 (as quoted in the Harvard Divinity Bulletin)

Why her, and why 2026

Carson had been on Nobel shortlists for years. AFP reported that some observers doubted the Academy would honor another Canadian so soon after Alice Munro in 2013. The other pressure was just as real: of 122 literature laureates since 1901, only 19 have been women; after the 2018 sexual-assault scandal that nearly broke the Swedish Academy, every other laureate has been a woman. With Hungary's László Krasznahorkai named in 2025, many critics expected a woman this year.

That explains the gender, not the choice. "Nobody writes like her," committee member Steve Sem-Sandberg said after the announcement, "and nobody would even, I think, dare to do this in this way." The committee's own account stresses two things: that she broke away from lyric poetry bound to the narrative self and opened new forms in its place, and that her collaborations with visual artists, choreographers, and opera and theater directors have carried her work deep into the international art scene since the 1990s, well beyond poetry circles.

Her Nobel Lecture is due in December and had not been delivered when this page was written.

◐ Off the podium

Not everyone is convinced. Writing in the New York Review of Books in 2005, the poet Charles Simic praised Autobiography of Red and The Beauty of the Husband highly, yet complained that she "labels almost everything she writes as poetry," that many of her typographic experiments "have been around at least since the days of Dada," and that Decreation has "too much poetics and too little poetry." Emily Wilson noted that Carson's Sappho hides what the Greek of Fragment 31 makes clear: speaker and beloved are both women.

In 2024 Carson disclosed in the LRB that she has Parkinson's disease, and that "a symptom particularly mortifying to me was that my handwriting disintegrated." Asked on a windy Icelandic roadside whether the Nobel would change her readership, she held on to her cap and said: "I'll get a sticker on the front of my books."

◎ What it means for the rest of us

What reading her can change

A way to handle loss. Nox never turns her brother into a complete story, because she couldn't; twenty-odd blank years stay blank. Instead of filling them with invention, she lays out the fragments, the mismatches and the things she could not find. For anyone who has sorted a parent's belongings or reread an unfinished message thread, that is a kind of permission: mourning doesn't have to be a smooth eulogy.

A sharper eye for translation. After a bracketed Sappho you start noticing where other translations have quietly "completed" the original for you, and which of their fluency the translator supplied.

Where to start

Autobiography of Red. In her prize-day call Carson said most people read it — "it seems accessible" — and Sem-Sandberg called it the gate for many readers over decades. Moderate difficulty: the story is clear and the lines are short and quick. If the scholarly introduction up front doesn't grab you, skip it for now. Or take her own advice: "Start in the middle."

From there, try Nox — in print if you can; it is an object, not just a text — and If Not, Winter. "The Glass Essay" is in Glass, Irony and God.

What it does not mean

Not "you need Greek to read her." The translations, notes and introductions are part of the work, not a toll gate. Not "making the classics relatable": she says outright that she doesn't care whether the ancients are useful to us; the arrow points the other way. And not "her translations are faithful": her Sappho hugs the Greek word by word, while Autobiography of Red rewrites Stesichoros wholesale. Same writer, two opposite stances.

🌀 Crossing over · interdisciplinary links

  • AI · Completion vs. masking: language models are trained partly by masking words and asking for the blank to be filled; a Hopfield network earns its keep by restoring a whole memory from a damaged pattern. Carson's brackets do the opposite: they keep the missing data as data, the way a NULL in a database is not a zero. Completion buys fluency; preserving the gap buys honesty — and the price of the former is exactly what we call hallucination.
  • Representation learning · The channel between languages: she locates the perfect language in "the little channel in between the two languages." Neural machine translation maps sentences from two languages into a shared intermediate space and decodes from there; that space belongs to neither language. The difference: the model compresses the channel into coordinates, while Carson stops inside it and looks at one word at a time.
  • Control theory · Desire runs on distance: the core of Eros the Bittersweet is that eros lives in the unbridgeable gap between lover and beloved; close the gap and the drive disappears. That is the shape of a feedback loop: what drives the actuator is the difference between setpoint and state; when the error goes to zero, the system goes still. Much of her work maps the shape of that error signal.
  • Buddhism · Non-self and "decreation": Simic's review quotes Simone Weil, "we participate in the creation of the world by decreating ourselves," the idea Carson borrows in Decreation to read Sappho alongside medieval mystics. Olsson says she has always dreamed of a work in which the writer's self disappears. This shares a structure with the Buddhist teaching of non-self: loosen the grip on the self and the object comes into view. Weil's frame is Christian, and her endpoint is union with God, not liberation.

// Think further

Translating a broken ancient poem: fill the gaps, or leave them open?
A filled-in version reads well but disguises the translator's guesses as the original; a bracketed one is honest but can lose the general reader, as some reviewers complained. Another way in: does the reader want "what Sappho wrote," or "how much we actually know about Sappho"? Those questions have very different answers.
"I'm interested in us being relevant to them." What does that mean?
The usual question asks what the classics can do for us, which makes the present the judge. Carson hands the judge's seat to poets dead two thousand years: do our breakups, families and mourning hold up next to Sappho and Catullus? It reads as humility, and also as a very high bar. In your own field, do you ever test work against "what would the people who came before make of this?"
Does Nox have to be a box?
Part of its meaning comes from its body: the sheet has to be unfolded fold by fold, and the two facing pages must be seen together. An ebook or web page could keep the facing pages but not the "tombstone you can touch." Which works can't be separated from their medium? And how do the ways you keep family memories — a phone camera roll, a cloud drive — shape how you remember the people in them?
